## **Abstract**

Globalization has flourished many sectors of the society, including higher education. This article researches the existing literature of globalization of education in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) with the establishment of international branch campuses (IBCs), particularly in Dubai. UAE has its laws and legislations in place for IBCs which must be followed by all. There are different accreditation boards in UAE that universities undergo based on their type and locality within the country. Due to UAE having a strong political, religious, and cultural context, tensions are faced by IBCs and local students in ensuring the implementation of curriculum borrowed policies. Despite the tensions, it is seen that UAE has a steady increase in the growth of IBCs. This article further researches the tensions and reasons of the success of IBCs in UAE's context.

## **Keywords**

education policy, higher education, middle eastern education, globalization of education, international branch campus, social justice, borrowed education policies

## **Introduction**

Education has become a target of globalization with its own policies, framework, and procedures affecting local education policies and practices. This has formed a superstructure above all local and national schools and universities (Spring, 2015). Globalization or internationalization of education has triggered an increase in universities offering transnational education. The main concept of transnational education is to cross national and regional borders to give access to students to a university from another country in their home countries (British Council, 2013). United Arab Emirates (UAE) is no exception to the concept of globalization of education withholding several international schools and universities from various parts of the world, often called as international branch campuses (IBCs). The Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research (MOHESR) regulates the higher education system in UAE. The laws regulating the implementation of overseas universities and their policies in UAE are managed by the Minister of State for Higher Education and Advanced Skills to ensure delivery of high-quality postsecondary education with respect to the labor market requirements (Ministry of Education [MOE], 2018). The question that arises is that how is UAE enduring a vast number of IBCs provided its strict cultural context?

Not much research has been done in the context of UAE with respect to higher education policy borrowing and its implementation. The aim of this research is to explore the existing procedures undertaken by the UAE governmental education body to regulate international policy borrowing concepts and their acceptance in UAE. With the growing education industry, organizations must bring innovative changes to stand out in the market and be able to attract consumers. These changes further create scope for new policy implementations based on the cultural, religious, and economic factors of the country. During this transition, organization leaders must have the capability of driving the changes and implementing new strategies effectively. This creates challenges for the leaders to effectively implement the policy change. Moreover, with the implementation of policies and strategies, organizations must undergo a rigid change management system to make internal amendments.

## **Research Methodology**

The primary methodology of this research is a secondary research study done through a systematic literature review. It is aided with personal work experience in UAE's tertiary education system. To conduct the secondary research, an intensive amount of literature is studied by reviewing peerreviewed journal articles, newspaper articles, government documents via their websites, official World Bank reports, and local historical data. ## **IBC**

The concept of IBCs is to provide international education to people in their home or resident countries without having the need for them to relocate to an international country (Wilkins, 2010). This can be done either by the institution itself or as a joint venture with the help of a partner as per the criteria made by the government or through strategic alliances (Altbach, 2010; Borgos, 2016; Lane, 2011). The

completely owned model seems to be the riskiest model of IBC (Borgos, 2016). The Middle Eastern countries, such as Qatar and UAE, seem to have strategic alliances as a common way of hosting IBCs, where the home campus manages the physical arrangements of the institution, the curriculum, and the degrees awarded (Lane, 2010). However, in countries like China, where the host country's policies state that a foreign institution must have a host entity, joint ventures are common (Kinser, 2010).

The two leading global authorities taking care of IBCs are C-BERT (Cross-Border Education Research Team) and OBHE (Observatory on Borderless Higher Education). According to C-BERT, IBC is an entity, operated by a foreign education provider, with its name and face-to-face teaching, offering a complete program that leads to the credentials of that provided by the foreign education body (C-BERT, 2015). These foreign branch campuses can help government achieve objectives related to increase of skill shortage within labor workforce, increase higher education quality, and bring knowledge-based economic sustainability among several other benefits (Fahmy, 2014; Lane, 2011; Naidoo, 2010; Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education [QAA], 2014; Verbik & Merkley, 2006; Welch, 2011). It is argued that IBCs are the upgraded concept of neocolonialism from which the providers take the advantage rather than the receivers (Wilkins, 2011). Also, most branches are not even IBCs because of their small size and relatively fewer courses being offered (Altbach, 2010). Henceforth, when evaluating countries to open IBCs, several factors should be taken into consideration, such as government legislations, cultural and social norms, level of bureaucracy, political and natural uncertainties, and financial risk factors (Wilkins, 2016). For instance, Swan (2013) reports that Michigan State University in Dubai had to close its undergraduate operation due to the home campus not being able to afford it.

According to C-BERT's trends and development report 2016, it is seen that UAE takes second place, after China, hosting 31 IBCs from around the world (OBHE & C-BERT, 2016). Considering just UAE and Qatar, together, from Middle East, a total of 22% of IBCs are accounted (Healey, 2015). Among the Arab countries, UAE comes second in quality of education, after Qatar according to the Global Competitiveness Report 2015–2016 (Ashour & Fatima, 2016). According to Idriss and Hanauer (2011), considering Middle East, 61% of the IBCs are in UAE, followed by 14% in Qatar and 7% in Egypt. It is also seen that the number of IBCs worldwide have increased to 249 which is 26% increase from that of 2010 (OBHE & C-BERT, 2016). This increase in IBCs shows a positive hope for transnational education and its growth. Contradicting to it, it is also seen that some IBCs fail and shut down in a short span of time due to various reasons. It is seen that approximately 10% failure has been recorded since mid-1990s (Lane & Kinser,

In 2009, a few universities in UAE faced downgrade in student enrollments since their start up in 2008, such as Rochester Institute of Technology, Michigan State University, and Murdoch University (Lewin, 2009). ## **The Need of IBCs in UAE**

UAE is well known for the number of expatriates residing in the country due to the significant growth of economic development that triggers the influx of workers from various parts of the world (Wilkins, 2013). An expatriate, also called expat, could be defined as someone residing in a foreign country for various purpose, such as work, and intends to return to his or her home country after a certain tenure (Zilber, 2009). Back in the days, expat children had the tendency to leave the country where parents resided because higher education was not offered to noncitizens or because the local institutions were categorized by expats as low quality (Wilkins, 2013). However, this has now changed as public universities have started to admit intake of expats and international students, which is now a source of competition for the IBCs (Wilkins, 2016). Nevertheless, with the amount of fees asked at such universities, the enrolments of expats are limited.

According to the World Bank, an estimate of 9.2 million people residing in UAE are accounted as of 2016 from which the local citizens are less than a million, therefore, contributing less than 20% of the total population only (The Official Portal of the UAE Government, 2018; UAE Interact, 2009; "UAE Population Statistics in 2018," 2018; Wilkins, 2010). The high percentage of expatriates trigger the need for international university campuses to open in UAE because of the cultural diversity and the economic drive. For instance, an American citizen is most likely to get their children educated from an American school/university, whereas someone from India would want their children to get educated from an Indian school/university because they want their heirs to get the same level and standard of education as what they got back in their country. Wilkins's (2013) research on the most important choice of expat student's selection for starting a university from UAE showed that students "demonstrated a sense of belonging to their passport country." This implies that they were more likely to return to their home countries to continue postsecondary education because that is the place they regarded as home, a

place to return to. Another main motive to leave UAE for further education was the quality of education. With the establishment of IBCs, these students are now able to pursue foreign education by staying in UAE with their families as they expect the same quality of education as that of the home campus and get a foreign-accredited degree without having to relocate and leave their friends and families. However, this implies that the students and parents expect the same quality and management of IBCs in UAE as that of the home campus, including the teaching faculty's qualification and experience, perhaps also their nationality.

Another major factor contributing to the need of international education is the fact that UAE does not permit expats to obtain its citizenship. This implies that expat residents living in UAE would eventually have to move back to their home countries after completing their work/business tenure. This results in people wanting their children being educated at an IBC, so that if required to leave the country half way, they could get the same education for their children back at home without having to worry about the education equivalency. In the past, this was majorly fulfilled by sending students abroad to international countries to get the best standard of education (Wagie, 2005). However, several issues such as financial management, place to stay, safety of the child in a foreign country, and so on, is faced by the parents of the students, and to overcome this limitation, transnational education found its ways in the Arab world, particularly UAE.

Majority of expats living in the UAE are on work or business visa which gets renewed after every 1, 2, or 3 years tenure. By having access to international universities in UAE, people get to benefit by educating their children in the same country and cutting down costs of additional traveling, accommodation, and food expenses in another country (Government of Dubai, 2017). Moreover, university fees in a foreign country are higher as compared with the fees of the same IBC established in UAE. According to the UAE legislation, from the age of 18, unmarried daughters can continue to stay under father's sponsorship but not the sons; they must either find a permanent job or be a full-time student at an institution (Bobker, 2014; Mehta, 2018). In such situations, it becomes almost mandatory for a student interested in further studies to enroll at a university in UAE or travel abroad for education. This fact also contributes to the rise of international education facilitators in UAE.

Westernization is also something looked upon in the Arab culture. With the fast-growing establishment of multinational companies (MNCs) in the MENA (Middle East and North Africa) region, a need for economic, technological, and knowledge-based development is required for the new workforce to thrive. There is a huge rush of foreign institutions ready to set up in UAE (Ashour & Fatima, 2016). A few reasons behind this increase of interest by the Western institutions in transnational education in the Arab

## **Policy Borrowing**

Education policies are being borrowed, copied, and implemented from the most successful education systems with the hope of having the same results and performance (Auld & Morris, 2014; Morris, 2012). Transnational policy borrowing is not about completely transferring actual education policy; it is rather about observing, analyzing, and interpreting the policies in a different context. From a neoliberal context, it is up to the state to regulate and monitor standard but also be open to provisions of education to private sector (Steiner-Khamsi, 2016). It is debatable if the quality of education in an IBC is maintained at the same level to that of the main campus, considering the policies borrowed from a different context. Altbach (2010) states that the breadth of education hardly comes close to that of the main campus in terms of the academic staff's skills and qualifications, availability of resources, physical environment, social facilities, and learning resources. Tan (2016) agrees by stating that the borrowed policies are being reformed based on the locality and could be very different from that of the original policy. Also, that the changes made to education policy transfers in a different context depend on what one observes or perceives and are interpreted through their prior beliefs, values, expectations, and so on. The trigger for policy borrowing came into existence due to leverage, legitimization, and recognition at a national and international level (Spreen, 2004). To ensure that the branch

campus is providing an equivalent education as the home campus, it is important for the student body to match in terms of quality and selectivity (Altbach, 2010).

There are times that not all policies that are borrowed from the home campus can be effective in the host campus due to cultural difference. Culture plays a vital role in acceptance of policies and it is necessary to respect and obey the laws and regulations in these circumstances (Morgan, 2011). Wilkins (2016) agrees that cultural differences between home and host country must be given importance. UAE is an Islamic country; therefore, there are few things unacceptable according to the Islamic culture. Issues concerning homosexuality, academic freedom, treatment of women are a concern in countries such as UAE, Singapore, and China (Wilkins, 2016). According to United Kingdom's Quality Assurance Agency (QAA, 2014), it is noted that UAE gives cultural importance to the higher education curriculum, the staffing of faculty and other members, and students (Wilkins, 2016). UAE's policies for Israel had led to University of Connecticut abandoning its plan to open IBC in Dubai (Wilkins, 2016). Similarly, New York University opened its campus in Abu Dhabi on a condition that they would welcome Israeli staff and students (Wilkins, 2016).

## **Higher Education Framework of UAE**

The higher education framework consists of three main categories of higher education institutions—the federallevel public system, the state-level semi-public/private system, and the free zone system (Austin et al., 2014; Government of Dubai, 2017; Wilkins, 2010). The federallevel system, also called as public education system (Wilkins, 2010), includes universities owned by the government and is primarily for Emirati (UAE citizen) students. The privileges provided to Emiratis attending such universities vary from free tuition to direct entry at governmental firms after successfully graduating (Ashour & Fatima, 2016; Wilkins, 2010). United Arab Emirates University, Zayed University, and Higher Colleges of Technology are the three main universities under this category (Bhayani, 2014; Wilkins, 2010). The state-level semi-public institutions are owned by private and state shareholders and open for the expat students; however, few Emiratis also attend these universities, based on their preferences. American University in Dubai (AUD), Abu Dhabi University, New York University, and Al Ghurair University are few examples of these institutions. The free zone university body involves ties up between the local Emirati partners and international higher education provider (HEP) from around the world. They are mainly IBCs run by a foreign home campus or funded by federal or individual Emirate governments (Austin et al., 2014). The first IBC known to be opened is University of Wollongong from Australia in 1993 (Wilkins, 2010), which is currently

## *Laws and Regulations for Opening IBC in Dubai*

Dubai has established free zones for HEPs and allows 100% foreign ownership (Ashour & Fatima, 2016; Government of Dubai, 2017; Wilkins, 2016). Before a borrowed policy is implemented at any IBC in Dubai, a thorough legislative and contextual evaluation process is undergone by the KHDA. For any HEP to be approved in Dubai, it must undergo UQAIB's approval to ensure that policies, practices, and resources are in place like the home HEP, so that sustainable learning environment and conditions are provided to students to match that of the home HEP (Government of Dubai, 2017; Quality Assurance Manual, 2016). This doesn't mean that all programs offered at the home campus must be offered at the branch campus; it could apply to any faculty or graduate school. According to the UAE resolution, Article 4, for institutions outside the

free zones, the specifications and standards of the building architecture must be compliant with that of the home campus (KHDA, 2014). In case, an HEP fail to meet the criteria evaluated by UQAIB, a fixed term of probation is given to the HEP, and after the term ends, a revaluation is done.

To be granted permission from KHDA to open an IBC, an academic authorization must be obtained from KHDA (Government of Dubai, 2017). This can only be obtained if the HEP is approved by UQAIB or if it already has CAA accreditation.

A few important things that HEP must be able to submit with evidence are as follows:

- HEP must be free of any criminal conviction;
- HEP must provide financial capabilities and stability;
- Must submit educational and economic feasibility study of the IBC, including details such as the proposed name of the institute; vision, mission, goals, and values; legal status of the institution; the home recognition of the institution; the number of academic programs to be introduced with their details; 5-year plan for student enrolments and financial budgeting; proposed quality and assessment standard; internal policies and regulations and their consistency with the home campus; plan for the proposed site and architecture of the institution building/space; the education materials and language curricula or any other supporting document requested by KHDA (KHDA, 2014, Article 2).

The entire process can be broken down into four phases as shown in Figure 1. During the first phase, an email with all the required information is submitted. If accepted, the HEP registers via the KHDA e-services portal, along with a nonrefundable processing fee (Government of Dubai, 2017). KHDA reverts in 30 days, and if approved, an initial approval is granted. For those wanting to establish outside free zones, a no objection certificate will be forwarded by KHDA to MOE and CAA who will take care of the case ahead and resume at Phase 4 of the KHDA requirements. The third phase undergoes a thorough quality check, followed by the last phase where academic authorization is issued that gives permit to HEP to establish and operate in Dubai free zone (Government of Dubai, 2017). The academic authorization is valid for a year to complete the procedures as per the academic authorization and obtain trade license (Quality Assurance Manual, 2016). If not completed in this time frame, the approval will be void. If rejected, the institution may apply again after a year's time frame by rectifying mistaken based on the rejection reason (KHDA, 2014, Article 3). The KHDA higher education department has the right to reject any application if it doesn't comply with the strategic needs and goals of Dubai as well as the demographic and economic trends in the Emirate (Quality Assurance Manual, 2016).

## *Laws and Regulations for Introduction of * *Program for IBC*

The academic authorization obtained by KHDA consists of list of programs intended to offer by the HEP. These programs must be registered before enrolments can happen (Government of Dubai, 2017). Each academic program must be registered through a separate application and must be registered annually by KHDA. The notification of any amendments to KHDA must be done and applied within 2 weeks of the information becoming known to the HEP branch (Government of Dubai, 2017).

According to Article 12 of the UAE constitution for approving higher education programs, the program content must not conflict with the Sharia law and the customs and traditions of the State (KHDA, 2014). Sharia also means path in Arabic language and guides a Muslim in all aspects of living life, including financial dealings, religious obligations, daily routines, marriage, inheritance, divorce, and custody (Johnson & Vriens, 2011). The program details must be submitted, including the mode of delivery, number of hours, the borrowed quality system as per the home campus, the graduation requirements, the name of academic certificate awarded during graduation, and the entity issuing the certificate. It is mandating to check the laws and ensure that the name or content is not contrary to public or government by any means; otherwise, it will be rejected by the government (KHDA, 2014).

## **Laws and Regulations for Control and ** **Assessment for IBC**

Article 19 of the UAE resolution states that it is important for KHDA to regulate education system as per their scope for the country. They may at any time inspect the course material, books, or any resources to verify its compliance with the resolution. A yearly report must be submitted by the academic institution on their academic, administrative, and financial position. Any violations can lead to KHDA taking an action on the academic institution (KHDA, 2014).

## *Laws and Regulations for Recruitment of Faculty for IBC*

Staffing of IBC can be done in numerous ways; lecturers can be bought in from the home campus with various tenure

agreements, such as full-time, part-time, or on a contract basis. Similarly, they can also be recruited locally within the host country (Swan, 2013; Wilkins, 2010). The UAE government has enforced a law focusing on Emiratization for the past few years, that is, a process involving the national citizens to increase their numbers in the public and private workforce. This has led to amending policies so that nationals are given higher precedence over non-nationals (Austin et al., 2014). However, due to not having sufficient Emiratis meeting the employment needs in the academic sector, it is not a preferred option of employment (Melly, 2010). As per the UQAIB's Quality Assurance Manual (2016), the teaching staff must have minimum qualification of a level higher than the one they are teaching. For example, a postgraduate lecturer must have a higher level qualification, that is, a PhD in a relevant principle, whereas an undergraduate lecturer must at least have a postgraduate degree obtained. The teaching practices must also be equivalent to that of the home HEP. Along with the responsibility of the HEP, it is also faculty's responsibility to ensure that students get the same standard and level of education as that of the home HEP. The course moderation is done by the home HEP to ensure the same. However, this is not the case with federal universities as they do not have to be moderated externally (Swan, 2013).

## **Tensions Faced by IBCs in UAE**

One of the main factors that cause tensions for IBCs in UAE context is the era of postcolonialism—the ideology of Western versus oriental or colonizer versus colonized (Diallo, 2014). There is no evidence that UAE was a victim of colonialism; hence, their educational experience differs from the other Asian or sub-Saharan African countries (Burden-Leahy, 2009). This lack of impact did not expose UAE to education back in time as compared with the other sub-Saharan countries. This triggered the early UAE government to be impressed with globalization of education from the West and accept the implementation of their education system without seeking and replacing it (Burden-Leahy, 2009). However, UAE is strongly known for its political, religious, and cultural beliefs. A violation made to any of these tangents could lead to an expat being deported from the country. The question that arises here is how are the IBCs adapting to the UAE context provided its strong norms and values?

## *Local Versus Federal Control*

Based on the legislations of UAE discussed above, it is seen that studying and graduating from a federal university will aid in getting employed anywhere in the UAE, including governmental jobs where pay scales are high and timings are minimal. However, the federal institutions only admit Emirati students. The other option for non-Emirati students is to get into an institution accredited by CAA. When it comes to international ranking of the universities, the private institutions, such as University of Birmingham, UK, which is ranked 116th in the World University Rankings (Search, 2018) and Curtin University, Australia, which is ranked between 301 and 350 (Search, 2018), are significant in UAE's education market being the top 2 rank holders. Students seeking well-renowned international education are most likely to go to such universities. These universities are accredited by KHDA, but not by CAA. This means the graduates can obtain employment anywhere in UAE except for the federal organizations. This academic federal control over education makes it difficult for those planning to stay in UAE in the long run when it comes to seeking employment in government sector. Emiratization at workplaces has also increased the chances of private institution graduates to be left unemployed as compared with the federal graduates.

## *Academic Freedom Versus UAE Political Control*

UAE's political system is a constitutional monarchy, where political parties are not permitted (UAE: Political Structure, 2017). Several analysts don't consider the Gulf states as liberal or democratic (Mazawi, 2005). Academic ties in the country can also be a motive to increase political ties between the home and the host country. Having an international brand labeling in the UAE of an education institute increases the welfare economy of the country and promotes the country in other parts of the world. Some Arab leaders view such educational reforms as a vital move in educating the public who can further contribute toward the economic and political reforms. However, there are no records of the political motivation for Western or American institutions to expand in the Middle East region (Idriss & Hanauer, 2011).

It is challenging to engage in political conversations with the students of UAE as some aspects of culture and gender can be sensitive despite having no religious implication to it

(Diallo, 2014). Topics related to terrorism or sex education or expressing political views that contradict to the country's political views are sensitive to the people and action may be taken against those delivering them (Diallo, 2014). One must be very careful in talking about the existing law or sociopolitical values in open. It is very common for someone speaking against the law to be deported from the country, if reported. This creates an academic barrier where political current affairs of the world cannot be talked about in classrooms openly for discussions. This implies that the borrowed content material of an IBC that includes current affairs that object the political mindset of local students could become controversial and cause tension in a classroom, affecting students as well as the teaching faculty.

## *Academic Freedom Versus UAE Religious * *Control*

UAE is an Islamic country and the education system in UAE is highly influenced by the Arab and Islamic values and epistemology (Diallo, 2014). It is against the legislative law to not abide by the Islamic principles and values, even when it comes to education system. On the contrary, the Western education framework is influenced by liberal and secular epistemologies (Diallo, 2014). It differs from the Islamic education system in terms of ideologies and attitude toward knowledge (Diallo, 2014). For an IBC to be based in UAE, there are tensions faced with respect to the religious differences of the countries. IBCs coming from the West have their education system based on scientific aspects than the religious beliefs. However, previously, the Western education system was also based on partial religious aspects of Jewism and Christianity (Diallo, 2014). Nevertheless, the current system has been carried forward and leveled more based on science, proofs, evidence, and facts. Topics such as evolution theory could cause tension between the students as it differs from what science has to say and what Islam teaches.

Various cultural and religious sensitive topics could create tensions in a classroom for students. These topics include homosexuality, polygamy, transgender, prostitution, evolution theories, drug and alcohol abuse, pork meat, Sharia law versus secular law (Diallo, 2014), and gambling and luck (Kadiwal & Rind, 2013). Such topics must be dealt with care as local (Emirati) or Muslim students might get offended by taking it as a contradiction to their religious/ cultural beliefs. The concept of international education is not just about moving education from one country to another, but it is about negotiating with the local culture and religious beliefs in the GCC context (Kadiwal & Rind, 2013). Considering IBCs whose course curriculum and material are borrowed from the home country, it becomes essential to verify the content and modify it before delivery of the lecture. Teaching something that cannot be related to

<!-- page 9 -->

the student's environment or practices is equivalent to not teaching at all (Kadiwal & Rind, 2013). However, IBCs have a legitimate amount of pressure in adapting the course content, assessments, and pedagogy (Willis, 2004) because in principle, the concept of IBC is to deliver the exact curriculum in terms of content and learning outcomes (Healey, 2015).

Due to the religious rules and beliefs of the country, the teaching faculty tends to have limited academic freedom (Ghabra & Arnold, 2007). Anything contradictory to the local beliefs cannot be voiced out; otherwise, chances of their dismissal increase. For example, the Danish cartoon issue in 2005 could not be discussed openly due to the limited academic freedom (Ghabra & Arnold, 2007). This limited academic freedom makes knowledge an early casualty.

## *Academic Freedom Versus UAE Cultural Control*

UAE is an Arab culture–oriented country and intends to preserve its culture even while integrating and encouraging other cultures from the world, including Westernization. Offshore academic institutions sit at the border of cultural diffusion and cultural borrowing (Idriss & Hanauer, 2011). Academic institutions need to cater culture-sensitive issues that could arise when borrowing policies from offshore. One of the main things that must be looked at is the academic textbook contents in schools and universities. The use of images or examples to explain various concepts must be screened for modesty and noncontroversial issues; otherwise, it could be of resistance to the Emiratis or Muslim students (Diallo, 2014).

Looking at the cultural norms, women voice and freedom had not been of any importance for a long era in the Middle Eastern society. Education was viewed by traditional culture-oriented parents as a mean for their daughters to stay out of the home (Burden-Leahy, 2009). The globalization of education through IBCs has bought in enormous change in the perception of education for the Arab population. In the recent years, a general interest of increasing education for women is seen in the Gulf region, including the UAE, leading to a demand of high-quality postsecondary education (Idriss & Hanauer, 2011). Higher education is perceived by women as a gender equalizer, empowering them to have a freedom of speech and challenge the established order (Findlow, 2013). This is a good opportunity of IBCs within the country because majority of the Emiratis are not comfortable in sending their daughter abroad for higher education and prefer having them educated within the country (Idriss & Hanauer, 2011). With the concept of Emiratization, several Emirati females are seen taking over top industry-specific jobs after getting educated at renowned universities, including few IBCs. Nowadays, the number of female students tends to overcome the male students at all

education levels, where they are highly encouraged by their parents (Burden-Leahy, 2009).

Another important factor brought in due to globalization of education is the use of English language. The world culture theorists consider that univerzalization is obtained by diffusing pedagogies of the education system toward a uniform model (Idriss & Hanauer, 2011). With UAE being a multicultural and multinational country, there is a huge population of expatriates as seen earlier. This has made Emiratis to adapt to bilingual learning skills in Arabic and English, from a very young primary school age. It is also seen that for multiple Emirati generations learning and studying in English as a primary language at universities, the conversations at home also tend to be led by English (Burden-Leahy, 2009). However, some parents and schools resist to the impact of offshore education as they see it as a threat to their religious and cultural identity (Kadiwal & Rind, 2013). The local citizens resist to the concept of homogenization and Westernization by the replacement of Arabic to English language instructions as it may threaten their local traditional beliefs (Idriss & Hanauer, 2011). According to the Islamic governance, the Arabic language symbolizes modernity and secularism, and with respect to the neocolonialism, it reflects religiosity and tradition (Findlow, 2008). It also gets difficult for IBC managers in enforcing the home campus quality regime in the host country by adapting to the language of delivery and instruction to local needs and limitations (Waterval et al., 2015).

When it comes to the teaching staff, enforcing pedagogies from IBCs home campus in the UAE's cultural context could also be challenging. Local staff could be uncomfortable in using Western case study approach that overshadows the local cultural beliefs (Healey, 2015). According to Tikly (2004), the idea of Westernizing education in the developing countries that enforce them to think in the Western way is nothing but colonization of the mind. On the contrary, several IBCs intend to bring in faculty from overseas so that the teaching pedagogies and methodologies are leveled at both home and the host country. This could be problematic as they have less experience within the Arab society and may not have any prior cultural-sensitive training about the local region, its cultural values, and norms (Idriss & Hanauer, 2011).

## **Conclusion, Limitation, and Future ** **Work**

With the increase in transnational education worldwide, UAE is one of the major host countries for IBCs to explore. The current state of education system and legislative policies shows a clear indication of the need of IBCs in UAE. These IBCs tend to bring in the home country's education policies and pedagogies. Not all pedagogies and policies can be implemented in the UAE's context due to not having

the complete academic freedom for an IBC. Despite the tensions faced by IBCs with respect to the academic freedom they have in the country with respect to UAE's political, religious, and cultural context, a steady rise in IBCs is seen in the UAE. The primary reason for it being globalization that brings in financial economy boost in the country. This points that globalization of education can work out in other parts of the world too if it can in the strict environment of UAE. However, the local community fears the emergence of cultural colonization in the long run because of the Westernized education system that the country is walking toward.

The study could be explored further with the aid of a primary research that will enlighten the challenges and tensions of the existing IBCs in UAE by the viewpoints of current stakeholders who set internal policies and controls the management and amendments of policies when it comes to implementing borrowed policies for IBCs, keeping the local context in mind. Also, those who are experience the Western versus Oriental education war. Therefore, for future work, I'd like to take this research ahead to conduct a primary research with few of the existing IBCs in Dubai to first and find the challenges and limitations faced by the IBCs in setting up the campus in UAE, introducing new programs, program evaluation and assessment, and faculty recruitment. Second, what also needs to be looked at is if any of the current legislative education policies should be proposed with amendments to overcome the challenges and limitations faced by the IBCs, keeping the current framework in mind. Third, when recruiting teaching faculty from other parts of the world or hiring a Western faculty, what impact does the faculty have considering the strong religious and cultural norms of the country? More research on sharing people's voice to demonstrate the impact of IBCs on local cultural, political, and religious identities and views need to be carried out.
