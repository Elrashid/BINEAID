## **Abstract **

This case study describes a research project that used a secondary data set entitled "The National Survey of Postsecondary Faculty, 2004" (NSOPF:04). These data allowed us to study how the family lives of higher education faculty in the United States influence their classroom teaching practices. More importantly for students, we use this data set and our research to provide an overview of the characteristics of secondary data and how these characteristics systematically and structurally influence important research decisions. We also discuss the different research approaches associated with secondary data analysis. This case study traces our research project from the very beginning conceptualization until eventual publication.

## **Context of the Project and Researchers **

We are both full-time faculty at a public 4-year university where our main roles and responsibilities are the traditional triumvirate of research, teaching, and service, and where we include undergraduate and graduate students in our Sociologies of Education and the Family research projects. We have over 30 years of combined experience in these roles, which means that we have not only experienced the expectations of being productive researchers and teachers but have also seen countless colleagues—especially younger faculty—both succeed and not succeed in these capacities. At these public U.S. universities, faculty are generally and increasingly rewarded more for their research outputs than for their teaching efforts [(Green, 2008](#page-15-0);[ Jaque-](#page-15-1)

<!-- page 3 -->

[tte, 2013)](#page-15-1). Given this reward structure, faculty must decide how to divide their finite time between work efforts in research versus those in teaching. Often, life circumstances shape the amount of this finite time available to faculty, which may further influence how they distribute their efforts across their roles and responsibilities. In our combined experiences, we have noticed that family-related life circumstances are some of the most impactful on a faculty's available time, especially that of parenthood but also of marital status. Indeed, [Coser ](#page-15-2) [(1974)](#page-15-2) argues that work and family institutions are greedy because both want a worker's investments in time and energy.

Prior research has focused on how family life affects research productivity (e.g., [Mason, Wolfinger, & ](#page-15-3) [Goulden, 2013](#page-15-3)), and for good reason given the above-mentioned emphasis on research. However, we believe strongly that teaching productivity and effectiveness are equally important because of how these types of teaching practices help students develop academic, thinking, personal, cultural, and social skills as well as boost their retention and graduation rates ([Kuh, 2008](#page-15-4)). Thus, we embarked on a research journey to understand how family life influences effective teaching efforts and practices of faculty at four-year institutions. This was a topic that had yet to be empirically and statistically studied with a national and representative sample. In this case study, we will walk the readers through the typical steps, decisions, and tips that are often required of research with secondary data from conceptualization to eventual publication. Our case study will focus on our quantitative statistical approach that used secondary data from the 2004 wave of the National Survey of Postsecondary Faculty (NSOPF:04).

Oh, we forgot to mention—not only are we research collaborators, but we are also life collaborators given that we are a married, academic couple. Therefore, the research project of focus in this case study was quite personal and meaningful because of our own experiences and those of our many colleagues. This is not uncommon in the social sciences as research topics may be derived from theoretical, practical, and empirical sources but also from personal experiences. Our intimacy with the research topic did necessitate that our decisions be sound, aware of bias and conflicts, and based on data best suited to the research questions.

## **Research Practicalities With Secondary Data **

## **Beginning the Process **

We are mostly quantitative researchers, which means that we typically use statistical procedures with quantitative data (i.e., variables that are represented by or transformed into numbered codes) that test a series of research hypotheses. Given this, we knew that we wanted to use quantitative data for our research. This left us with two important initial decisions if we wanted to adequately address our main research question of *How * *does the family life of faculty influence their effective teaching practices? *

- 1. How do we generate these data?
- 2. How do we operationalize (i.e., conceptualize and measure) the concepts of family life and effective teaching?

These questions are not mutually exclusive. Indeed, the way in which we could operationalize our main research variables depended on the data decisions that we made. Generally, quantitative research in the social sciences has two data source options: primary or secondary data. Primary data are generally defined as those that are collected directly by a research team (or an individual researcher) for the specific research or analysis that is designed by this team. The team is typically involved in the entire research project—from conception to publication—and significantly influence what methodology is used, which topics are measured, what type of data/variables are collected, and who are studied and for how long. These primary data become secondary data when used by researchers not involved in the primary study and data collection. This means that researchers who use secondary data often use data that may have been intended for a different research purpose and goal. Given the characteristics of secondary data—which are detailed later in this case study—we made our first decision in this research: to use secondary, pre-existing data. This decision saved us time, effort, money, and personnel but the use of such data restricted us to whatever information was initially collected.

Our decision to use secondary data demanded another decision about what approach to adopt: a hypothesisdriven approach or a data/variable-driven approach? In a hypothesis-driven approach, researchers use prior research and theory to generate or deduce hypotheses and then look for a suitable data set that have the requisite information and variables to test these hypotheses. In a data-driven approach, researchers look

<!-- page 5 -->

through one or many data sets and determine what kind of research questions and hypotheses can be addressed with the available information and variables contained in the data set. This approach *does not* mean that a researcher first runs statistical analyses, and then proposes hypotheses based on the findings. Typically, the pure scientific method endorses the hypothesis-driven approach. More recently, it is argued that, in practice, these two methods are used jointly and iteratively and that the data-driven approach is increasingly used and accepted across a wide range of scientific fields especially in the era of increasingly large data sets and supercomputing (Cheng & Phillips, 2014; [Mazzocchi, 2015;](#page-15-5) [van Helden, 2013)](#page-16-0). Our research was a hybrid approach because as we began our literature search and review it became apparent very quickly which pre-existing data set was the most appropriate for our research question. Luckily for us, this data set—the NSOPF:04—was available publicly and through a restricted-use license and one of us (Carrie) had prior experience working with this data set.

There is another practical reason that supports the use of a hybrid approach—the complexity of social life and behaviors. The structure of and information collected by your chosen secondary data set will constrain how you think about, conceptualize, and empirically and statistically address your research question. Because of our training, personal experiences, and prior research, we tend to think about social behaviors as being influenced by two general forces—proximal and distal factors ([Bronfenbrenner, 2005](#page-15-6)). Proximal factors are those that are situated very close to the individual and may directly influence their behaviors. Distal factors reside farther away from the individual and may indirectly influence their behaviors. For example, in this current case study, we know from personal experiences and prior research that faculty teaching practices are influenced by a set of proximal factors such as class size and level of the course (e.g., Introductory) as well as distal factors such as the available resources of the institution.

But we were interested in another set of proximal factors—the family life circumstances of the faculty member that may directly influence how they go about organizing their teaching practices. We also wanted to make sure that our chosen data set had a rich set of other proximal factors as well as distal factors known to influence teaching practices. As we looked through individual and institutional variables collected in the NSOPF:04, we felt confident that our preferred approach to thinking about social life could be mostly satisfied with these data. If the NSOPF:04 did not meet these expectations, we would have (a) looked for other secondary data; or (b) changed the way that we approached and conceptualized our research question.

## **Methods in Action **

## **Finding Secondary Data and Developing Measurable Concepts **

Once we determined to use secondary data, our next task was to immerse ourselves in the prior studies that best overlapped with our own research topic. As indicated above, we first approached our research from a hypothesis-driven angle, so our next tasks were, in this order: (a) identify major gaps in the existing knowledge, (b) develop theoretically and empirically informed measures of family life and effective teaching practices, (c) find these measures in an existing data set, and then (d) elaborate theoretically and empirically informed research hypotheses. We felt that this approach was necessary to limit the influence that our personal experiences may have had on our study. That is, we wanted to eliminate our first-hand biases so we could more fully strive toward the goal of science as "the search for truth about reality." To achieve this, we needed to move from the subjective to the objective and to embrace the notion that

our individual minds are not altogether reliable. "More fully" simply put, the subjective realm refers to the possibility that inaccurate perceptions and thought we have through the medium of our minds, yours and mine, whereas the objective realm refers to that which lies outside and is independent of our minds. [(Babbie, 2011,](#page-14-0) pp. 36-37)

However, something happened when we began reviewing the prior studies for our research gap—we identified our secondary data set. Very early in our literature review, it became clear that most studies on effective teaching practices relied on two data sets: the NSOPF (e.g., [Webber, 2012](#page-16-1)) and the Faculty Survey of Student Engagement (FSSE) (e.g.,[ Nelson Laird, Garver, & Niskode-Dossett, 2011](#page-15-7)). We opted to use the NSOPF data set for several reasons, but also because the FSSE data are proprietary and not publicly available as a secondary data set. Saying this, we believed that the NSOPF data were better suited to our approach to our research topic and were more nationally representative of higher education faculty in the United States compared to the FSSE. At this point, our hypothesis-driven approach morphed into a hybrid approach as we toggled our efforts between the existing research and the NSOPF:04 data, codebooks, and manuals available online.

We went back to reviewing prior studies and found that previous research on U.S. faculty focused on how marriage and parenthood statuses influenced merit, promotions, and research outcomes. Research was lack-

<!-- page 7 -->

ing on whether family life is associated with teaching practices. Thus, we empirically identified and justified a research gap. The next step was to define the concept of "effective teaching practices." Again guided by our hybrid approach to secondary data, we zeroed in on practices called learner-centered assessment (LCA) for three reasons: (a) prior research used similar or identical teaching practices as a measure of effectiveness, (b) LCA practices were measured in the NSOPF:04, and (c) research showed that the use of LCA practices are time and effort intensive for faculty (Skelton, 2007), which must be taken into account when they make decisions about dividing their time between research and teaching. LCA activities are viewed as effective pedagogy that enhances student learning, and nearly all research finds positive outcomes among undergraduates who are exposed to LCA practices, including greater levels of engagement, learning, and persistence in the course and college ([Armbruster, Patel, Johnson, & Weiss, 2009](#page-14-1);[ Braxton, Jones, Hirschy, & Hartley, 2008](#page-14-2); Gasiewski et al., 2012). Examples of our six NSOPF:04 measures of LCA were oral presentations, multiple drafts of written work, and service learning.

However, the way in which these six LCA practices were measured in the NSOPF:04 was quite limited. In the original survey, faculty members were asked whether each LCA practice was "Not used," "Used in some classes," or "Used in all classes." This is often a main weakness and limitation of secondary data as the researcher is bound to use the data and variables collected by the original research team. However, the data set did contain six different measures of LCA and a large sample—both characteristics that are typical of secondary data sets and which allowed us to create a more dynamic measure with a multi-item summed index of LCA practices that ranged from 0 (all six not used in any classes) to 12 (all six used in every class).

Prior research suggested that we focus on the family life characteristics of marital and parenthood status, but once again the variables in the NSOPF:04 were limited in scope and range. Indeed, marital and parenthood status were the only family life variables collected in the NSOPF:04. We constructed sound measures of marital status by creating a set of dummy-coded dichotomous variables (0 = no; 1 = yes) indicating whether the faculty member was single and never married, married, living with a partner, or separated/divorced/widowed. For parenthood, our options were limited to the original surveys. Thus, we only measured how many dependent children under age 24 the faculty member supported—0, 1, 2, or 3 or more.

## **Developing Testable Research Hypotheses **

At this stage in the research, we knew what the NSOPF:04 data offered in the way of measuring our main study variables of family life and effective teaching. Still, we wanted our research hypotheses to be grounded theoretically and not influenced by our own experiences or by the way we measured our study variables. To inform our study, we adopted the prevailing family-relatedness-of-work theoretical approach in the social scientific study of work–family dynamics that is a combination of role conflict, role allocation, and gender role norms ([Greenhaus & Powell, 2012](#page-15-8)). We then embedded this theory into the realities of the roles, responsibilities, and reward structures for a full-time faculty member. Overall, we argued that individuals face role demands and responsibilities at both work and at home. As these demands intensify and conflict with each other due to the acquisition of new roles, individuals find themselves having to make time and effort allocations and compromises. These decisions are influenced by a complex set of objective and subjective factors including the real and perceived availability of resources, benefits and costs of actions taken and not taken, role expectations, and the normative gendered life courses and scripts differentially experienced by men and women.

Based on our theoretical approach, prior research, and the NSOPF:04 data, we determined that it appears that faculty who are married and are parents may have less time to dedicate to their academic work roles, spend more time in family roles, and work in an occupation that overwhelmingly rewards research productivity. Thus, *Hypothesis 1*: Faculty who are married will be less likely to use time-intensive LCA practices in their undergraduate courses compared to their unmarried counterparts. *Hypothesis 2*: Faculty with dependent children will be less likely to use time-intensive LCA practices in their undergraduate courses compared to their childfree counterparts. Gender occupied a prominent conceptual position in our theoretical approach where decisions about work and family efforts are structured along gender lines. We proposed *Hypothesis 3*: The negative associations between marriage and LCA and parenthood and LCA will be significantly smaller for female faculty.

## **Statistical Modeling and Hypotheses Testing **

One benefit of using secondary data in general and the NSOPF:04 in particular is that the structure of the data often permit the use of empirically informed and sophisticated quantitative statistical analyses. In our case,

<!-- page 9 -->

the NSOPF:04 collected proximal and distal data on faculty and the institutions where they worked, which allowed us to test our hypotheses with hierarchical linear models (HLM). It is beyond this case study to provide a detailed introduction to HLM, so we provide a reference below that may be of interest to the readers. In brief, the NSOPF:04 is a nested data set where multiple faculty from the same institution are represented throughout the data set. What this means is that we had to account for this nested structure because data for faculty at the same institution may be correlated as these faculty all share the same institutional characteristics (e.g., distal factors). And perhaps, it is these characteristics that influence LCA practices and not our measures of family life. Indeed, [Umbach (2007)](#page-15-9) found that faculty teaching practices were influenced by institutional culture as well as individual characteristics. By using NSOPF:04, our statistical models explicitly captured these empirical forces influencing teaching. The other benefit of using secondary data in general and the NSOPF:04 in particular is that these data sets typically collect a wide range of variables and characteristics, which permit the researcher to include a large number of empirically informed covariates into their statistical analyses. Indeed, besides measuring gender, family life, and LCA we were also able to include 14 relevant individual/proximal and 6 relevant institutional/distal characteristics that we had found in prior research to influence teaching practices.

## **What Did We Find? **

Our findings returned mixed support for our research hypotheses. The results for male faculty modestly supported our expected patterns where those who were married and had one dependent child used LCA practices far less than their unmarried colleagues and those with no children. Even so, the results for parenthood did not return consistent patterns as faculty fathers with two or more dependent children used LCA practices equal to those of male faculty with no children. Our significant findings for female faculty were in direct opposition to our expectations. Female faculty with dependent children used LCA practices far more than their female colleagues without dependent children, and these differences increased with each additional child. However, marital status was inconsequential for females in their use of LCA practices.

For our general approach, we did find support for including individual/proximal and institutional/distal predictors of teaching practices in higher education. A large number of both types of forces were significantly related to the use of LCA practices, and the fitted HLM models were much better when both individual and institutional variables were included. Our choice to include both sets of variables led to an interesting finding—there

<!-- page 10 -->

was more variety in the use of LCA practices among faculty within in the same institution than among faculty at different institutions.

## **Strengths and Weaknesses of the NSOPF:04 and Sec****ondary Data **

The NSOPF:04 survey was sponsored by the U.S. Department of Education's National Center for Education Statistics (NCES). This nationally representative study collected data on the characteristics, workload, and career paths of full- and part-time postsecondary faculty and instructional staff at public and private not-forprofit 2- and 4-year institutions in the United States. Conducted previously in 1988, 1993, and 1999, we used the 2004 data as it represented the most recent and last wave of the NSOPF survey project. There are no plans to continue the NSOPF into future waves. The NSOPF:04 contained a Faculty survey that was a sample of 35,000 faculty and instructional staff across 1,080 public and private not-for-profit degree granting postsecondary institutions in the 50 states and the District of Columbia of the United States. Completed interviews were obtained from 26,100 faculty for a weighted response rate of 76%. There was also an institutional survey that included 1,080 eligible institutions, of which completed interviews were obtained from 920 colleges and universities for a weighted response rate of 86%.

For our research, the NSOPF:04 was not intended specifically to understand how family life influences higher education teaching practices, but it did contain enough information and variables to directly examine this research question. Furthermore, the NSOPF:04—and secondary data in general—often have these characteristics that may strengthen your research ([Boslaugh, 2007;](#page-14-3)[ NCES, 2006)](#page-15-10):

- *Economy*: The use of NSOPF:04 saved us time, money, effort, and personnel as it was commissioned and paid for by the NCES. And, amazingly, it was free for us to use.
- *Research Preference*: We typically conduct quantitative analyses that test formal hypotheses using pre-existing data. We are less interested in writing grants to fund the collection of data and then being managers and supervisors of a research team to oversee the collection, processing, and entry of these data.
- *Breadth of Data*: Because the NCES financed and oversaw the NSOPF: 04 project, the data set has a vast array of topics and variables. For example, the Faculty survey included over 180 questions covering eight broad sections ranging from employment and responsibilities to opinions about work-

<!-- page 11 -->

ing conditions.

- *Breadth of Sample*: As mentioned above, the NSOPF:04 contained interviews with over 26,000 faculty from over 900 higher education institutions that nationally represented the 50 states and DC. This breadth would have been unattainable if we attempted to collect our own data.
- *Technicality and Expertise:* The NSOPF:04 was administered, collected, managed, and organized by a vast number of scientists, researchers, experts, and statisticians as well as the foremost social science/education data collection agency in the United States.
- *Documentation:* Lastly, because of the involvement of the above-mentioned personnel, the NSOPF:04 included vast documentation about research design, sampling, weighting, and the computation of standard errors that assisted us in our own statistical analyses.

Yet, we were exceedingly aware that the NSOPF:04 contained limitations that would restrict the scope and contributions of our research. Our main study variables were LCA, marital status, parenthood status, and gender. While we had good measures of gender (i.e., female and male) and marital status, the measures of LCA practices were not ideal. These response categories were quite restrictive and only tapped the surface of how faculty organize, execute, and distribute their classroom practices. We were also frustrated with the measured dynamics of parenthood. Ideally, we would have liked to know such things as the age, gender, and residential status of the child, all of which could influence family-work decisions and roles. Another major limitation of the NSOPF:04 is that the data were nearly a decade old when we began the research. This means that our findings would pertain most directly to higher education faculty and institutions in 2004, and less to these entities in 2013 when we started our research. These examples represent the typical weaknesses and trade-offs of using secondary data as we were not involved in the design and purpose of the NSOPF:04 and alternative and more recent data were not available to us.

## **Intersubjectivity: The Next Step for Secondary Data Analy****sis **

We do not want to mislead the reader into thinking that using secondary data is a research short cut. The decision to collect primary or use secondary data is based on topic, methodological preferences, and data availability. We prefer to use secondary data because of the six characteristics we listed above. Still, our research journey up to this point took about 12 months (certainly not "a short cut"), and we still had more questions:

<!-- page 12 -->

how do we know if our research product was any good and whether we limited personal biases? To this end, we prepared and submitted it to a peer-reviewed journal. "Peer-review" is the process whereby independent scientists are chosen by the journal's editor and editorial board to read submitted manuscripts and evaluate whether they are worthy of publication. If a positive consensus is reached, then the manuscript will be accepted for publication as the research was agreed upon to be of high enough quality and contributions—thus achieving intersubjectivity. As argued by [Babbie (2011),](#page-14-0) scientists are aware of the power of subjective biases and are explicitly committed to avoiding them in research. Social scientists need to be especially committed given that their subject matter is much more personal than is the subject matter of the natural sciences. So, peer review provided a mechanism where our research and decisions were scrutinized by our peers and colleagues as a sort of quality control check.

If the reader would go to our article based on the research detailed in this case study, you can see the Article History reads as "Received 15 October 2014, Received in revised form 10 March 2015, Accepted 10 March 2015." Two things should stand out—the review process took another 12 months and the initial review of our manuscript prompted a decision by the editor labeled "Revise and resubmit." What this means is that the independent peer reviewers liked our research, but also had enough concerns and identified flaws that needed fixing before it was deemed worthy of publication. Once we attended to their concerns, we were invited to resubmit the research back to the journal for another round of peer reviews. Ultimately, our twice-revised manuscript was positively evaluated by the peer reviewers and editor and accepted for publication on March 10, 2015. Our *two-year* research journey resulted in an intersubjective agreement among us and other scientists that our final research product represented a valuable contribution to the field of sociology and education.

Some of the readers of this case study will not be submitting their research papers to peer-reviewed journals. But, we still highly suggest that you do have several individuals read your paper, perhaps your instructor, committee members, and fellow students and researchers with more advanced training. Research finds that these non-journal peer reviewers provide valuable insights, feedback, and suggestions that tend to improve one's research and work toward achieving intersubjectivity [(Li & Gao, 2016](#page-15-11)).

## **Practical Lessons Learned: From Beginning to End of the ** **Research Process **

- Be comfortable developing research topics from personal experiences as well as theoretical, practical, and empirical concerns.
- Begin with a solid secondary data research strategy, but also be willing and flexible to take advantage of unforeseen events and discoveries. Gaining familiarity with your data set will help you think more substantively about your research topic and the theoretical and empirical selection of variables within this data set.
- Use the existing research to help identify appropriate secondary data sets. Rely on digital resources associated with a secondary data set to help guide and fine-tune your research.
- The use of secondary data may result in less-than-ideal measurements of behaviors and attitudes. Use the existing research to help aid in and support the operationalization of concepts measured as variables in the secondary data set. Also, there may be opportunities in the data set to combine multiple survey items to create a more multi-dimensional measure.
- Even though you have identified your secondary data and operationalized your variables, it is still possible to return to a hypothesis-driven approach to your research questions. For precision, the language used in your hypotheses will often be determined by the variables available in your secondary data set.
- Look for secondary data that allows for the most empirically informed, flexible, and sophisticated statistical approaches. Things to consider are sample size, generalizability, substantive range of variables, and the properties of these variables or of your recoded or transformed variables.
- All secondary data contain strengths and weaknesses. Researchers need to work to leverage the strengths and to minimize the limitations.
- Peer review is an important stage in the research journey that produces positive outcomes for the final product and possibly advancing scientific understanding and knowledge through intersubjective agreement.

## **Conclusion **

In this quantitative Sociology of Education case study, we walked the reader through issues surrounding sec-

<!-- page 14 -->

ondary data and how these issues influenced our research approaches, decisions, and learnings throughout the entire research process, which were highlighted in "Practical Lessons." We tried to strike a balance in our presentation—providing wide-angle and general treatments and examples as well as discussing more upclose and intimate details of the decisions we made in our research. While no two secondary data sets are identical, we hope that this balanced approach will help student researchers find, organize, and analyze their own secondary data.

We also believe that quality and successful quantitative research goes beyond data and statistical analyses. Over the years, when we include students in our research or teach a research methods course we spend a lot of time with them on how to "think" about social science research so that it reflects the way the researcher views social life while considering data availability and structures. In this case study, we offered several ways in which to combine theory and data so that one's research may best approximate the forces driving social behaviors. In doing so, we stressed that there will always be strengths and limitations of secondary data that must be dealt with as well as accepted.

Lastly, we introduced the concept of "intersubjectivity" so students can see that it is important for research to be read and judged by others. While often a humbling experience, we are strong in our belief that scientific knowledge and understanding is best accomplished when scientists come to an agreement on the value and worth of research. Indeed, the feedback and suggestions that we received from our peer reviewers undoubtedly improved our research presented in this case study. We sought intersubjectivity through a formal peerreviewed process, but this task can also be accomplished in less formal contexts.
