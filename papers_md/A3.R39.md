## **ABSTRACT**

This study aims to understand the dynamics and impacts of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction in higher education in the UAE. Employing a quantitative questionnaire for students and qualitative questionnaire for staff, the research was conducted across selected higher educational institutions situated in DIAC. Quantitative data underwent statistical analysis, while qualitative data was analysed using thematic analysis. The results derived from the quantitative data underscore a robust acknowledgement of the significance of internationalisation among higher education students in the UAE. However, they also reveal noteworthy gaps in awareness of this phenomenon. The results from the qualitative data shed light on potential and practical avenues to enhance the impacts of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction within higher education in the UAE. The study contends that internationalisation has yielded positive effects on curriculum and instruction in higher education in the UAE. These effects could be further optimised through pertinent strategies.

## **Introduction**

Over the past three decades, the landscape of Higher Education (HE) in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) has undergone significant transformation, a change primarily shaped by the forces of internationalisation, as highlighted in the 2017 report from the Quality Assurance Agency (QAA [2017)](#page-14-0). This transformative journey encompasses a wide array of elements, spanning ideas, personnel, programmes, branch campuses, collaboration, and networking. The interplay of these internationalisation dynamics presents both opportunities and challenges, necessitating careful navigation by the UAE. This paper aims to delineate the trajectory of internationalisation in higher education, focusing on its impact on curriculum and instruction, and to analyse its diverse effects on the UAE's higher education sector. At the core of this inquiry is the assertion that for internationalisation to exert a significant impact on higher education, encompassing international staff, students, programmes, policies, and practices, a crucial component is the internationalisation of curriculum and instruction. The investigation specifically focuses on comprehending the dynamics and effects of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction within a group of higher educational institutions (HEIs) in the UAE, unveiling the complex ways in which these institutions are moulded by these processes.

To provide a contextual backdrop, it is relevant to offer a brief overview of the higher education sector in the UAE. Despite being a nation of only 52 years, the UAE has swiftly developed a robust higher education sector, significantly influenced by international experiences and perspectives

<!-- page 3 -->

(David et al. [2017](#page-13-0)). According to the Commission for Academic Accreditation (CAA [2022)](#page-13-1), there are a total of 67 accredited HEIs, with 50% receiving government funding, 42% operating as private institutions, and the remaining 8% functioning as branch campuses of overseas HEIs. Together, these institutions offer a total of 1175 accredited higher education programmes. While the number of branch campuses is relatively low, the pervasive impact of internationalisation dynamics is evident in the curriculum and instructional practices across the majority of HEIs. According to the most recent statistics, the UAE currently hosts 133,841 students enrolled in licenced higher educational institutions, with 85% pursuing undergraduate programmes. Notably, 65% of the student body consists of Emirati nationals, while 35% represents the international community. The gender distribution among students leans towards female enrolment, constituting 63% of the total. The academic faculty in the UAE comprises 8,861 members, with a significant majority (91.5%) hailing from more than 130 countries and being non-Emirati. Among the total faculty, 38% are female (3385 individuals). The ensuing literature review elaborates on the ways in which internationalisation have made a lasting impact on higher education in the UAE.

## **Literature review**

Navigating the intricacies of conceptualising the dynamics and impacts of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction within higher education poses a significant challenge. The internationalisation of higher education unfolds as a complex phenomenon where diverse policies and programmes evolve bilaterally, regionally, and globally (Verger and Hermo [2010)](#page-14-1). Drawing on Jane Knight's [(2003)](#page-13-2) comprehensive definition, Luxon and Peelo ([2009](#page-14-2)) characterise the internationalisation of higher education as a process that integrates international, intercultural, or global dimensions into the purpose, functions, or delivery of postsecondary education, operating at national, sectoral, and institutional levels. De Wit and Hunter [(2015,](#page-13-3) 41) suggest that universities have historically displayed international dimensions, whether in the concept of universal knowledge and associated research or in the movement of students and scholars, a phenomenon that inevitably influences curriculum and instruction in higher education. To unravel the complexities of how internationalisation influences curriculum and instruction, the literature review delves into various scholarly works. This exploration culminated in the creation of a framework that outlines the dynamics and impacts within higher education, as detailed in [Figure 1](#page-3-0) below.

The provided framework illustrates the intricate interplay of dynamics and impacts associated with the internationalisation of curriculum and instruction in higher education. The study intended to explore both dynamics and impacts of internationalisation of higher education as the dynamics highlight the forces and processes that stimulates internationalisation while the impacts indicate the effects of internationalisation on higher education. The phenomenon of internationalisation is propelled by the rise of hyper-modernity, with advancements in information and communications technology serving as a pivotal force in eroding physical boundaries. In its initial stages, internationalisation has primarily been championed by institutions in countries with high GDPs, driven by market logic and neo-liberal economic principles that emphasise competition and profit. In many of the modern nations, direct state support for higher education has dwindled, compelling institutions to seek opportunities in overseas markets to ensure their continued existence. The process of internationalisation varies; for some, it occurs gradually and unconsciously, akin to osmosis (De Vita [2007)](#page-13-4). In contrast, others, such as Prasanthi and Bhaskara Rao [(2019](#page-14-3)), contend that internationalisation is strategically driven, propelled by specific motives and incentives. Knight ([2004](#page-13-5)) contributes to this discourse by positing four complementary dimensions of internationalisation: activity, competency, ethos, and process. Together, these dimensions offer a comprehensive perspective on the multifaceted nature of internationalisation within the realm of higher education. Scholars argue on both opportunities and challenges of internationalisation of higher education. Tight ([2022)](#page-14-4) argues that the contemporary trends on internationalisation of higher education benefited beyond the west, elite institutions and students. While, R'boul [(2022](#page-14-5)) argues that

To comprehend the intricacies of the dynamics and impacts of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction in higher education, a contextual understanding is essential. While the international influence on higher education has historical roots (De Wit and Merkx 2012), recent decades have witnessed a surge in more pronounced influences, characterised by the proliferation of internationalised curricula, programmes, staff, students, and institutions. Luxon and Peelo (2009) underscore the necessity for tertiary-level curriculum design, course development, and pedagogical practices to embrace internationalisation, particularly in response to the increasing presence of international students and staff in higher education across many countries. Defining the internationalisation of the curriculum proves to be a challenging task. According to the OECD (1995, 9), an internationalised curriculum demonstrates an 'international orientation in content and/or form', with the aim of preparing students for performance in an international and multicultural context, catering to both domestic and foreign students. Leask (2013) outlines a five-stage process of curriculum internationalisation that involves imagining, revising/planning, implementing/acting, evaluating, and reviewing/reflecting. Kirk et al. (2018) argue that internationalising the curriculum plays a crucial role in fostering global citizenship skills among students.

Leask and Bridge (2013) shed light on the non-neutral nature of curriculum decisions, indicating their susceptibility to the influence of dominant paradigms and worldviews. They acknowledge occasional radical attempts to challenge prevailing worldviews and emphasise the complexity involved in internationalising the curriculum. McPhee (2009) observes that the landscape of teaching and learning in higher education is undergoing transformation due to a larger, more diverse student body, an emphasis on quality and accountability, rapidly advancing technological possibilities, uneven levels of student familiarity with these technologies, evolving government expectations, and the anticipation by students and employers that graduates will be equipped for rapidly changing and globalising workplaces.

Mertova (2013) emphasises that teaching and learning in many universities worldwide are internationalised, influenced in part by the growing presence of international students and staff.

The internationalised practices of tertiary teachers, who function as discipline experts and role models, position them to facilitate student preparedness for life in a rapidly globalising world (Sanderson [2011)](#page-14-9). Internationalisation has fundamentally reshaped the way curriculum unfolds (David and Hill [2020)](#page-13-10), as well as teaching and learning approaches (David and Hill [2021)](#page-13-11) in tertiary programmes and institutions, largely driven by the inclusion of internationalised staff, students, and programmes and it is important to appreciate the differences (Rodríguez-Izquierdo [2022)](#page-14-10).

In the development and delivery of an internationalised curriculum and instruction, educators and experts commonly undergo a systematic process. This involves assessing learning needs, establishing specific objectives, devising teaching and learning strategies, implementing plans of work, and evaluating instructional outcomes (IGI Global [2018)](#page-13-12). In the context of internationalised higher education, the curriculum is expected to be globally relevant (Zou et al. [2020](#page-14-11)), and many higher education institutions leverage their internationalisation strategies to modernise the curriculum more broadly. According to Robson ([2015](#page-14-12)), this modernisation is often driven by a desire to foster global mindsets, skills, and understandings. Leask ([2009](#page-13-13), 209) defines the internationalisation of the curriculum as integrating international, intercultural, and/or global dimensions into various facets, including content, learning outcomes, assessment tasks, teaching methods, and support services within a programme of study. Leask ([2015](#page-13-14)) proposes specific measures to enhance the impact of an internationalised curriculum on student learning, including attention to internationalising learning outcomes, content, teaching and learning activities, and assessment tasks. This approach goes beyond isolated, optional subjects and experiences for a minority of students, focusing on the learning of all students. The process is suggested to be systematic rather than sporadic, avoiding occasional international case studies scattered haphazardly across the programme of study. Van den Hende and Whitsed ([2022](#page-14-13)) distil essential lessons for internationalising the curriculum: a clear commitment to Curriculum Internationalisation (CI) communicated and demonstrated, identification and engagement of all stakeholders from the outset, opening channels for consistent, regular, and multimodal communication, recognition that CI is a longer-term change process, and a pragmatic approach. Such an approach addresses challenges noted by Clifford [(2009)](#page-13-15), who argued that the top-down nature of some internationalisation efforts faced resistance from academics, and approaches often failed to consider disciplinary differences. Scholars like Gopal ([2011](#page-13-16)) recommend preparing faculty for cross-cultural teaching by enhancing their intercultural competency.

The European University Association (EUA [2020](#page-13-17)) supports the meaningful integration of internationalisation in teaching and learning, tailored to the specific needs of each institution and country. It endorses the idea of an institutional ecosystem actively engaging staff, students, and all stakeholders in the internationalisation process. This comprehensive approach aligns with the evolving landscape outlined by Clifford ([2009](#page-13-15)) and acknowledges the significance of addressing disciplinary nuances when internationalising the curriculum.

The internationalisation of higher education brings forth both opportunities and challenges, contributing to a dynamic landscape. Tadaki ([2013](#page-14-14)) asserts that embracing internationalisation enables greater global networking in the knowledge economy, urging universities worldwide to leverage various means to capitalise on the benefits of global interconnectedness while avoiding the pitfalls of parochialism. However, critics, including Beck ([2012](#page-13-18)), caution that internationalisation may pose risks to local higher education systems by emphasising competition and commercialisation. In response, Schapper and Mayson ([2004](#page-14-15)) advocate for a counter-hegemonic, ongoing, comprehensive, multifaceted, and integrated approach to internationalisation, seeking alternatives to the Taylorisation of academic work. Contrary voices argue that internationalisation is imperative for relevance in modern times. Green and Mertova ([2009](#page-13-19)) emphasise that technology and the need for global alliances shape internationalisation dynamics in universities, particularly to better serve the increasing international student population. Marinoni, Egron-Polak, and Green [(2019](#page-14-16)) identify a multitude of benefits associated with the internationalisation of higher education, including enhanced international cooperation and capacity building, internationalisation of the curriculum,

improved institutional prestige, enhanced graduate employability, improved teaching and learning quality, increased international awareness and engagement with global issues by students, expanded international networking for faculty and researchers, diversified revenue generation, opportunities for benchmarking institutional performance against international good practices, and strengthened institutional research and knowledge production capacity. Specifically, in the context of the UAE, the author notes a growing trend where students increasingly prefer internationalised courses (David [2017a)](#page-13-20) and institutions (David [2017b](#page-13-21)). This underscores the importance and relevance of internationalisation in shaping the choices of students in the region.

The internationalisation of curriculum and instruction in higher education offers strategic opportunities along with challenges and risks. Ryan (2013) underscores the prevalence of opportunities over challenges, noting significant progress in internationalising curriculum and instruction, with the majority of challenges already addressed. Similar sentiments are echoed by others, asserting that internationalisation has profoundly transformed teaching and learning in higher education, positively influencing quality and competition, albeit accompanied by some confusion and anxiety (Mertova [2013)](#page-14-8). David and Motala ([2017](#page-13-22)) cautions against homogenising the curriculum, emphasising the importance of preserving diversity. On the other hand, Rensburg, Motala and David [(2016)](#page-14-17) emphasises that research collaboration among staff and institutions increases opportunities to internationalise higher education.

Additionally, the internationalisation of higher education creates opportunities for staff and student learning mobility (Rensburg, Motala and David [2015](#page-14-18)). It is also interesting to observe that internationalisation empowers students. Raby, Ward and Rhodes ([2021](#page-14-19)) highlight that international students indicated that their study abroad experience empowered them. Wang and Sun [(2022)](#page-14-20) advocate for an inclusive approach to internationalisation. Gallagher [(2023)](#page-13-23) indicates the role of transnational teacher educators in capacity building by nurturing future educators in the Arabian Gulf. In conclusion, the literature exploration indicates that numerous universities modify their curriculum and instruction to cultivate an internationalised environment, uniting students and staff from diverse nationalities for teaching and learning. These international or global universities frequently investigate curriculum, pedagogic, and assessment strategies that better cater to the needs of international students and staff. Various studies suggest that local students and staff also derive benefits from internationalisation dynamics as it enhances their experiences. To summarise, the review emphasises both the opportunities and challenges associated with internationalisation in the context of curriculum and instruction in higher education.

## **Methodology**

To investigate the dynamics and impacts of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction in higher education within the UAE, a suitable methodology was developed to address the primary research questions: 'What are the dynamics and impacts of the internationalisation of curriculum and instructions in higher educational institutions in the UAE? And how do these shape the higher education sector in the UAE?' The student survey questionnaire drew inspiration from the Tripod ([2024](#page-14-21)) 7Cs Framework of effective teaching. This framework encompasses seven central constructs for assessing teaching practices, categorised into three domains: personal, curricular, and academic support. The dimensions of personal support include 'Care' and 'Confer'; those of curricular support consist of 'Captivate', 'Clarify', and 'Consolidate'; and academic support involves 'Challenge' and 'Classroom Management'. Following confirmatory validation by two field experts, the instrument underwent a pilot study for further validation. The questionnaire was distributed to 20 respondents, specifically students in higher educational institutions at Dubai International Academic City (DIAC).

To evaluate the reliability of the quantitative questionnaire, Cronbach's alpha, serving as the measure of internal consistency (α), was employed, resulting in a value of 0.83. According to the guideline suggested by George and Mallery ([2003](#page-13-24)), a value exceeding 0.8 indicates good reliability. The questionnaire, developed by the authors based on the literature review of internationalisation in

<!-- page 7 -->

higher education, encompassed four dimensions: conceptualisation of the dynamics and impacts of internationalisation; impacts of internationalisation on the curriculum; impacts of internationalisation on instruction; and attitudes towards the impacts of internationalisation. This comprehensive approach ensures a nuanced exploration of the multifaceted aspects of internationalisation within the higher education context in the UAE.

The qualitative questionnaire as well underwent a thorough validation process, which included review by two field experts. Following confirmatory validation, a pilot study involving two academic staff members was conducted. They were assigned the task of completing the questionnaire and analysing it for credibility, with a specific focus on addressing the question of 'how congruent are the findings with reality' (Stahl and King [2020,](#page-14-22) 26) as a crucial step towards enhancing the trustworthiness of the instrument. Subsequently, the structured questionnaire was administered to a broader sample, comprising 123 students for quantitative data and 12 academic staff members responded on semi-structured questionnaire for qualitative data. The respondents are from higher educational institutions located in Dubai International Academic City (DIAC). The selection of DIAC was intentional, given its diverse hosting of various higher educational institutions.

Throughout the research process, standard ethical protocols were strictly observed in both data collection and analysis. The research received approval from the research and ethics committees of the institution affiliated with the author. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were also provided with the necessary background information about the research before their involvement.

Quantitative survey data underwent analysis using both descriptive and inferential statistics, offering a comprehensive overview of the numerical findings. Simultaneously, qualitative data were analysed using both deductive and inductive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke [2006)](#page-13-25). The main themes were identified from the existing literature, while sub-themes emerged during the data collection and subsequent analysis. Informed reflection supported the discussion and interpretations of the findings, facilitating the drawing of necessary inferences and conclusions. This multifaceted approach ensures a robust exploration of the dynamics and impacts of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction in higher education in the UAE.

## **Results, analysis and discussion**

Quantitative data were gathered through a structured questionnaire comprising 40 items, focusing on the four dimensions of the study. Within these, 32 items (eight for each dimension) were dedicated to exploring various facets of internationalisation, while an additional eight questions sought demographic details from participants. Respondents were initially presented with a screening question to determine whether their institution embraces internationalisation in curriculum, teaching, staffing, student recruitment, and other forms. The majority, 94.3%, responded affirmatively, 1.6% answered negatively, and 4.1% indicated uncertainty. A total of 123 completed responses were considered for further analysis. A reliability test was conducted to assess the internal consistency of the 32 items across the four dimensions. The results revealed that the Cronbach's alpha for two of the dimensions was above .8 (indicating high consistency), for one dimension it was above .7 (indicating good consistency), and for the remaining dimension, it was above .6 (indicating acceptable consistency). These findings suggest that the internal consistency of the instrument was good. [Table 1 ](#page-6-0)provides a summary of the reliability results.

The emergent sub-theme 3 highlighted the 'key features of internationalised curriculum'. In this context, R3 pointed out 'the emergence of standardised curriculum', while R7 considered 'internationalised curriculum as an opportunity for global experiences'. R9 noted 'internationalised curriculum enabling students with a global mindset/experience and preparing them for global exposure'. R8 indicated that 'internationalised curriculum is often diluted and neither helps reach global nor local best'. Sub-theme 4 related to the impacts of internationalisation on curriculum design and delivery. In this respect, R1, for example, indicated that 'some subjects, such as science and technology, are more conducive to internationalisation compared to others, such as arts, humanities, and social science that have contextual grounding'. Meanwhile, R5 suggested that 'staff and students are prepared to perform globally relevant tasks', while R6 cautioned that 'strong reliance on internationalisation might result in the loss of local knowledge'. R11 considered 'internationalised curriculum to develop cultural sensibility and global citizenship behavior'. R12 pointed out that 'designing and delivering internationalised curriculum supports staff in developing appropriate skills'. For R3, 'internationalised curriculum is rather demanding for some students as well as staff', while R4 indicated 'a lack of valid reasons and methods to adapt internationalised curriculum results in failure'. Most respondents indicated that staff are relatively well prepared to deliver internationalised curriculum, while continuous professional development might further support their readiness. Many respondents pointed out the lack of student consultation on the internationalisation of the curriculum, as this is not a common trend in many institutions. R8 recommended that 'modernising any curriculum should be relevant to the local and global market'. All the respondents considered their library to be relatively well-equipped to cater to internationalised curriculum.

Theme 3 focused on the 'impacts of internationalisation for instruction in higher education'. Respondents shared reflections primarily based on their experiences. Most of them acknowledged that instruction in their institution and programmes is relatively well internationalised. Sub-theme 5 related to the 'key features of internationalised instruction'. Regarding this sub-theme, the following responses are illustrative. For example, R5 indicated that the internationalisation of instruction supports 'the facilitation of collaborative teaching involving guest lectures and research peers', and R6 mentioned that internationalised instruction prepares students to be relevant in an internationalised environment. R8 argued that 'internationalised instruction helps satisfy a variety of learners using a common language of instruction and pedagogic strategies suitable for a wide range of student backgrounds'. R10 insisted that 'internationalised instruction must be universal and appealing to all'.

Sub-theme 6 highlighted the 'impacts of internationalisation on instructional design/delivery'. In this context, illustrative are the responses of R1, who pointed out 'the emergence of technology integration and online teaching', and R9, who indicated that 'internationalised instruction depends on instructors' international experience and their ability to relate to multicultural stakeholders'. Many respondents indicated that most staff are ready to embrace technology and other emerging trends, while necessary support for this might strengthen such readiness and interest among staff. R10

<!-- page 13 -->

recommended ensuring that 'internationalised curriculum and instruction must help students to gain the best'.

Theme 4 explored the approaches/prospects towards the impacts of internationalisation for higher education in the UAE. Most respondents considered that the trend 'internationalisation of higher education' will continue to grow widely, responses that relate to the sub-theme 7 'approaches towards internationalisation for higher education'. Specific comments include those of R6 who suggested that 'internationalisation helps institutions to build their brand appealing globally' and R8 who pointed out that 'internationalisation provides a global dimension for higher education', while R9 considered 'internationalisation as pervasive, imparting western influence'. In relation to sub-theme 8 'the future of internationalisation for C&I in higher education', R7 indicated that 'internationalisation helps to benchmark with global best'. For R12, 'internationalisation could be truly beneficial if institutional commitment, organisational leadership, and resource availability are optimized'. R7 cautions that 'overemphasis on internationalisation may result in the loss of national identity'. R12 insisted to 'embrace internationalisation as a prize and not as a burden'. Many respondents recommended preparing and supporting staff and students well for internationalisation of curriculum and instruction. Some of the respondents recommended foreseeing internationalisation beyond western countries with more interconnectedness and interdependence and collaboration. In this context, R2 recommended that 'the partnership pathways need to deliver knowledge exchange rather than unidirectional impact'. In general, the respondents relatively shared positive views about the impacts of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction in higher educational institutions in the UAE, highlighting the opportunities, challenges, and prospects by reducing the risks and optimising the benefits.

## **Conclusion**

This study delved into the intricate dynamics and far-reaching impacts of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction within higher educational institutions in the UAE. The quantitative data resoundingly underscores the substantial influence of internationalisation on curriculum and instruction in higher education in the UAE. Meanwhile, the qualitative data illuminate potential avenues for optimising these impacts, suggesting realistic and meticulous strategies. The study's implications and benefits extend to various stakeholders, providing valuable insights for policymakers, practitioners, and researchers. The findings suggest that higher education institutions could strategically internationalise curriculum, while academics and practitioners may meaningfully embrace internationalisation in their instruction as it may be suitable to the nature of the subjects. The empirical evidence discussed in the previous section serves as a foundation for informed decision-making and further exploration in the field. The process of internationalising curriculum and instruction in higher education unfolds with a panorama of opportunities and challenges for all involved parties. To navigate this trajectory strategically, stakeholders are encouraged to adopt measures that mitigate risks and maximise benefits. The research contends that, on the whole, internationalisation exerts predominantly positive impacts on curriculum and instruction in higher education in the UAE. However, the realisation of these benefits hinges on the conscientious adoption of relevant strategies by all stakeholders. The experiences and perceptions shared by students and staff regarding the impacts of internationalisation offer valuable contributions to the existing body of knowledge in this dynamic field.
