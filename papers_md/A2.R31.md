## Connectivity for whom and for what? A normative dimension of education hub

William Yat Wai L[oa](#page-1-0) and Felix Sai Kit Ng[b](#page-1-1)

a Department of International Education and Lifelong Learning, Hong Kong Institute of Education, Hong Kong; ^b^ Department of Applied Social Sciences, Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong

## ABSTRACT

This argument in this study is that the descriptive dimension of the concept of education hub, which focuses on the importance of connectivity in enhancing the competiveness of a place, is inadequate in addressing the educational values in globalising higher education. Therefore, it attempts to initiate a normative dimension of education hub through an empirical account of the experience of a group of Myanmar university students in Hong Kong and an engagement with the ideas of cosmopolitanisation and the notion of higher education as a 'transnational' human right. By considering these stories from Myanmar, this study reveals the adoption of the internationalisation of higher education in promoting transnational justice and development.

## KEYWORDS

Connectivity; Education hub; internationalisation; right to higher education; student mobility

## Introduction

The Hong Kong higher education system has undergone a process of internationalisation for over a decade. During this process, increasing the number of international students has entered the policy agenda and has become a rationale for the policy of developing Hong Kong into a regional education hub. In this context, several studies have recently been conducted on factors that influence the decision of international students to study in Hong Kong (Bodycott & Lai, [2012](#page-13-0); Li, [2011](#page-14-0); Li & Bray, [2007](#page-14-1); Ng, [2011](#page-14-2); Zeng & Watkins, [2011)](#page-15-0). Given an awareness of the effects of neo-liberal globalisation on higher education, many of these studies view enhancing the global interconnectedness of Hong Kong as a response to the challenges of global talent competition and the international higher education market. This instrumentalist approach, in which the notion of developing Hong Kong into a regional education hub is adopted in the examination of the role of international students (Cheung, Yuen, Yuen, & Cheng, [2010,](#page-13-1) [2011](#page-13-2); Lai & Maclean, [2011)](#page-13-3), is useful in terms of revealing the connection between higher education and economic growth and development in the context of neo-liberal globalisation.

However, the instrumentalist approach is inadequate in addressing the educational values in the process of the internationalisation of higher education (Stier, [2004,](#page-14-3) [2010)](#page-14-4) and may overlook the relevance of the notion of higher education as a right in the

<!-- page 3 -->

discussion of the rationales for enhancing interconnectedness. Thus, this study attempts to examine the relevance of the concept of higher education as a right to the understanding of interconnectedness by considering the experience of a group of Myanmar university students in Hong Kong. The study presented in this paper aims to identify and analyse factors that may be barriers and facilitators for this under-represented group in its pursuit and navigation of higher education in Hong Kong. It also examines the extent to which the current approach to the internationalisation process is related to the notion of higher education as a right.

This study initially delineates Hong Kong's policy initiatives in building a regional education hub and their rationales, thereby illustrating a descriptive dimension of the concept of education hub. It then turns to a method section that explains the way that a qualitative case study is conducted. Thereafter, it reports the research findings that are divided into three categories, namely, structural issues, community issues, and familyindividual issues. This categorisation is followed by a discussion on the way that the instrumentalist approach to internationalisation may have omitted the relevance of the right to education in understanding the mobility of students. The study concludes by arguing that the internationalisation of higher education must engage a cosmopolitan vision as a normative guide.

## Background: the descriptive dimension of education hub

The policy of developing Hong Kong as a regional education hub, which was announced in 2004, primarily aimed to recruit more non-local students for the internationalisation of higher education (Tung, [2004](#page-15-1)). However, according to the University Grants Committee (UGC), developing Hong Kong into an education hub is 'a policy of investment in the competitive knowledge economy by providing educational services to a population that is non-local with a strong emphasis on inward pull' (University Grants Committee, [2010,](#page-15-2) p. 54). This statement demonstrates that apart from diversifying the student population, the policy also served the mission of driving forward the economic and social development of Hong Kong, as education initiatives generate revenue and attract overseas talent (Knight, [2013](#page-13-4)). The idea of building an education hub emerged in a context of an economic decline during the late 1990s and early 2000s. Therefore, education was viewed as an industry that can be exported to satisfy the growing demand for education in neighbouring countries and regions, thereby providing a new force for economic growth. The idea of exporting education services was consolidated after the 2008 global financial crisis. In 2009, to create new business opportunities and enhance Hong Kong's competiveness, the government decided to consider educational services as one of the six new economic engines that could complement the traditional economic pillars (Tsang, [2009)](#page-14-5).

The education hub strategy was implemented by an increase of the number and types of non-local providers and consumers of education services. Non-local provision refers to the emergence of self-financing tertiary education programmes and branch campuses, which are run by overseas institutions. As regards non-local consumption, the number of non-local students in the tertiary education sector has continuously increased since the UGC expressed an intention to expand the non-local student population in 2004 (University Grants Committee, [2004)](#page-15-3). In 2010, the UGC raised

<!-- page 4 -->

the allowed proportion of non-local students from 10 to 20 per cent in the UGC-funded programmes of its institutions (UGC, [2010](#page-15-2)). Concurrently, the government relaxed the regulations on immigration and employment and increased scholarship opportunities to attract more non-local students. Specifically, the revised regulations allow non-local students to take part-time jobs and internships during their study period and to stay and work after the completion of their degrees. The government also provides new scholarship opportunities through the establishment of the HKD 1 billion HKSAR Government Scholarship Fund in 2008. Targeted scholarship schemes were established for non-local students from ASEAN countries, India, Korea and Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) member economies in 2012 and 2014 (Education Bureau, [2015](#page-13-5)). Consequently, the ratio of non-local enrolments in UGC-funded programmes has significantly increased from 2.86 to 15.33 per cent between 2001 and 2013 (University Grants Committee, [2015)](#page-15-4).

An important aspect of the education hub strategy is its economic imperative, which can be illustrated by two ideas. First, higher education is viewed as a tradable item in the context of the emergence of a global higher education market. Indeed, the neoliberal ideology and practices have widely spread around the world and have framed the global agenda for higher education development. Consequently, higher education is perceived as a profitable investment that can be used to generate revenue from tuition fees and other expenses of foreign students, and thus many countries have attempted to participate in the international trade in education services (Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development, [2004)](#page-14-6). This idea of revenue generation through participation in the international higher education market appears in Hong Kong's policy documents. However, as reported by Lee [(2014](#page-14-7)), policymakers tended to depreciate the relevance of this idea. Alternatively, they view the idea of human capital development as the core of the education hub strategy. In the age of knowledge-based economy, an important function of higher education is to cultivate human resources as well as to innovate and apply new technologies in economic activities (Marginson & Van Der Wende, [2007)](#page-14-8). In this regard, building education hubs is a way of developing human capital. This statement explains why 'talent competition' and 'brain drain and gain' become keywords in the illustration of a human capital approach to the internationalisation of higher education and the education hub strategy. The idea can be applied to explain the education hub strategy in Hong Kong. As the UGC notes, 'A vibrant research environment is essential to attract talents to Hong Kong. . . all talented people are attracted by the presence of other talented people in the same and related fields' (UGC, [2010](#page-15-2), p. 89). This statement indicates an awareness of the connection between the notion of education hub and human capital development.

The awareness of the essential role of higher education in national development in the globalisation era intimates the rationales for Hong Kong's education hub initiatives, which focus on the importance of connectivity in enhancing the competiveness of the city (Chow & Loo, [2015)](#page-13-6). However, this instrumentalist approach has overlooked the relevance of the recent conceptualisation of higher education as a right (Burke, [2012;](#page-13-7) Hodgson, [1998](#page-13-8); McCowan, [2013)](#page-14-9) in the discussion of ideological rationales for interconnectedness in the arena of higher education, and this approach is inadequate in addressing and responding to the question, 'what is missing in the process of internationalisation?' (Ng, [2012)](#page-14-10). To demonstrate an alternative dimension of the notion of

<!-- page 5 -->

education hub, this study considers a group of Myanmar university students studying in Hong Kong with the assistance of the HKSAR Government Scholarship Fund and other assistance of international education aids. Through literature review, observations, and interviews, this study intends to organise and analyse the facilitators and barriers for this under-represented group in their pursuit and navigation through higher education in Hong Kong. Thus, the way that the interconnectedness of a place is related to the basis and nature of the right to higher education is understood.

## **Stories from Myanmar **

The focus here is on students from Myanmar (formerly known as Burma) because significant attention has been paid to students from mainland China, who form the majority of the non-local student population in Hong Kong, in previous studies on international students in the city. Examining the experience of a group of Myanmar students who gained access to higher education because of the financial support of government scholarships and other educational aids is relevant and effective in evaluating the non-economic aspects and episodes of connectivity in the context of the marketisation and commercialisation of higher education.

We started the study with a review of the literature on Myanmar's higher education system and the circumstances of surrounding migrant and refugee students from the Thai-Myanmar border region. Based on the literature review, 14 semi-structured interviews were conducted in 2013. Given that the respondents were restricted to Myanmar students who were studying in Hong Kong and that this group of students was relatively small, this part of the non-local student population was difficult to reach. Hence, the respondents were recruited through chain-referral sampling. The respondents are from ethnically diverse backgrounds. Some of them have refugee and forced migrant backgrounds because their families have fled from the country because of political prosecution and economic hardship and have settled in the Thai-Myanmar border region or in Thailand. It is noteworthy that most of them were studying subjects of social sciences at the undergraduate level in Hong Kong. Table 1 summarises the background information of the 14 respondents.

The interview data were sorted for themes. The sorting scheme was developed based on the sociological approach to education, which assumes connections between education and social changes at the macro level. Indeed, early sociological studies on education focus on the usefulness of education to society instead of individuals (Colclough, 2012). This perspective is further illustrated in Buchmann and Hannum's (2001) study of education and social stratification in developing countries. For them, the provision of educational opportunities is a consequence of the dynamic interrelationship between various factors at different levels in developing countries. In light of this analysis of the effects of multilevel factors on educational opportunities, three broad themes, namely, sociopolitical factors, community-school factors, and family-individual factors, were used to sort the interview data.

## Determinants of the decision to study abroad

## Sociopolitical factors

The sociopolitical contexts of Myanmar are essential for understanding the decisions to study abroad by the students from the country. Two political issues are relevant to illustrate the recent political developments of the country. First, after experiencing a long period of military dictatorship, Myanmar has been undergoing a series of political reforms since President Thein Sein came into office in 2011 (Steinberg, 2013). Since then, a more relaxed political atmosphere has been created. Opposition political leaders were released, and opposition political parties were permitted to participate in the elections in recent years (Topich & Leitich, 2013). Although queries were made about the sustainability of the political reforms (Rogers, 2012), suggesting that the country is slowly transitioning from military dictatorship seems reasonable, despite political instability and associated social problems remaining concerns. Second, ethnic conflicts exist in the country. Indeed, Myanmar is one of the most culturally diverse countries in the world. Some 130 ethnic groups have their own languages and dialects (Topich & Leitich, 2013). According to online statistical data (Central Intelligence Agency, 2013), Burmans account for 68 per cent of the population. The second largest ethnical group is Shan (nine per cent), followed by Karen (seven per cent), Rakhine (four per cent), Chinese (three per cent), Indian (two per cent), Mon (two per cent), and others (five per cent). According to this demographic profile, Myanmar is dominated by the Burman majority in different domains, in different ways, and to different extents. In addition, conflicts exist between the central government and the ethnic minorities in the country. Although President Thein Sein attempted to reduce the tensions among ethnic groups by inviting former dissidents to return to the country (Steinberg, 2013, p. 195), ethnic hostility remains a concern in Myanmar. In short, the complex ethnical profile explains some of the political tensions and instabilities in the country.

These political contexts and developments provide a useful contextual lens in viewing the interview data. For example, Susan, a Burman, left Myanmar because her father participated in the anti-government protests in 2007. Her family therefore fled to the Thai–Myanmar border region. This family background affected her study. She attended 2 years of secondary school in Myanmar before moving to Thailand. However, she expressed that she was unable to continue her study in schools in Thailand because of her refugee background. She tried to attend a school in the refugee camp. However, she

<!-- page 7 -->

decided to quit because she had safety concerns given that the school was run by Karen. Consequently, she stayed in the referee camp for 2 years without formal education and decided to study for General Educational Development tests and to apply for scholarships to study abroad. Her experience reflects the way that political issues and tensions between ethnic groups restrict the educational opportunities in local communities and encourage students to pursue education outside their home country. Several respondents also noted that their ethnic minority identities affected their educational choices. Philip preferred to study a diploma programme run by Kachin, which was not recognised by the authorities, rather than studying in the formal schooling system. Zac noted that as a Shan, he felt uncomfortable studying in Burmese and accepting the official history of Myanmar. Alan, a Kachin, also felt unhappy with the policy of the medium of instruction. For him, teaching in Burmese is considered a form of oppression. These reasons somehow explain their decision to leave the country for better education.

Indeed, most of the informants indicated that pursuing better education was the main reason for their decision to study abroad. However, the quality issues in Myanmar's education system are likely related to the political contexts. As Dolly said,

Because of political reasons, universities were closed when I graduated from high school. I was able to go to Pan Kachin College for a diploma only. Two years later, I was enrolled in a public university. However, I quit before completing the first year because I was not happy with the quality of education in Burma. Then, I went to Thailand, and worked in an NGO.

Evidently, higher education development was closely connected to the political developments in Myanmar. All universities were closed for 2 years after the student protests in 1988. The student strikes in 1996 and 1998 resulted in a further three-year closure. Owing to the active participation of students in political activities, many universities originally located in Yangon were relocated to other parts of the country, and undergraduate program were taught in campuses far from the urban centre. These steps are taken as measures to keep students away from politics (Lall, 2008). In addition, as reported by Lwin (2006, 2010), the Myanmar government exercises strict controls over the university sector to impose its political ideology. Universities were under centralised control and had limited autonomy. Lwin therefore argues that 'the structure of a university has all the appearance of an army with the rector looking like an army general commanding colonels, majors and captains' (Lwin, 2012, p. 8).

Such situations are reflected in our interview data. For example, Chloe earned a bachelor's degree in English in Myanmar. However, she expressed hesitation about the quality of the qualification because the attendance requirement was loose and most of the courses were conducted in distance learning mode. Her understanding of such an arrangement was due to the government's restrictions on university operations. Yang completed his bachelor's degree in electronics engineering in Myanmar. He shared his observations on the relationship between political issues and the quality of higher education:

The differences in the course contents between Myanmar and Hong Kong are minor. However, the levels of university are different; that in Myanmar is lower. I would not blame my professors because this is related to the political environment in the country,

## **Community-school factors **

Community-school factors include the availability of resources at the community and institutional levels, teaching and learning culture, and employment opportunities and career prospects in Myanmar.

Despite President Thein Sein addressing the need for improvement in public education in his inaugural address (Steinberg, 2013, p. 194) and ordering to prioritise quality enhancement in higher education (Department of Higher Education, 2014), educational resources have been scarce in Myanmar. According to the United Nations Educational Scientific and Cultural Organization's (UNESCO) data, the public expenditure on education accounted for 0.8 per cent of the gross domestic product (GDP) of the country, out of which approximately 15 per cent was spent on higher education, compared with 56 per cent on primary education and 29 per cent on secondary education in 2011 (United Nations Educational Scientific and Cultural Organization UIS, 2014). All higher education institutions in Myanmar are publicly funded (Department of Higher Education, 2014), and the country adopts a Soviettype higher education model in which every institution is under the authority of a central ministry. Although political factors are identified as a significant influence, the lack of resources is considered as another major cause of the quality issues in higher education in Myanmar. It leads to the concerns, which have been reported in the previous section, and encourages students to seek overseas educational opportunities. Such an analysis is confirmed by Heidi's assessment of higher education in Hong Kong. She said, 'I like Hong Kong's education very much. The facilities and equipment are great here'.

The quality issues in higher education are related to the teaching and learning culture in the country. Our interview data suggest a distinctive difference between the teaching and learning culture in Hong Kong and that in Myanmar. Whereas Hong Kong has a more student-centred learning culture, Myanmar retains the traditional teacher-centred model of teaching. This view is widely shared among the respondents. Tin (2008) suggests that the teaching style in Myanmar is connected to the religious contexts, in which schools are established in a hierarchical structure and the commands of superiors are not to be questioned or challenged (p. 114). The conservative atmosphere is reflected in the pedagogy. As reported by the respondents, teachers in Myanmar emphasise the memorisation of facts and give tests to measure the memory of the students. This pedagogical conservatism is reflected by Chloe's comment:

'I found that Burmese education was deeply set in the traditional instruction and passive learning style. Students were passive in learning and professors were closeminded. The mere goal was to pass the examinations'.

By contrast, the respondents appreciate the teaching style in Hong Kong, which connects them to critical thinking and analytical skills. As Philip remarked,

'I like the teaching style in Hong Kong, which encourages students to think independently. It is quite different to the way I experienced in Burma, which emphasises memory training'.

Zac compares his learning experience in Thailand, the Philippines, and Hong Kong, and thinks that Hong Kong's education is the best among those of the three countries because it encourages students to think independently.

Zac's experience also reveals the relevance of career prospects in his decision to study abroad. For him, studying abroad was a result of the hardship he endured during his time in Thailand and Malaysia, where he worked as an illegal migrant worker. He was disappointed with the working conditions and decided to look for educational opportunities. He therefore returned to Myanmar, learned English, and went to the Philippines and Hong Kong for higher education. Sally also faced difficulty in finding employment opportunities in Myanmar, although she earned a medical degree from a public university. She could not find a job in the medical field and had to work in NGOs providing community services. Largely, our respondents confirmed that their decision to study abroad is linked to the lack of career prospects in Myanmar. However, our interview data reflect a situation that is different to the report in Perkins and Neumayer's study (2014), which has indicated that university quality is not significant in shaping international student mobility. By contrast, our data suggests that the pursuit of better education is an important motivating factor for studying abroad among our respondents.

## Family-individual factors

Existing literature suggests that plans for post-graduation settlement (Robertson, 2011) and social ties to others in foreign countries influence decisions to study abroad (Collins, 2008). However, these two factors are not shown to be highly relevant in the data in this study. Among the 14 respondents, only Mike said that he had ties across borders. He wanted to study abroad because his sister worked in Singapore. He visited her and liked the city. His top destination choice was Singapore rather than Hong Kong, and he hoped to work in Singapore after graduation. All other respondents said that they would return to Myanmar in the long term, although seven of them were interested in pursuing further studies in English-speaking countries, and one respondent wanted to work in Hong Kong for several years after graduation. An interesting aspect of this part of the data is the commitment of the respondents to the development of Myanmar. For example, Sandy has a forced migrant background. Her father was a politician. He was exiled by the military government around 20 years ago. Sandy was adopted by her relatives and stayed in Myanmar. She moved to Thailand and was reunited with her family after her completion of high school. She was completing her bachelor's degree and would study in the US for her master's degree. However, she noted that she was keen to return to Myanmar, although her family members hesitated to move back to their home country. Louie has a similar family background. His father was a political prisoner. With this background, Louie was brought to Thailand, where he lived and studied for around 5 years. He said that his father's influence was a key factor affecting his choice of studying public policy. He noted that although he had planned to seek a master's degree abroad, he was keen on returning to Myanmar and pursuing a political career there. His goal was to become a member of parliament.

It is noteworthy that only four respondents, including Louie, said that the subjects they were studying were their topic choices. Several respondents indicated that their choices somehow were tied to their scholarship opportunities. However, they were eager to link their study to their career development plans. As Susan put it, 'I hope I can become a social worker and work in Kachin, because there are many refugees. I would like to help these refugees in the region, even though I have never been there'. Sally also said,

I will return to Myanmar after graduation. I would rejoin the agency (which is in Kachin) in which I was working. I am a member of Kachin Peace Network. I hope I will be able to contribute to peace and stability in Kachin.

Indeed, several other respondents had similar personal visions and hoped to devote themselves to the development of Myanmar or their communities after graduation. The significance of this part of the data urges us to rethink the emphasis on studying abroad as capital-enhancing opportunities (Xiang & Shen, 2009). We argue that this account of international student mobility stresses the marketised setting of education provision, in which higher education is primarily seen as private consumption. The personal visions of the respondents may exemplify the non-economic considerations in the decisions to study abroad, thereby implying the publicness of international student mobility.

## Link between the factors

The interview data suggests that the macrostructural forces (i.e., the sociopolitical factors) play an important role in shaping factors at the meso (e.g., education

<!-- page 11 -->

quality) and micro (e.g., personal vision) levels. Indeed, as already discussed, the quality of education is closely connected with the government's policy. In addition, education is commonly used as an ideological control by governments in many nondemocratic countries. In this sense, the quality issues in higher education and the individuals' life-course aspirations and plans reported in this study are political in essence.

Furthermore, the data in this study suggest that the push factors in Myanmar are stronger than the pull factors in Hong Kong. For instance, democracy is identified as a relevant variable in the analysis of the decision to study abroad (Perkins & Neumayer, [2014)](#page-14-20). However, the respondents in the present study are not pulled by the presence of democracy in Hong Kong but are pushed by the lack of democracy in Myanmar. Indeed, three respondents noted that Hong Kong was not their top choice, and six out of the seven respondents who indicated the interest to seek postgraduate study after completing their undergraduate study preferred going to other English-speaking countries rather than staying in Hong Kong. This preference may reveal that Hong Kong is not very attractive in their view. Importantly, many of the respondents are able to study in Hong Kong because of the financial support of the Hong Kong Government Scholarship. These issues constitute a query about the relevance of providing scholarship to this group of students to the policy goals of promoting the higher education industry in Hong Kong and of bringing the city to the global competition for talent, which are embedded in the internationalisation agenda for higher education in Hong Kong.

Thus, one's views should be shifted from a human-capital-based perspective to a right-based perspective on the internationalisation of higher education. Specifically, we argue that the instrumental approaches used by the Hong Kong Government in its policy on the internationalisation of higher education is inadequate in understanding the educational value of international student mobility and in rationalising its recruitment of international students (Lo, [2015](#page-14-21)).

## Future prospects: a normative dimension

As captioned in the previous section, the existing instrumentalist approach to the internationalisation of higher education focuses on the economic utility of international student mobility but is inadequate in rationalising the policy that facilitates the international mobility of the respondents who have a disadvantaged background. Therefore, the notion of higher education as a right has been examined. The existing literature offers different arguments for the notion. For example, Hodgson [(1998)](#page-13-8) argues that higher education is a right because it has social and individual functions; its social utility is a prerequisite to its individual use that promotes dignity, development, and welfare of individuals. Burke ([2012](#page-13-7)) suggests that widening the access to higher education should be undertaken as a project for social justice to tackle the complex issues of inequality, exclusion, and misrecognition, given that neo-liberal discourse and framework have marginalised the concept of social justice in the domain of social policy. Similarly, McCowan ([2013)](#page-14-9) notes that 'all people have a right throughout life to engage in educational processes that are intrinsically as well as instrumentally valuable, and that embody respect for "human rights"' (p. 173). These arguments provide a right

<!-- page 12 -->

perspective on higher education that forms a base for an argument for the borderless provision of higher education.

Furthermore, researchers recognise that access to higher education provides extra benefits to students from refugee backgrounds. For example, Dryden-Peterson (2010) considers higher education an instrument of protection that keeps the youth out of military service. She also notes that higher education is an important component of post-conflict reconstruction and personal development planning. Zeus (2011) believes that higher education plays 'a key role in bridging the gap between relief and development by building refugees' capacity and self-reliance' (p. 264), and 'can serve the purpose of refugee empowerment' (p. 271). In short, higher education serves a function of capacity building (p. 260). She argues that the provision of higher education should not be constrained to institutions of nation-states and should not be bounded by national borders because of the benefits stated earlier and the usual unavailability of quality higher education in many developing countries. This argument rationalises the significant role of student mobility in promoting 'transnational' human rights in the context of cross-border higher education.

These ideas are highly relevant to our discussion on the educational values of global interconnectedness. Our interview data indicates that the political and community factors have limited the access of the respondents to quality higher education in Myanmar. This finding reveals the importance of the borderless provision of higher education (i.e., the educational opportunities in Hong Kong) to enhancing the access of this underprivileged group to higher education. Their personal visions of contributing to their home country connects the individual functions to the social functions of higher education, thereby implying that international student mobility not only plays a role in empowerment and development at the individual level but also helps promote social justice and development in a transnationalised context. This implication shows that the effects of student mobility on human development may be more evident than promoting individuals' rights to education.

Viewed from a conceptual perspective, this case study illustrates the link between the goal of enhancing interconnectedness and that of nurturing humanity. As argued earlier, the neo-liberal policy and its influences on education ideology impose a constraint on our understanding of global interconnectedness (e.g., international student mobility). Under this influence of neo-liberalism, the purpose of recruiting international students is narrowly defined as a way of stimulating economic growth and development (e.g., generating national income and competing for talent). However, the process of the internationalisation of higher education carries a cosmopolitan vision that guides us to re-evaluate 'the relevance of the concepts of 'borders' and 'national outlook' in examining the cross-border student flow and the policy of exporting higher education services' (Lo & Ng, 2013, p. 44) and to view higher education development from a transnational perspective. Specifically, referring to the thesis of cosmopolitanisation by Beck (2006, 2011), we argue that the instrumentalist approach presents an exclusive duality, in which national and international dimensions are mutually exclusive. This unequivocal model justifies a universal agenda of market competition at a global scale, as it retains the significance of national borders in understanding globalisation. Nevertheless, the instrumentalist approach cannot properly respond to the proliferation of transnational and translocal risks in the context of globalisation because

<!-- page 13 -->

its logic of unequivocalness has oversimplified local, national, regional, ethnical, or religious cultures and traditions. Thus, the logic of equivocalness, which represents a form of inclusive pluralism and particularly views local, national, and global dimensions as a side-by-side existence, should be adopted in the internationalisation process.

Given an awareness of the impossibility of living in a world without borders, the logic is important in terms of promoting a cosmopolitan empathy, with which we are able to address both opportunity and threat attached in global risks, recognise local and national differences, and adopt a mélange principle interpenetrating local, national, ethnic, religious, and cosmopolitan cultures and traditions. The adoption of these principles infers a paradigm shift from a national outlook to a cosmopolitan vision, which involves a sense of borderlessness, in analysing and countering social problems (Lo & Ng, 2013).

Adopting this cosmopolitan vision to the internationalisation of higher education enables us to view Hong Kong as a part of the global higher education system. Importantly, this understanding of the positioning of Hong Kong in the global higher education system consolidates the argument about adopting the initiatives of enhancing the connectivity among people from around the world by internationalisation as a project of nurturing humanity and global citizenship (Ng, 2012). It also rationalises the argument stating that talent flow can be seen as part of the global brain circulation and global knowledge production, whereby non-local students are prepared for and connected to the global academic community (Lo, 2015). We argue that this cosmopolitan vision is greater than idealism, which is only connected to the moral world (Stier, 2004); instead, it is a reflexive practice denoting a form of cosmopolitan realism because the world has been experiencing the disappearance of borders in different aspects of life and activities, which unfolds the reality of cosmopolitanisation (Beck, 2011). Therefore, a cosmopolitan vision, as a normatively guided approach, is needed in critically examining transnationalised social reality and navigating the way to becoming an education hub.

## **Conclusion **

Using a three-level framework, this study reported the determinants of the decision to study abroad of a group of Myanmar students. In doing so, the study revealed the significance of international student mobility in promoting the right to higher education in developing countries. We believe that this conceptualisation is especially important in examining higher education and development in transitional societies, such as Myanmar. The country has been undergoing significant transitions in its political arena since 2011. Some of the respondents noted that these changes are factors encouraging them to return to the country and to contribute to their homeland after they have completed their studies. Myanmar needs a more educated population to sustain its reforms and opening up because of its years of isolation. In this context, the diaspora may become a driving force for further changes and development in the country. Nevertheless, what is the role of Hong Kong in sustaining Myanmar's transition process? The inexistence of special tie between the two places guides us to have a deeper reflection on the significance of national borders in the design and implementation of approaches and strategies for the internationalisation of higher education. We

<!-- page 14 -->

consider this case study as a preliminary step towards further discussing the significance of cosmopolitanism in higher education development.
