## Federated Learning for IoMT Applications: A Standardization and Benchmarking Framework of Intrusion Detection Systems

Amneh Alamleh, O. S. Albahri, A. A. Zaidan, A. S. Albahri, A. H. Alamoodi, B. B. Zaidan, Sarah Qahtan, H. A. Alsatar, Mohammed S. Al-Samarraay, and Ali Najm Jasim

Abstract—Efficient evaluation for machine learning (ML)based intrusion detection systems (IDSs) for federated learning (FL) in the Internet of Medical Things (IoMTs) environment falls under the standardisation and multicriteria decision-making (MCDM) problems. Thus, this study is developing an MCDM framework for standardising and benchmarking the ML-based IDSs used in the FL architecture of IoMT applications. In the methodology, firstly, the evaluation criteria of ML-based IDSs are standardised using the fuzzy Delphi method (FDM). Secondly, the evaluation decision matrix (DM) is formulated based on the intersection of standardised evaluation criteria and a list of ML-based IDSs. Such formulation is achieved using a dataset with 125,973 records, and each record comprises 41 features. Thirdly, the integration of MCDM methods is formulated to determine the importance weights of the main and sub standardised security and performance criteria, followed by benchmarking and selecting the optimal ML-based IDSs. In this phase, the Borda voting method is used to unify the different ranks and perform a group benchmarking context. The following results are confirmed. ## I. INTRODUCTION

HE real-time intrusion detection system (IDS) is a common cyber security development to deal with the major issues of information and system security through detecting the malicious activity of host and/or network environments. As the network complexity increases, network systems become more vulnerable to various security problems, including intrusions.

A network or computer intrusion is described as an attempt to violate the security of a network or computer, such as a breach of confidentiality, integrity or availability [1]. Recently,

IDS faces significant major challenges from several perspectives: the massive size of network traffic data, high-dimensional training dataset, frequent alterations in environments, changing nature of intrusions and the need for real-time detections. A tremendous amount of detection methods and strategies have been proposed to overcome these challenges; machine learning (ML) method is one of the most common [2]. ML techniques allow the IDS to generate baselines of trustworthy activity known as the trust model [3]. IDS uses different techniques for detecting anomalies. Developing reliable IDS is critical for effectively mitigating various sorts of threats to efficiently mitigate different types of attacks [4]. Therefore, ML technologies are being utilised for the detection of attacks and system design of effective IDS. ML techniques, especially shallow models, have improved significantly in recent years and are now widely used in various fields [5], including healthcare systems. Numerous ML models have been applied to current IDSs to improve IDS research and boost detection rates whilst decreasing false alarm rates and processing costs [6]. For instance, [6] used ML techniques with IDS to learn from traffic (normal/up normal), and the model's training process was performed on the dataset to discover patterns indicative of malicious traffic. ML models have been successfully used for IDS research because of their superior features for detecting intrusion. However, they frequently necessitate a

<!-- page 2 -->

central authority to process the data acquired from all network users. The prediction accuracy decreases as the network scales due to the high packet loss rate associated with this centralisation requirement.

The Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) is a collection of software applications and medical devices which can be connected via networking technologies to healthcare services and information systems[7], [8]. IoMT is playing an essential part in the healthcare industry by increasing electronic device accuracy, reliability and productivity. IoMT enables safe communication between wireless and remote devices over the Internet [9], which allows faster and more flexible monitoring of medical information. According to a recent Deloitte survey, the global IoMT market would grow from $41 billion in 2017 to $158 billion by 2022 [10]. Certain IoMT devices can generate huge amounts of health data (e.g., sleep monitoring with EEG and EMG signals) through the use of sensors [11]. IoMT demands a complete security infrastructure built on effective and efficient IDS due to the sensitivity and stringent rules surrounding healthcare data (e.g., health information gathered by sensors) [12]. Attacks on IoMT can potentially cause significant physical harm and life-threatening damage to the patients introducing multitude of security and privacy concerns. Therefore, IDS is necessary to secure the network of connected medical devices, hospital network topology and medical centres.

By contrast, Google presented the notion of federated learning (FL) to enable mobile device training whilst preserving privacy [13]. FL is a distributed ML technique which trains models on a huge corpus of decentralised data. FL fundamental is an example of a broader concept of 'bringing the code to the data, rather than the data to the code', which solves the fundamental issues of data ownership, privacy and localisation [14]. A central entity is unnecessary for FL to work because FL provides collaborative learning between devices without the necessity for data sharing [15], wherein ML models can be trained on data distributed across multiple servers and devices [16]. FL is fundamentally composed of two steps: local learning and model transmission; both of which are designed to assure data privacy and cost reduction [17]. In each round, the FL server selects a group of clients to engage in the learning process and provides them with the current global model. Each round in the FL server begins with selecting a group of clients to engage in the learning process and provides them with the current global model [18]. After the global model is obtained, each client trains the model locally using their own data. These clients would then transmit their newly constructed models to the FL server to aggregate them. This procedure is repeated several times until the desired result is obtained. In this context, the clients can profit from others' data without sharing personal information with the FL server [19]. Thus, many researchers have examined the application of FL in various scenarios involving sensitive data, such as developing predictive models for health diagnosis and fostering collaboration between different hospitals [20], [21]. ## II. CHALLENGES OF IDS FOR FL IN IOMT

Using IDS for FL to identify anomalies in local IoMT devices has been recommended by researchers. As a result, the addition of IDS to FL enables ML to deliver customised protection mechanisms for various networks and IoMT devices. Furthermore, it alleviates the computational load on the central processing server, optimises bandwidth use, protects the privacy of health data and manages various health data communications. However, FL does not ensure privacy when malicious servers or users are present. Protecting sensitive health information, such as medical records, is challenging [22].

Recent studies have demonstrated unequivocally that malicious users may exist in FL and can infer the patients' healthcare information merely from the shared models [23]. Consequently, FL security and privacy concerns, particularly in the context of IoMT applications, would demand additional consideration via the use of IDS [24]. Potentially malicious healthcare institutions might inject ML models with incorrect or fake data, which causes the trained model to provide incorrect predictions by misclassifying the healthcare institution's economic losses and authorising its operation as normal. A lack of trust between parties or a secure communication channel for transmitting the local ML model updates is a typical concern in the attacks. Various protocols, service types, source/destination IP addresses and more are usually included in an IDS. If these data are transferred through open communication channels or even insecure routes, then it could be disastrous to the users and the server given that they might have a direct effect on people's lives [25]. Compared with data stored permanently on the device, the data in transit are unprotected by the device's security mechanisms [21]. Several IDS studies presented plausible implementation solutions associated with the identified challenges using ML models [24], [26]. Unexpectedly, numerous studies on IDS are offered to keep up with advancements in security issues.

Many ML-based IDSs are available, and this fact can be found in the literature [26]–[29]. However, the increase in the internet applications of FL resulted in more sophisticated attacking strategies, which required the answer to the question '*What is the* *better IDS system which can serve the FL for IoMT applications* *and health data privacy preservation?*' [27]. To our knowledge, no study has been presented to IDSs for FL in IoMT applications.

## III. RELATED WORKS FOR BENCHMARKING OF ML-BASED IDS

The IDS faced a wide variety of limitations and needed to compromise for resolving the trade-off between the performance and security aspects [28]. Selecting the best IDS classifier is critical because it improves the security of the computer networks and healthcare information systems and provides comprehensive information to IoMT developers, which can help manufacture effective and efficient versions of IDSs. According to the huge number of developed supervised ML-based IDS studies, this field continuously faces a major challenge in the evaluation of ML classification models used in IDSs because of the discussed evaluation criteria of security and performance [29], [30]. Table I shows the related studies of evaluation and benchmarking of ML-based IDSs.

On the basis of different evaluation criteria in the academic literature, the authors attempted to evaluate different classification models used in the IDS to select the best one. The literature analysis shows no agreement on which criteria are the most suitable in evaluating IDS classifiers because the current studies depend on a single incomplete aspect [35]. Each study conducted numerous evaluations based on a set of security criteria, and the majority of studies overlooked performance. To our knowledge, no exclusive study presented a reliable benchmarking solution of IDS classifiers based on the standardised

evaluation for FL. Three significant issues could be encountered in providing a comprehensive evaluation solution for IDS classifiers. Firstly, several evaluation criteria must be considered whilst taking the most suitable ones based on standardisation procedure [36]. Secondly, the evaluation criteria do not have the same importance; thus, properly increasing the significance of some standardised criteria and reducing others are necessary in the evaluation process of IDS classifiers [37]. Thirdly, criteria conflict/trade-off should be considered. According to the discussed issues, the evaluation and benchmarking of IDS classification models fall under standardisation and complex

<!-- page 4 -->

multicriteria decision-making (MCDM) problems. For eliminating such problem complexity, the fuzzy Delphi method (FDM) [38] must be used to solve the standardisation problem of IDS classifier evaluation criteria and the MCDM approach-based solution is highly recommended to overcome the evaluation and benchmarking problem of IDS classifiers. Several MCDM methods are developed for solving the problem of evaluation and benchmarking in various fields [39], [40]. Vlsekriterijumska Optimizcija I Kaompromisno Resenje (VIKOR) is a frequently used and effective method because it utilises quantitative or qualitative data to select an option from a set of options [39]. However, VIKOR cannot assign weights to the criteria; thus, it requires an external weighing mechanism for this purpose. Evaluating the precise weights of the criteria is essential because it might affect the outcomes of the evaluation and benchmark of IDS classifiers. Determining the weights subjectively using the direct rating method is suitable due to the limited number of the main evaluation criteria of IDS classifiers (i.e., security and performance) [41]. The reason is that it has a considerable reputation in simulating human decision for weighing the limited criteria numbers. However, the most common objective weighted method called entropy weighting method [42] is a proper solution for obtaining importance degrees to each subcriterion because it is overly difficult for the experts to determine the degree of preference between the sub-criteria subjectively due to the huge numbers of sub-evaluation criteria of IDS classifiers. Thus, considering the FL for IoMT applications, this study aims to develop a standardisation and benchmarking framework of IDSs using the integration of direct rating and entropy and VIKOR methods.

## IV. METHODOLOGY

Phase I: Standardisation of evaluation criteria based on FDM This phase presents the determination process of evaluation criteria standardisation using FDM. Fifteen security evaluation criteria and five performance evaluation criteria of IDS classification models are collected from the literature. These criteria need to be standardised by experts to determine which are essential and should be used in evaluating and benchmarking IDS classification models. The steps of FDM are explained as follows [43]:

Step1: Expert selection

The authors have selected and contacted three professional experts from the field of IDS research and development to obtain their opinion for examining and finding the most important set of evaluation criteria.

Step 2: Design expert form

The expert form was developed and used for the experts' opinion data collection following the structure and design of studies [44], [45]. Such questionnaire contains information on the research conducted, information on the expert and evaluation criteria of the IDS classification models.

Step 3: Expert form dissemination and data collection

The developed expert form was disseminated to the experts by broadcasting the form URL by email. The experts were asked to assess the evaluation criteria regarding their importance in the security and performance groups.

Linguistic variables were translated to triangular fuzzy numbers (m1, m2, m3). In the present study, a five-point Likert scale was selected to collect the experts' feedback [34]. Table III shows the linguistic variables for assigning criteria weight by the experts' opinion [46], [47]. Then, the average of the fuzzy numbers was calculated and divided by the experts' number [44].

Step 5: Calculating threshold value

Threshold value (d), which is the distance between every two fuzzy numbers, was calculated based on the vertex method using (1). The value of threshold (d) should be less than or equal to 0.2 \ (d < = 2) as a first condition for accepting any of the evaluation criteria in security and performance groups [44].

$$
d(m,n) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{3}[(m_1 - n_1)^2 + (m_2 - n_2)^2 + (m_3 - n_3)^2]}
(1)
$$

Step 6: Computing the expert's consensus

The percentage of the expert's consensus was calculated. It must be greater than/equal to 75%, which may fulfil the second criterion acceptance condition.

Step 7: Defuzzification

The defuzzification process was performed using the following equation:

$$ A = 1/3 * (m1 + m2 + m3) \tag{2} $$

In this step, the third condition of criterion acceptance was achieved, wherein the value of (A) should be higher than/equal to the median value (\alpha - cut value) of 0.5 [48]–[50].

Phase II: Decision matrix construction

The NSL-KDD dataset is presented as a possible solution to some of the KDDCUP'99 dataset's inherent flaws. It contains 125973 records, and each record consists of 41 features. The records are instances of network attacks, including 23 classes: one normal and 22 types of attacks: neptune, warezclient, ipsweep, portsweep, teardrop, nmap, satan, smurf, pod, back, guess passwd, ftp write, multihop, rootkit, buffer overflow, imap, warezmaster, phf, land, loadmodule, spy and perl [51]. Accordingly, the binary classification task is performed; 22 attack types are considered the attack class, whereas the last type (i.e., normal) is considered the normal class. To develop classification models, the dataset is split into two sets: one for training and one for testing. The training set is utilised to train classification models, whereas the test set is used to validate the trained models. Weka tool [52] is used to build 12 different ML classification models and evaluate the security of those models. The Jprofiler tool [53] is used to evaluate those models' performance criteria. The output of such evaluation is considered the input to the decision matrix in the next section. Thus, the proposed decision matrix is constructed on the basis

<!-- page 5 -->

of the intersection of IDS standardised evaluation criteria and ML classification models.

In this DM, the standardised security evaluation criteria are either benefit criteria (i.e., Acc, TPR, TNR, PPV, F1-score, MCC and AUC) or cost criteria (i.e., FPR, FNR, ICC, MAE, RMSE, RRSE and RAE). All standardised performance evaluation criteria are considered cost criteria (i.e., TraT, CPU time (CPUT) and MU). According to the proposed DM, three issues are created (i.e., multicriteria, criteria importance and conflict/trade-off), as explained in the Introduction section. For overcoming these issues, proposing a decision-making solution is vital to determine the importance of each criterion and benchmark the discussed ML-based IDS classification models for FL.

Direct rating is a common and simple subjective weight elicitation method. The weight of the criteria is obtained by asking the expert to rate each criterion by giving a score out of 100 according to how important he thinks it is [54].

## VII. CONCLUSION

In this study, the standardisation and MCDM problems exist in the evaluation and benchmarking of ML-based IDSs used in the FL architecture of IoMT applications, have been solved by developing a decision-making framework. The implications of the developed framework benefitted IDS developers in making the right decisions in selecting the best classification model. The study has certain limitations, which could be addressed in the future. Firstly, the IDS classifiers were operated and evaluated on the basis of the binary classification task only. The evaluated classifiers will be tested using a multiclass dataset to confirm the benchmarking results. Secondly, given that the IDS evaluation criteria were standardised using FDM, the triangular fuzzy numbers were used to convert the linguistic variables, which has many shortcomings related to uncertainty issues. Thus, FDM based on other powerful fuzzy sets is worthy. Thirdly, the classifiers were benchmarked using VIKOR ranking method. This method usually uses different normalisation approaches, and such utilisation definitely affects the benchmarking results. VIKOR also lacks weight generation provision. Thus, the fourth limitation has been raised, which is related to using external MCDM weighting methods. Specifically, although the inconsistency issues could not be produced whilst using the entropy method, this method does not depend on the subjective judgement of decision makers on weight assignment. Thus, it fails to reflect the opinions of

<!-- page 9 -->

highly qualified experts with extensive experience. To eliminate the third and fourth limitations, other MCDM methods including fuzzy weighted zero inconsistency method [58] and fuzzy decision by opinion score method [59] might be used in the future. Fifthly, the importance measurement, which reflects each DM's preferences on the IDS classifiers benchmarking process, is ignored. In the future, more investigation on MCDM methods that consider the experts' importance is needed. The performance criteria might be expanded and may include any newly proposed criteria, such as energy consumption [60], in the future work.
