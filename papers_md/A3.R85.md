## Retention: rhetoric or realistic agendas for the future of higher education

## Jennifer Rowley

School of Management and Social Sciences, Edge Hill College of Higher Education, Ormskirk, UK

## Keywords

Students, Customer retention, Relationship marketing, Higher education

## **Abstract **

Proposes the use of relationship management as a perspective on student retention management in higher education. "Good practice" that might aid in the management of student engagement with learning is hungrily shared, but there are some inherent contradictions amongst these recommendations. Previous work on withdrawal identifies some of the factors that influence the withdrawal decision; this approach aligns with transaction based marketing and study of consumer behaviour. This article proposes that the relationship marketing and management approach that focuses on customer loyalty and commitment may offer different insights. This model is used to describe the development of the relationship between the student and their college or university. An important facet of this relationship development is that it needs to be maintained by different "service agents" through different stages of the relationship, and therefore communication, and student record systems that share student knowledge across the university are crucial.

## Introduction

In an era in which knowledge is viewed as an important economic asset, governments have become more concerned about the development of intellectual capital in their populations. In the UK, this has, for example, led to government policy initiatives that suggest that by 2010, 50 per cent of those in the age group 18-30 will benefit from higher education. Students from social and family groups that have never before participated in higher education, and who in many cases did not achieve "well" at school, are being encouraged to engage in higher education. Progress towards government targets is slow, and is made all the slower by the increasing percentage of the student population who commence higher education courses and then drop out or fail their course before completion. Using the ever-effective stick of budgetary allocations based on student numbers on course, HECE has succeeded in focusing institutional attention on the twin agendas of recruitment and retention.

Within institutions the agenda for retention has been driven down by organisational hierarchies with the same blunt instrument, but typically leavened with initiatives, systems and training courses that are supposed to enhance retention. No one really knows which of these initiatives will work, or the extent to which they might impact on the problem, partly because there has been insufficient thinking about the nature of the problem. In addition, no institution wants to be at the top of this particular league table, and can not therefore possibly accept that the problem is intractable, or, perhaps less extreme, that the resources allocated to reduce the problem, far outweigh their impact, and value. This set of problems and their potential solutions have a number of inherent contradictions. For example, although tight coupling of learning and assessment might ensure continuing engagement, it tends to rule out the use of formative assessment, which could be very valuable to students in developing their study skills. In addition, many of the solutions shown in Table I involve more "staff input" into relationships with or support for students. This poses two problems. In a climate of declining unit of resource staff may be dealing with large numbers of students and face a considerable challenge in making the individual student feel special. From the student perspective, the more they need support, the less likely are they to be in a position to take advantage of it. For example, a dyslexic student often reads more slowly than other students, and their normal assignments take longer; fitting in additional study skills sessions may present a further burden. The student who is working, and finds himself unable to attend some sessions, is heavily penalised by assessment strategies that design in continuing engagement. Admittedly there is an underlying tension promoted by government policy that whilst it seeks participation from social groups to which higher education has previously been an alien experience, it does not address the issue of resourcing higher education institutions to work with these students, and neither does it support a student financing model with which these students feel secure. But, the problem goes beyond this, to the conceptualisation of student commitment to higher education.

Marketers have recognised that the heart of the marketing exchange is not a transaction, but a relationship. For businesses, like higher education institutions, a loyal customer base leads to enhanced success. A clear customer profile, and the benefits that customers seek offer the organisation a position in which it can more easily create value for customers. than would be the case with a floating customer base. Further, capturing new customers requires a much higher level of investment than retaining existing customers.

A concern with loyalty and commitment to organisations may be endemic. In a society in which commitment at a personal, or community level has become associated with staidness and lack of willingness to change and participate in innovation is viewed as a negative quality, loyalty and long-term commitment has been downgraded. In addition, students have grown up being bombarded by marketing messages to a far greater extent than for previous generations that encourage them to try a new experience, or to discard a product before the end of its lifetime because a new and improved model is now available. Are these the young people we expect to commit to three or more years of learning, without any clear view of the benefits to them as individuals at the end of that period of time, and beyond?

This article visits some of the models that have been proposed in the context of relationship marketing in consumer

marketplaces, in order to build a different perspective on retention in higher education. It suggests that thinking about the nature of the relationship that the student forms with the institution is paramount. The concept of loyalty is seen to have both attitudinal and behavioural dimensions. The concept of the relationship life cycle provides a useful framework for structuring analysis of the development of student relationships with their university or college.

## Withdrawal and retention

Student withdrawal from institutions of higher education has been a focus for research in the USA and Canada for many years (for example, Tinto, 1975; Lam, 1984; Myton et al., 1992; Fralick, 1993; Pyke and Sheridon, 1993; McKeown et al., 1993). However, UK universities have traditionally enjoyed relatively high undergraduate retention and completion rates. University entry was highly selective; there was an established tutorial framework and full student funding. All these factors contribute to high retention rates. However, recent political agendas, which have led to an emphasis on widening participation in higher education, increased student numbers and a more diverse student population, coupled with changes in arrangements relating to student funding, have put the issue of retention more firmly on the agenda. Poor retention rates may also have funding consequences for institutions.

Withdrawals from higher education incur significant institutional and personal cost. Each withdrawal poses the question "why?". Institutions are likely to look to their admissions procedures, course and institutional information, student care and counselling services and student finances, in an attempt to investigate the causes of noncompletion (Thomas *et al.*, 1996).

Tinto (1975) provided a model of withdrawal that has influenced much subsequent research. This model asserts that students who were integrated into the academic and social life of the institution were less likely to drop out. More recently, Rickinson and Rutherford (1995) examined the factors influencing withdrawals/ retention rates in the first term. Key factors for them were the degree to which students felt prepared, both academically and emotionally, for the transition to university and the availability of appropriate academic and personal support at the transition stage. In a subsequent survey of withdrawal, they identified "course difficulties" and "being away from home" as significant for withdrawal.

Yorke (1997) conducted a major survey of non-completion across a consortium of six higher education institutions. The most significant factors in non-completion were identified as:

- incompatibility between the student and their course or institution;
- lack of preparation for the higher education experience;
- lack of commitment to the course;
- · financial hardship; and
- · poor academic progress.

Yorke also identified specific groups for which these factors were more or less significant.

Identification of the significant factors in student withdrawal or retention is really concerned with understanding the influences in the decision-making process that motivate students to continue their studies or to withdraw. Indeed, some studies (Rickinson and Rutherford, 1996) have found that a significant proportion of students who withdraw intend to resume their studies at another time or place or in a different subject. Motivation to pursue higher education appears to be high even amongst students who withdraw. This approach parallels traditional research on consumer decision making that tends to privilege discrete decision-making episodes, such as those associated with purchase decisions. It is proposed that these approaches align with the transaction-based approach to consumer

behaviour and marketing. The alternative relationship marketing approach provides a more holistic perspective and set of strategies for working with students. This recognises the value inherent in extended relationships with customers, and views customer groups as business assets. A key theme in such a paradigm is customer loyalty and commitment.

## **Relationships and loyalty **

In commercial arenas, customer loyalty is perceived to be linked to profitability. With the marketisation of the public sector and the introduction of provider-purchaser relationships within the supply chain, and a consumer orientation in relation to the provision of service to the public, it is appropriate to consider the relevance of marketing concepts to this sector (Rowley and Dawes, 1999). Typically, in the public sector, consumers may have few alternatives with loyalty being imposed. Withdrawal from a course could be viewed as an extreme form of disloyal behaviour.

Loyalty is associated with the customer's willingness to continue in a relationship, which is often represented by switching behaviour, or the propensity to switch to another provider. Loyalty can be exhibited in different ways depending upon the nature of the product or service that the customer is being offered. For example, bank customers typically have an ongoing relationship whilst they hold an account, and switch when they change accounts. On the other hand, an owner of a Mercedes only really demonstrates their loyalty to the brand when they next make a purchase, although they may demonstrate their commitment in the interim between purchases by word-of-mouth recommendations to friends and colleagues. Loyalty is a complex construct that involves both behavioural and attitudinal aspects. For example, Dick and Basu (1994) propose four conditions in relation to loyalty which represent the intersection between relative attitude and repeat patronage. Customers who exhibit loyalty behaviour engage in repeat purchases, where this is appropriate. Customers who are loyal in attitude are likely to make recommendations to someone else, and, sometimes their loyal attitude will lead to loyal behaviour in the form of repeat purchases. Loyal students, then, might not only stay themselves, but also have a role in encouraging other students to stay. This means that strategies for relationship building need to extend to all students, and

Henry (2000), on the other hand, suggests that it is dangerous to be wooed by the attractiveness of the loyalty concept, and a mistake to conflate loyalty and retention. Customers may stay for a variety of reasons that have no connection with a positive affinity for an organisation or a brand:

- no good alternative:
- no perceived differences among the electives, and convenience dictates staying put;
- · risk avoidance:
- switching costs may be higher than the perceived value of going to an alternative:
- · unique products; and
- explicit loyalty programmes.

There is a long-standing belief that service quality and customer satisfaction can affect retention. For example, Dick and Basu (1994) identify satisfaction as one of the antecedents of a loyalty relationship. There is a considerable literature associated with the concept of service quality and customer satisfaction in higher education. It therefore seems reasonable to investigate the relationship between satisfaction and withdrawal in higher education, but it would be premature to assume that there is a direct and simple relationship. In the marketing literature there is considerable debate about the relationship between loyalty and satisfaction. Increasing satisfaction does not necessarily produce an increase in loyalty for all customers, and satisfied customers still switch. Strauss and Neuhaus (1997) suggest that the intensity of satisfaction is an element in customer loyalty. Storbacka et al. (1994) differentiate between the effect of satisfaction with a particular service episode, and the effect of satisfaction with the relationship as a whole on lovalty attitudes and behaviours. Further work on the relationship between satisfaction and loyalty in a wider range of service contexts, and including specifically those in the public sector, is necessary to enhance our knowledge of the relationship between service quality, satisfaction, and customer responses as characterised by loyalty.

## Relationship life cycles

The concept of the relationship life cycle encourages consideration on the phases in a relationship between a customer and an organisation. Hunt (1994) suggests that there are five stages in the process of developing a relationship life cycle. The labels for the stages in a more recent model are shown in

brackets. These stages can be used to structure analysis of the relationship between students and universities over the life of an episode of engagement:

- 1 Choosing a partner (introduction) is the first stage during which a customer makes a careful choice of the communities and organisations with which they wish to engage. For organisations this phase could consist of establishing awareness of the existence of an organisation through promotion, meetings with various stakeholders, special events, and special offers. Customers need to be persuaded that they may want to renew this initial contact. This stage might be mapped onto the initial acquaintance and building of awareness, that occurs during the admission process for students. Students gather information through marketing communication and open days, and seek to assess whether what the university or college has to offer matches their requirements, both at a cognitive and an emotional level. This part of the relationship life cycle tends to be dominated by the marketing department, although academic staff have traditionally had some involvement and interaction with potential students during these processes, and are often keen to start to establish relationships. Structuring the relationship (experimentation) is the period during which the stakeholder and the organisation become better acquainted. Personal contacts, preferably at a variety of levels, and between a number of individuals, are important during this stage of the process; understanding, knowledge and trust need to develop. Customers need to be satisfied with the service quality, and should be encouraged to extend their range of involvement with the organisation, so that the relationship becomes more multi-dimensional. For students this period occurs early in their time in higher education. Induction and first week experiences are pivotal, not just because they come at a time when the student has a lot to learn about the nature of the relationship, but also because this is a time of great change in their lives. The responsibility for representing the relationship tends to shift to tutors, other support staff in departments, and central support staff during this period. In other words, there is a key shift in the source of the messages, and the management of the relationship within the institution at the early stage of structuring the relationship.

- Devoting time to developing the relationship (identification) may involve diligent enquiries about service quality, customer satisfaction, seeking opportunities for creating value, building trust and ensuring commitment. Both student and various "service agents" on behalf of the institution need to spend time during this phase getting acquainted. and understanding each other's expectations. This could be described as the courtship phase during which the student gradually becomes a member of the academic community. This membership may have multiple meanings, but to some extent it will involve the student recognising the values and expectations of higher education, and responding to these. This may mean embracing values and expectations, or it may mean challenging them. Withdrawal situations are likely to arise when the student either can not accommodate the values and expectations and the relationship becomes unacceptably conflictual, or when the student does not have time to work through the commitment process, due to other circumstances in their life. The university can assist in this process by making it easier for the student to "structure the relationship", by being clear and consistent in defining and communicating values, expectations, and standards.
- Maintaining lines of communication (continuous renewal) is the period during which the dialogues are well established. Customers and organisations know what to expect of each other, and they each understand their own role in the relationship. For the relationship to be continued, it must accommodate change in any of the parties. Both organisations and customers may be subject to changing environments, and competencies, which may affect what they require from the relationship. Parties have mutual investment and loyalty, and will strive to manage instabilities. Disruption in the relationship is painful. For students, this phase of the relationship probably corresponds with the second and third years of their study, when they have settled into the community, and understand what study is about. They have relationships with academic and some support staff, and, most importantly, other students. They are committed and generally know how to manage through the difficulties. Withdrawal is less likely to occur, because the student has by now invested a considerable amount in the

- relationship, and wants to manage that relationship in order to succeed and complete their studies. However, once again circumstances in the student's personal life may conspire to make it difficult for the student to continue the relationship. As this stage a mature discussion between the student and representative of the institution, such as might occur in a progress review, can assist the student and strengthen the relationship.
- Parting on good terms (dissolution) must be managed so that trustworthiness is not diminished. Happy memories may inspire valuable word-of-mouth recommendations to others, and leave the door open for the development of other relationships in due course. Activities, which offer opportunities for continued contact at a reduced level, or support the development of an alternative relationship in which one or both parties fulfil different roles, are helpful during the parting stage. Most students leave university on a high; they will have achieved their degree, and probably enjoyed the graduation ceremony which is important in marking the termination of a particular phase of the relationship between the institution and the student; the very term "graduation" signifies maturity. At this stage it is important that the student leaves with good memories, so that the parting is on terms that would allow the student to re-initiate the relationship with the university, possibly as a part time student, or as a work placement supervisor for future generations of students. For those students for whom the outcome is less positive, it is important to conduct appeal, and, if necessary, reassessment processes so that the student comes to see these in a positive light, and does not sever the relationship with the university with negative feelings.

As discussed above, withdrawal can take place at any stage in the relationship. Once the relationship has broken down, the likelihood of de-registration and formal withdrawal is high. Universities and colleges need to manage each of the stages in the relationship life cycle, even though the players or "service agents" at each stage may be different. A whole college and coherent strategy of retention is necessary. In addition, retention strategies need to embrace all stages in the life cycle. Too many withdrawal strategies focus on the first two stages, and take the later stages for granted. The institution needs to nurture its students.

In commercial customer terms, the core objective becomes that of optimisation of the use of products and service capabilities that the customer has currently in order to increase their well-being and boost profitability. The relationship life cycle encourages a shift in emphasis from acquisition to retention.

## Conclusion

A relationship management approach to student retention in higher education lifts the focus from key decision points to longerterm relationships. Although customer service is likely to be an antecedent to satisfactory long-term relationships between students and their university, the relationship between loyalty and satisfaction is not straightforward. Using the relationship life cycle it is possible to explore the way in which a relationship is developed with the student over the life of their engagement with a college or university. Significantly, this relationship needs to be sustained through different "service agents" at different stages in the life cycle. This might suggest that mechanisms need to be in place to inform the translation of relationship building at different stages in the life cycle. Both effective communication between different parts of the university, and detailed student record systems are likely to feature in the infrastructure that will support the proactive development of relationships, as a substitute for reactive crisis management when retention figures do not reach their target.
