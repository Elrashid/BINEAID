## the marketing review

## An exploratory enquiry into the internationalisation of higher education in the United Arab Emirates

**Nnamdi O. Madichie,** University of Sharjah, UAE* **Jerry Kolo,** American University Sharjah, UAE

In its bid to be at the forefront of higher education in the Arabian Gulf region, the United Arab Emirates (UAE) has embarked upon major internationalisation initiatives. This paper documents the extent of these efforts in the light of observed challenges. Our paper provides some practical/social implications for foreign universities - past, present and future, contemplating 'breaking in' or 'out' as the case may be.

**Keywords** Branch campuses, Franchise universities, Higher education, United Arab Emirates (UAE)

## Introduction

According to the Commission for Academic Accreditation (CAA) of the United Arab Emirates (UAE), there are 69 licensed higher education institutions (HEIs) in the UAE, with 552 accredited programs of study. These figures make the UAE an acclaimed higher education (HE) hub in the Arabian Gulf and by extension the Arab world. The influx of higher education by both 'home-grown' and 'foreign universities' into the UAE is a direct consequence of its liberalisation policy for market entrants. Notable amongst these is the establishment of Free Zones in the emirates of Dubai and Ras Al Khaimah (RAK) in particular. Free Zones provide the most liberal or laissez-faire environment for foreign businesses to operate, and have enabled the internationalisation of HEIs in the UAE.

A key observation made in this paper is that HEIs established within these Free Zones do not require any license from the government to locate and operate within these designated zones found in Dubai and RAK (see

## **A background to UAE higher education landscape**

The proliferation of foreign universities in the Gulf region began at the end of the 20th Century, due primarily to aggressive government policies on education, the massive oil wealth in the region, and the educational demand fuelled by both native and immigrant populations in the region. Westernbased universities and those from the home countries of some of the largest immigrant groups, such as India, 'trooped' to the region with the intent of capitalising on the opportunities presented in the region.

In the UAE, the influx of foreign universities began in the early 1990s (and is still ongoing as we write this paper) with the American University in Dubai and the Australian-based University of Wollongong in Dubai (UoWD), see Whelan and Kratochvil (2011). The emirate of Sharjah soon followed in 1997 with the establishment of two universities, namely, the franchised American University of Sharjah and the home-grown University of Sharjah. Other noted institutions that have ventured into the UAE include Dubai Aerospace Enterprise University in 2005, George Mason University in RAK in 2007, Michigan State University in Dubai in 2008, India-based Manipal University, Dubai University of Waterloo, Ontario, Canada in 2010, Paris-based Sorbonne University in Abu Dhabi, Michigan State University Dubai, New York-based New York University in Abu Dhabi in 2010, University of Bolton in RAK in 2010, and Middlesex University, Dubai. In addition to a very recent entry by Amity University, one of India's largest private universities, there are numerous other lesser known institutions all over the country, especially in Dubai's Knowledge Village and the Dubai International Academic City.

Examining the 'gold rush' of foreign universities into the UAE, it is quite intriguing that, just a quarter of a century after the rush began, reflections and assessments of the status of these universities leave a mixture of results, most of which lean toward an uncertain future for the institutions. Yet, foreign universities do not seem deterred by this uncertainty, as the case of India's largest private university, Amity University (referenced earlier) shows. The uncertainty was accentuated in an extensive study of western universities in the Gulf by John Willoughby, an economist at the American University in Washington (franchisor of the American University of Sharjah in the UAE). In an interview with the news reporter Calderwood (2011), Willoughby noted that "*there are important institutional, political and social * *challenges facing the new higher education institutions of the GCC*" (GCC = Gulf Cooperation Countries encompassing six members, Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates) that make it a risky

## Methodology

This paper is a theoretical enquiry into to the competitiveness of franchised institutions vis-à-vis branch campuses. We relied on personal observations drawing upon our over 10-year sojourn (collectively) in the HE environment within the UAE. In addition to reliance on extensive reviews of secondary materials (especially the media) on the subject, we also relied on informal 'conversations' on the subject of foreign universities (growth, closure and licensure matters) with multiple stakeholders. These methods of inquiry were recessitated by the well known reluctance of public and corporate officials in the Gulf to divulge information, due to a variety of reasons, as well as the sheer lack of systematic and coherent data on higher education in the UAE. From our personal observations, we postulate five key areas of research enquiry: first, most of the universities come into the UAE as branch locations of their parent institutions - direct 'Greenfield' investments. Second, most institutions come to the region on short notice, almost unannounced and/ for with little or no fanfare. Third, most institutions try to take advantage of the agglomeration of existing institutions in a few choice urban locations, resulting in the 'overconcentration' of institutions in Dubai and Abu Dhabi, UAE's two largest megalopoli. In our fourth observation, there seems to be an ambitious, yet factually unsubstantiated, projection of enrolment by each institution, veiled under the belief or hope that theirs would buck the trend of under-enrolment. This trend, which HEIs faced during the years of economic boom in the UAE and the world over, has changed quite noticeably since late 2009, when the effects of the global economic recession set in and unemployed people started to return to school to pursue higher education. Due to this new trend, increased enrolment figures across the board for HEIs in the UAE were reported for the first time in 2011 (see Moussly, 2011). Fifth, most HEIs often embark on some media campaign, upon their arrival in the region, stressing that they are here to fill a niche in the marketplace, and that their products would be unique or competitive.

## **Literature review**

Research has indicated that efforts directed at internationalisation at program level can be observed by the contribution of one or more of four factors (see Kreber, 2009, p. 7):

- 1. The recruitment of international students;
- 2. The teaching process, through selection of particular course content and forms of delivery (including ICT), student mobility, language of instruction;
- 3. Resources in the form of internationally recruited staff members, use of international course materials (e.g., literature); and
- 4. Location offering courses or setting up campuses abroad.

Taking an even broader lens on internationalisation, Knight (1993, p. 21) argued that internationalisation of higher education refers to "*the process * *of integrating an international/intercultural dimension into the teaching, * *research and service functions of the institution*". Echoing the notion of integration inherent in Knight's definition, Qiang (2003, p. 258) concluded that "*internationalisation must be *entrenched* in the culture, policy, planning * *and organisational process of the institution so that it can be both successful * *and sustainable*". Such debates over sustainability can be extended to the mode of entry as it concerns resource commitment and risk levels of key players - i.e., with a comprehensive presence in the UAE (see for example, Gulf News, 2008; Husain, 2002; Zaman, 2011).

## *Franchising versus direct investment*

Franchising (with roots in the word *francorum rex* or freedom from servitude) is a form of licensing of intellectual property rights (Adams & Mendelsohn, 1986; IFA, 2011). Franchising is also seen as a form of marketing and distribution in which the franchisor (brand owner) grants an individual or organisation, the franchise, the right to do business in a prescribed manner over a certain period of time and in a specified area or place (Ayling, 1987). The franchisor licenses the franchisees to carry out business under the name owned by or associated with the franchisor; control by the franchisor over the way in which the franchisee carries on the business; provision of assistance to the franchisee by the franchisor in running the business; and the franchisee

<!-- page 5 -->

provides as well as risks capital in the venture (Adams & Mendelsohn 1986). The relationship is a contractual one in accordance with a business format established by the franchisor. Factors contributing to the success of franchise arrangements include the quality of the business format system; the brand name associated with it; and the level of economic activity and business environment in the country where the proposed franchise is to operate.

Moreover, a recent review of a global marketing textbook seems to suggest that exports and franchising offer a risk-free alternative to the more appealing (due to the high degree of control associated with it) direct investment (see Lee & Carter, 2009; Madichie, 2010). Extending this to the context of higher education, the idea of foreign universities entering an international market by whatever name or means - whether home grown or established from the ground up by a local private entrepreneur or by acovernment (see typologies in Table 1) indicates two distinct modes of entry. One is the establishment of branch campuses, and the other is franchising, both of which are compared in Table 2, using distinct criteria compiled specifically for our purposes in this study.

Kogut and Singh (1988) in their widely cited article pointed out that joint ventures and acquisitions were substitute modes of entry. They also argued that "firms from marketing-intensive industries might engage in joint venture if they possessed the brand, but lacked other resources along the valueadded chain" (see Kogut & Singh, 1988, p. 421). This can be clearly seen in the invitation by Abu Dhabi extended to well-established university brands such as NYU, Paris Sorbonne and INSEAD. Nonetheless there are, and have been, debates around the risk versus rewards/returns in entry mode choice, which leads to the challenges we highlight herein.

## Internationalisation challenges facing universities in the UAE

The internationalisation challenges facing universities (especially foreign universities) in the UAE are discussed from two broad perspectives - theoretical; and practical. While the latter perspective would be useful for policy implications, the former perspective is intended to stimulate intellectual discourse in this area.

## Theoretical perspective

There have been numerous contentions over whether the selection of entry modes should be based on trade-offs between risks and returns where entrants tend to opt for highest risk adjusted returns (Chen & Messner, 2011). In addition to return and risk, behavioural evidence reveals that

<!-- page 7 -->

this selection is also determined by resource availability and control needs (Cespedes, 1988). Flexibility, another key variable, often plays an important role in determining market entry mode - thus prompting the need for potential entrants to consider five strategic effects (risk exposure, return, control, flexibility, and resource commitment) in a unified framework when they select an entry mode (Chen & Messner, 2011). While a brief description of these effects is outlined here, the reader is encouraged to peruse Table 3 for clearer presentation of these.

- 1. Risk takes two forms (investment risk and contractual risk). In the former situation, the uncertainty over the continuation of present economic and political conditions and government policies which are critical to the survival and profitability of a firm's operations in that country. These include the freedom to repatriate profits, control of foreign assets, and the risk of a breakdown in investment policies of the government. Contractual risk reflects the uncertainty and cost of making and enforcing contracts in a foreign country.

2. Return is usually the bridging point between risk and control. Arguably the more of the latter two the higher the returns. Kogut and Singh (1988, p. 421) once argued that "firms from marketing-intensive industries might engage in a joint venture if they possessed the brand, but lacked other resources along the value-added chain".

3. Control can be desirable in improving a firm's competitive position and maximising the returns on its assets and skills. The level of control is lowest in the case of licensing and highest in the case of a wholly owned subsidiary (Hill, Hwang & Kim, 1990), i.e., branch campuses.

4. Flexibility, is seen as a firm's abilities to reposition itself in a market, change its game plans or dismantle its current strategies when the market is no longer as attractive as it once was (Harrigan, 1981).

5. Resource commitment refers to the financial and managerial capacity of a firm for serving a particular foreign market (Agarwal & Ramaswami, 1992). It increases the firm's risk exposure such as the possibility of losses due to currency fluctuations.

## A practical perspective

From our documentary analysis and observations we note that both franchised and branch universities in the UAE face a number of challenges - some similar, and others expectedly specific to their entry mode. Although the differences in the severity of the challenges may be a matter of minor degrees, the common denominators for both types of universities are survivability and profitability, which both translate into success or failure. These denominators, also goals of the universities, are measured in terms of three key factors - student enrolment or full time equivalent (FTE); the quantity of financial resources/resource commitment; and the quality of faculty and staff. While these three are interrelated and complementary, they also seem to be mutually exclusive events in terms of their medium to long term implications for the success of universities in the region.

In the UAE in particular, the aforementioned factors have influenced the *survivability* of all foreign universities differently, albeit the impacts have been more weighty, visible and 'damaging' on the branch campuses than the franchised institutions. Taking enrolment figures (see Table 4), for example, the most widely reported 'obstacle' for most foreign universities that rushed into the UAE is their inability to meet enrolment forecasts, hence, their early or premature closure. Of several highly publicised cases, some examples are the University of Southern Queensland, which closed its campus in 2005, one year after opening its doors at the Dubai Knowledge Village (an education Free Zone); George Mason University, which opened its RAK campus in 2006, but closed a year later; and Dubai Aerospace Enterprise University, which shut its doors in 2010. Many of these and other foreign universities enrolled less than one-fifth of their projected figures.

However, it is worth pointing out recent enrolment figures for the Fall semester of the 2011/2012 academic year, have been led by private universities and even more so by branch campuses of these universities accounting for between 30% and 40% increases (see Table 5) for Heriot Watt, Middlesex and Manipal universities respectively.

For analytical purposes, we categorised these causes into three, namely, social demographic, economic and institutional.

## Social and demographic challenges

Challenges in this category include the fact that the sheer number of eligible students seeking admission into universities is much lower than the cumulative number of spaces available in the plethora of universities in the country. The UAE has a very small total population, which the latest official figure put at 8.26 million. Of this total, only about 947,997, or 11.47%, are citizens, or locals (UAE Interact, 2011). Of the total population, equally small is the percentage that is eligible for university entry. Another social demographic cause is the general preference by male citizens to navigate towards entrepreneurship rather than higher education, a phenomenon

<!-- page 12 -->

which remains a major concern for governments at the national and regional levels (Ahmed, 2010). A final reason is that many of the citizens who desire to pursue higher education have more incentives to study abroad than at home. In the case of branch campuses, therefore, students would rather attend the parent universities abroad than the branch campuses in the UAE. For the franchised institutions, however, the costs and admission requirements are similar or more stringent than their overseas counterparts. Thus, the opportunity costs of the latter outweigh those of the former, and Emiratis (i.e., UAE nationals) often opt for overseas studies as a result. However, a local survey in RAK (Nazzal, 2006), reported that UAE national students are no longer interested in studying at Western universities. The survey conducted by the Sheikh Saqr Government Excellence Programme in June 2006 revealed that 69% of the students of the Secondary School Certificate were not willing to carry on their university education in Western universities - further highlighting a lack of enthusiasm towards Western universities. ## *Affordability challenges*

One of the two main economic causes we discuss here is *household income * *status*. We observed that, of the large resident population, known as the expatriate population, in the UAE, many households are in the lower and middle income groups and, for them, the cost of education in foreign universities is very prohibitive. Without any assurances for scholarships and financial assistance, youngsters from these households would attend universities in their homelands, or use their family support to attend universities overseas, where they believe they can get more value for their

<!-- page 13 -->

money. Another economic cause is the relatively 'young' culture of strategic and aggressive fundraising by educational institutions in the UAE. While there are cases of generous donations to educational and civic causes by individual and corporate entities, those cases are few and far between. Increasingly, universities are establishing institutional development and endowment offices or divisions, in order to make fund-raising an integral part of university operations. This should bode well for universities, especially as the prevailing state of the economy compels universities to be more creative and innovative in ensuring their solvency - including match-funding projects such as the National Research Fund.

## Other institutional challenges

Another set of challenges is prompted by institutional lapses that are internal to each university, but which affect the institution's ability to thrive and be competitive. Perhaps most critical is the reputation of an institution, which is a culmination of several factors, such as the quality of education or curricula offered; the diversity or robustness of degree program offerings; the quality of faculty, facilities and amenities; the prospects for financial support; the record of the employability of graduates of the institution; and, how accessible the institution is geographically.

For all the factors listed, our observation is that the franchised institutions in the UAE tend to be more reputable locally than branch campuses, even where the latter are affiliated with reputable universities in their home countries. In a recent poll of the HE sector in terms of new programme launches, Amity University in DIAC (Dubai International Academic City) (which recently replaced the now defunct Michigan State University) was ranked first. Amity offers courses in the fields of nanotechnology, aerospace engineering, nuclear science and technology, solar and alternative energy as well as forensic science (none of which have been accredited by the Ministry of HE and Scientific Research, MOHESR, as yet). Meanwhile Manipal University is offering a doctorate programme in biotechnology (including human genetics, stem cells, neurobiology and cancer molecular biology). UoWD also recently introduced two accredited Masters' degree programmes in international studies as well as media & communications.

## **Conclusion and implications**

The challenges of foreign universities in the UAE bring with them some critical points of reflection especially as to what constitutes the most costeffective way of succeeding in an over-supplied market such as the UAE. Our observations and analyses point to franchising as the best mode of entry into this kind of market. From our observations two modes of market entry dominate; franchising (e.g., American University of Sharjah) and direct investments (e.g., UoWD). While we sought to highlight the most optimal given the risk and uncertainty of the market, we note the multiple advantages of the franchising option over direct investment (i.e., branch campus model). The appeal of franchising as a preferred option stems primarily from the diverse forms which it can take - from the adaptation of a tested educational product or service by the franchisee; long-term demonstration of financial solvency via the pro forma submitted for the approval of a franchise; the establishment of a multi-layer oversight or governing structure; access to a global pool of expertise for the institution's governance, administration and instructions (faculty); ready claim of, or identification with, an established 'brand' name and reputation; quality control of programs and operations due to the requirement for accreditation, certification or approval by international and national certification entities; robust support of proud and loyal alumni; and widespread support of corporations which want to demonstrate and promote their corporate social responsibility/sustainability programs.

In most parts of the world, and especially in the UAE, education below, and to some extent at the university level, has historically been a public good, which is provided to all persons regardless of their ability to afford it. In the last quarter century, however, globalisation, privatisation and public-private partnerships have led to the 'commodification' of education, resulting in the provision of education by entities which are totally independent of government. As Kreber (2009, p. 13) argued:

*Universities and colleges are increasingly called upon to internationalise. A * *comprehensive conception of internationalisation can help us resist undue * *emphasis on economic imperatives on the one hand, and purely cosmetic * *efforts at internationalising curriculum on the other, both at the expense * *of considerations of the common good.*

<!-- page 15 -->

By this statement an inference on sustainability cannot be ignored. Indeed Kreber clearly stated that internationalisation efforts were explored through the lens of global sustainability and examines the role of universities in educating for sustainable development through their research, their teaching (and pedagogies), and by acting as models in their own physical operations. Rather than suggesting that sustainability should be promoted through specialised courses or programs, she advocates an integrated approach where sustainability is considered across the disciplines. She concluded that "in order for higher education to truly address sustainability problems and educate the citizenry to move toward sustainability, a fundamental rethinking of the purpose of the university and how we teach is needed".

Various arguments or cases can be made for and against the provision of university education by private enterprise. However, what this paper set out to achieve is to determine the least risky mode or path for foreign universities to enter the UAE education market, and to decipher some lessons for universities considering locating in the area in the future. The inquiries conducted for this paper show that the least risky and most profitable path is franchising as illustrated in Table 2. The practical implications for universities eveing the UAE market are outlined in the following practical guidelines, which we consider critical from our analysis.

- 1. Align with, or select an already established and tested educational institution, product or service that has a franchise option, rather than the branch campus option.
- 2. Prepare best- and worst-case, as well as short- and long-term financial solvency scenarios, in addition to "exit" strategies, should the best-case scenarios not materialise.
- 3. Establish a multi-layered, yet integrated or synchronised oversight or governing structure.
- 4. Always source for academic and administrative personnel from the global job market.
- 5. Develop a robust, powerful and practical institutional identity or brand, especially for recruitment, publicity and fund-raising purposes.
- 6. Develop the best quality products, and seek national and international accreditations and/or certification for all programs. Insist on standards and best practices, to avoid the cheap reputation of being a certificate mill.
- 7. Establish an institutional development and/or endowment office from the start.
- 8. Develop a strategy to track and engage alumni, especially citizens, as well as local and international corporations that are interested in the university, for one reason or another.
- 9. Collaborate with local and international organisations, both public and private, to plan educational, fundraising and publicity events on an on-going basis.
- 10. Always engage the local media to help tell your story.
