## ABSTRACT

The present research focusses on estimating the elastic constants of orthotropic laminates using ultrasonic guided waves (GWs) excited through Lead Zirconate Titanate (PZT) sensors and sensed using one-dimensional laser vibrometer. The elastic constants of a material are crucial for understanding its mechanical behaviour and are typically determined through experimental testing. However, this process can be time-consuming and expensive. We formulate this problem as an inverse problem of property estimation. Thus, in this work, the simulation models with PZT transducers have been employed for generating time series (TS) GWs for the orthotropic material. Then, an inverse machine learning model is trained using a TS dataset pertaining to different elastic constants generated using the simulations. The inverse model consists of deep neural networks and designing a loss function for the specific application. Limited number of unique sets of simulations were conducted. Out of available simulation data, 30% of the sets were used for validation. To further test the model, a blind experimental test was conducted, and the corresponding elastic constants were estimated with a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 12.89% and standard deviation of 5.47%. The results demonstrate that formulation of property estimation as inverse problem is capable of accurately predicting the elastic constants of a material, by using a model solely trained on simulation and a very scare amount of data. This approach has the potential to significantly reduce the computational time for predicting the elastic constants, and thereby could have wideranging applications in materials science and engineering.

## **2.** **Inverse problem formulation for property estimation **

Inverse problems refer to problems of inferring the unknown parameters or causes of a system from the observed data or effects [[18]](#page-14-0). Inverse problems arise in many fields of science and engineering, such as geophysics, medical imaging [[19]](#page-14-0), materials science, microwave application [[20]](#page-14-0), fluid mechanics [[21]](#page-14-0) and astronomy. One of the main challenges of inverse problems is that they are often ill-posed, meaning that they do not have a unique, stable, or well-defined solution. Therefore, various methods have been developed to regularize, discretize, and solve inverse problems in a robust and efficient way [[22]](#page-14-0).

[Fig. 1 ](#page-1-0)shows a generic view of the inverse problem formulation for estimating properties. In the physical world, we make different observations whenever the underlying properties change. In the forward model, we are aware of the properties, and we try to predict the observations. Whereas in the inverse model we are using observations to know about the causal properties. To use the inverse model, we need to

## **3.** **Data acquisition methodology **

In the present work, a methodology is proposed to estimate the elastic constants of orthotropic laminates using the guided waves actuated and sensed from only one-side of the sample. Firstly, the effective stiffness matrix is estimated for the sample. Then, several combinations across the effective stiffness matrix constants have been obtained randomly and fed to the simulation model to obtain the time-domain responses. These responses are then used to train the machine learning model as outlined in [Section 4.1 ](#page-11-0)to predict the stiffness matrix of the experimental dataset in[ Section 5](#page-12-0). ## *3.1.* *Material and specimen description *

The specimen is a CFRP composite with [0*,* ±45*,* 90] ^4^*s*as stacking sequence. The single lamina material here is IM7/8552. The lamina thereby has 32 plies with each ply thickness of 0.125 mm making the overall lamina thickness 4 mm. The three-dimensional (3D) dimensions of the specimen are 200 × 200 × 4 mm3. The dimensions are carefully selected to facilitate 3D simulations in terms of less computational time and savings in resources. The material properties of the 0-degree lamina are shown in[ Table 2 ](#page-1-0)in the row labelled as 00 whereas the row labelled as 'E*' represents the effective material properties of the laminate. The material properties of 00 lamina were obtained from the manufacturer and the effective material properties labelled as 'E*' were obtained using the homogenization method based on the Macromechanical Analysis of Laminates theory [[29]](#page-14-0). [Fig. 3 ](#page-2-0)shows the position of the actuator and receivers on the specimen which was used for generating TS data in this paper. Each point on the grid is separated by a distance of 30 mm in horizontal and vertical wave propagation direction.

## *3.2.* *Dispersion curve and mode propagation analysis of GW *

In order to select a suitable excitation frequency for conducting experiments and simulations, it is important to conduct a detailed analysis of the dispersion curves, mode propagation, and the displacement profiles. The dispersion curves shown in [Fig. 4](#page-2-0) are obtained using the effective stiffness matrix shown in [Table.1](#page-1-0). Based on the dispersion curve analysis in [Fig. 4,](#page-2-0) *A*0 and *S*0 modes are chosen for the inverse characterization of the CFRP orthotropic sample. Further, the separation distance between the reception points has been chosen according to the mode propagation analysis as shown in[ Fig. 5.](#page-3-0) This ensures that there are least chances of overlapping modes between the different wave modes propagating in the specimen at 100 kHz. Accordingly, authors selected the separation distance of 30 mm. Further, the displacement analysis of the *A*0 and *S*0 GW modes in[ Fig. 6 ](#page-3-0)show that for *A*0 mode the out of plane response shall be more dominant and for *S*0 mode, the in-plane shall be more dominant. However, as a 1D laser is used for data acquisition, it is therefore expected that the *A*0 mode shall have dominant displacement compared to the *S*0 mode.

The objective of the inverse model is to estimate the elastic stiffness matrix for a given material structure. For this we use ML model that takes time-series signal data received at various receiving sensors and excitation signal is used to train the ML pipeline. We built the ML pipeline for data analysis consisting of data pre-processing, feature engineering, model building and performance evaluation. ## 4.1. The training phase

The model will be trained using the simulation data recorded at different sensing points on the specimen surface that shall also account for the variation in the wave velocities along different wave propagation directions. The "trained model" will be used in the testing phase for predicting the elastic constants. In the training phase, simulation training data will be further subdivided into training and validation data. Each data point is pre-processed using the data pre-processing pipeline as shown in Fig. 11. The used model structure is shown in Fig. 12. In this figure, blue colour signifies processing steps, red colour is linked to output variables, and green colour to input variables.

For the model structure, we are using combination of 1D-CNN followed by dense linear layers for identification of elastic constants. One of the reasons for using 1D-CNN is that it trains faster as compared to other models such as recurrent neural networks. Set of convolution layers are used for identifying sparse connections and parameter sharing. In dense linear layer, every neuron interacts with every other neuron [28]. Time-series signal is a long temporal sequence and 1D-CNN can extract inherent features from such signals. The output of one convolution layer in 1D-CNN is shown in following equation [27,32].

$$
\begin{aligned} x_k^l &= b_k^l + \sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N_{l-1}} conv1d \left(w_{ik}^{l-1}, s_i^{l-1}\right) \\ y_k^l &= f(x_k^l) \end{aligned} [1]
$$

where x_k^l is the input, b_i^l is the bias of the k^{th} neuron at layer l, and s_i^{l-1} is the output of the i^{th} neuron, w_{ik}^{l-1} is the kernel weight connection between i^{th} neuron at layer l-1 to k^{th} neuron at layer l. The function f() is an activation function which can be ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, LeakyRelu etc. 1D-CNN[27] and Dense linear layer[33] were implemented using Pytorch [34].

The process of training a neural network involves several steps that utilize both training and validation data. Initially, the network is provided with training data, which consists of input samples and their desired outputs. The network's weights and biases are randomly assigned, and the training begins. During training, the network computes its output based on the given inputs, and the difference between the predicted output and the desired output is measured using a loss function. The loss function was designed in a novel way. Algorithm for calculating loss function is explained in Algorithm 1.

*Algorithm 1. Algorithm for calculating the employed loss function*

Algorithm: Calculating Loss function
Input: C^T is array of true elastic constants; C^P is array of predicted elastic constants
1. Initialize L=0

- 2. for i \in elastic constants:
- 3. \ E_i = MeanSquareError(C_i^T, C_i^P) \\
- 4. L = L + E_i
- 5. end for
- 6. return L

The backpropagation algorithm is then employed to calculate the gradients of the loss function with respect to the network's parameters. This iterative process continues for various epochs, with the network gradually learning the underlying patterns in the data. To assess the network's generalization capability and prevent overfitting, validation data is utilized. After each epoch, the network's performance on the validation data is evaluated by calculating the validation loss. The validation data serves as a guide, monitoring the network's progress and detecting signs of overfitting, where the network becomes too specialized to the training data. The training process continues until the desired level of performance is achieved or a specified number of epochs is completed. ## **6.** **Conclusions and future work suggestions **

In this work the authors proposed a new ML-based methodology for inverse elastic constants estimation of the orthotropic composites. The ultrasonic guided wave responses were obtained for different elastic constants combinations using 3D simulation. The obtained corresponding time-domain responses were used as an input to the model and the nine independent elastic constants were the output. Out of available unique simulation data, 30 % data was used for validation. The results obtained showed good accuracy. Further, a blind experimental test was also conducted, and the elastic constants predicted using the ML model were very close to the true values. This shows that the proposed framework has significant capability in predicting the elastic constants in less time compared to the traditional methodologies. Some of the challenges faced during the model development are as follows. First, a total of only 135 simulation datasets were generated. Approximately, 70 % (94 data points) were used for training the model, which is quite

low and practical. The remaining 30 % (41 data points) were used as validation datasets. 2. Second, all of the 9 elastic constants need to be varied for generation of ultrasonic TS signals. With that said, for the limited number of datasets, this is a challenge for the model to be trained well for prediction. Third, the spacing of the sensing points in the experiments was very scarce. This is intentionally kept so as to have a challenging case for the model training and prediction. Firth, data is recorded from only one side of the sample. This is close to most of the practical scenarios where there is limited or no access for data collection. To address the above challenges, following are the novelties in the modelling approach. Firstly, we developed a customed loss function in which we aggregated losses for individual elastic constants. Next, based on data exploration and devising relationships among various elastic constants from the simulated datasets, we reduced AI model computational complexity. This allowed us to work with scarce number of data points. Lastly, we employed extensive hyperparameter optimization and training weight optimization by using optimal learning rate.

In future, different layups and number of plies of composite samples could be considered and tested against the proposed methodology. Further, the actuation and sensing strategy could be changed such as using the laser excitation and sensing for the data acquisition to be fully non-contact.
