## ABSTRACT:

This research explores the international branch campus (IBC) model in Dubai, United Arab Emirates from the perspective of five United Kingdom (UK) Higher Education Institutions (HEIs). A total of ten interviews were conducted with similar senior level leadership roles at both the UK HEIs and their respective branch campuses. A further interview was conducted with a senior Government of Dubai representative providing context on the higher education system in the host country. Two timeframes were the focus area of the research: the strategic planning phase of the project at the home campus and the subsequent implementation phase at the branch. The business model, Uppsala Internationalisation Model (UIM) for business development is proposed as a practical tool that has the potential to help institutions navigate the challenges of setting up branch campuses in regions where the political, cultural, and economic conditions differ to those at the home country. The findings of this research suggests that experiential learning resulting from operations in Dubai and the establishment of network relationships developed in the host country impact assumptions made during the strategic planning phase. The extend to which HEIs are willing to adapt and modify strategic plans for the IBC, are key to branch campus success and long-term sustainability.

CHAPTER ONE: INTRODUCTION

1.1 Introduction:

The six Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) states – Saudi Arabia, Qatar, UAE, Kuwait, Bahrain, and

Oman have a distinctive identity among the Middle East states. The ruling monarchy in each of

these countries hold hierarchical powers entrenched in tribal loyalty, tradition, and Islamic values.

The UAE is differentiated from its GCC neighboring countries in that it is made up of seven

federations (Emirates) each with its own ruling family. This government structure is unique

because each ruling family has oversight of its Emirate's development strategies and is granted a

high degree of autonomy over its own affairs including education. Abu Dhabi is politically and

economically dominated by the ruling family of Al Nahyan who have overall power and represent

the federal government.

The diversification of UAE's economy away from oil was first echoed in the UAE's Vision 2021

and the 2021 Dubai Plan. More specific to Dubai, was the need for knowledge creation through

the establishment of an international hub for knowledge-based industries and sustainable activities.

To realise this vision, the UAE sought to attract foreign investments and branches of foreign

companies and in Dubai, whole cities have been built to attract these foreign companies.

Appropriately titled economic zone, these 'free zones', allow foreign companies to establish

regional offices and headquarters in purpose-built facilities that allow for 100 percent ownership.

Dubai's approach to its higher education sector is similar to its vision for other service sectors in

that this sector can also be imported to help build a knowledge-based economy. Higher education

## 1.2 Evolution of transnational higher education:

The breadth and scope of transnational higher education has been a large, potentially lucrative market for higher education institutions. Providing students with programmes in their own countries, gives access to a new group of students who previously were unable to attend the home institution for financial, cultural or other reasons. By brining programmes and research to host countries, TNE has played a dual role. In the first instance, it has provided higher education institutions with access to a new market of students and, for the host country, human capital locally educated with globally recognised degrees. TNE also has the potential to impact a host country by enhancing the quality of local institution through collaborations and partnerships. A number of different types of TNE partnerships have been developed with the movement of courses, staff or students. Programmes are delivered through a variety of teaching modes with the IBC being the riskiest form of TNE. IBCs have the potential for large financial and reputation impact on the higher education institution.

The IBC model is one that has been the preferred strategy to develop UAE into a knowledge-based economy. In conjunction with supporting the growth of local and private institutions, the government has established several incentives for foreign institutions to establish satellite campuses in UAE. No where is this more evident than in Dubai, the city state that houses the largest number of IBC in the region. Identifying a definition of this type of TNE is difficult because

<!-- page 12 -->

of the variety of different IBC partnerships models that exist in the world. From small campuses in rented facilities offering a limited number of programmes through the flying-faculty model, to large brick and mortar campuses with comprehensive undergraduate and postgraduate programme offerings delivered through locally hired faculty. Chapter Three will explore this form of TNE in greater detail.

The reputation of HEIs and their relative position in the landscape of the home country may well be established. However, host countries pose a challenge for HEIs as they enter a new regulatory and culturally foreign environment where their reputation is not as well established. IBCs become an extension of the home campus with autonomous features existing in a foreign environment. This organisational structure is similar to multinational organisations with foreign direct investment in the form of a satellite campus. Chapter Three will explore some of the challenges with IBCs and their relationship with the home campus.

## 1.3 The main aims of the study:

The aim of this research is to explore why UK HEIs choose to establish IBCs in Dubai as part of their transnational higher education strategy. Further, the research will explore how sustainability of IBC can be achieved in a foreign market with unique cultural, regulatory and economic environment.

<!-- page 13 -->

The Uppsala Internationalisation Model (UIM) is proposed as a useful tool in exploring the potential sustainability of IBC operations by analysing human behaviour and its interaction in a host country. It posits that decision making is based on information available at the time and that knowledge based on experiential learning through established networks in the host country leads to insider knowledge. This insider knowledge increases commitment through an incremental process. UIM as a business theory can aid in developing better strategic planning that could lead to better resource allocation and improve competitiveness in foreign markets. The research explores IBCs as a form of TNE in Dubai as a contributor to rapid economic development in the race to build a knowledge-based economy free from reliance on oil resources.

## The aims of the study are:

To explore the factors that motivate a select number of UK HEIs to establish an IBC in Dubai as their chosen form of transnational education strategy.

To analyse the type of networks in host market that lead to increased market penetration.

To evaluate the home campus response to knowledge that results for experiential learning and the relationship with its IBC.

These lines of enquiry will address the following central research questions:

1. What are the factors involved in the initial decision for UK HEIs to set up an IBC in Dubai?

2. What subsequent factors may have modified the original set of reasons for establishing and managing the IBC?

CHAPTER TWO: UAE CONTEXT

2.1 Introduction:

The United Arab Emirates was created on the second of December 1971 with the federation of six

emirates – Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Sharjah, Ajman, Fujairah and Umm al-Quwain. Later Ras Al

Khaimah joined as the seventh emirate forming a country in which the foundations of its social,

economic and political systems are rooted in Islamic culture and traditions (Fox and Shamisi,

2014). The country's main source of income is oil, producing approximately 3.7 million barrels a

day in 2021 (www.statista.com). This serves as the federal government's main source of spending

on infrastructure, education and healthcare. Abu Dhabi is the national capital and the Emirate with

most of the oil and gas reserves of UAE. The second largest Emirate in UAE is Dubai ruled by the

Al Maktoum family and while it has limited access to oil and gas resources, it has implemented an

economic development policy that has elevated the Emirate's status as an important global entity

with services in non-oil economic sectors such as trade, tourism, logistics and finance. The federal

government is located in the Emirate of Abu Dhabi where the founding ruler and president, Sheikh

Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan ruled for 33 years until his death in 2004.

An important and unique feature of the UAE is the composition of its population, which has

witnessed a growth of 10 million people in 2021 more than double the 4.1 million in 2005

according to the World Bank. This population composition is largely made up of expatriates

(approximately 9 million) as opposed to 1 million Emiratis who are citizens of the country (UAE

government portal). The reason the UAE attracts many expatriates is because of the relative political and economic stability of the country and the provision of modern infrastructure and taxfree salaries as compared to other countries in the Middle East and North Africa. It provides a better quality of life in a region that has seen much turmoil and regional instability.

During the spring of 2011 the Arab world was thrown into turmoil, first with the overthrow of the Tunisian government followed by the overthrow of President Mubarak in Egypt in March 2011. The demonstrations were based on national unrest and the large disparity of wealth among citizens. The 'Arab Spring' turmoil subsequently spread to Bahrain where fellow GCC members were called to end the demonstrations and unrest in the country (Fox and Shamisi, 2014). However, other countries were less fortunate such as the Republic of Syria, with a long-standing civil war and Yemen where unrest has devastated the country. Despite neighboring political unrest, the UAE has enjoyed a relatively stable economic and political environment due, in large part, to a fair and equitable government whose main focus has been on building the country into a knowledge-based economy.

## 2.2 Social norms and tribalism:

The concept of social norms is a one that has been advanced by social constructivists as entrusted norms that are built on traditions and cultural customs. The phenomenon is built on trust and the expectation that interactions assume that appropriate behaviour will be reciprocated and any defections is viewed as a sign of betrayal. Social norms are long-time social standings that are embedded in Islamic values and culture. In Middle East societies, an Arabic term has emerged to

<!-- page 18 -->

encapsulate the applicability of this societal behaviour. 'Wasta' is a term "that refers to an implicit social contract, typically within a tribal group, which obliges those within the group to provide assistance (favorable treatment) to others within the group" (Barnett et al., 2013, p.41). A large portion of current literature on wasta is focused on the negative connotations surrounding the term. These include notions of nepotism and corruption. However, wasta has been a long-standing cultural norm that dates back to the early years of Islamic culture and tribalism. It is regarded as a solution "to a set of social problems and resource allocation issues that could be achieved by alternative institutional arrangements" (Barnett et al., 2013, p.42). The word comes from the Arabic word waseet which means an intermediator or 'middleman'. This middleman facilities discussions amongst parties in a social network and can bring parties together that might otherwise have not met. In the context of conducting business, wasta has the added advantage of providing insider knowledge that may not be readily available to the general public. This social norm is important to the discussion of conducting business in the Middle East and especially in UAE where loyalty is governed by trust and mutual understanding.

## 2.3 Higher Education Landscape: UAE

The introduction of oil market the shift from traditional way of life dominated by trade in silk and spices to a knowledge and service industry (Fox and Shamisi, 2014). This has resulted in a growing need for human talent and skill sets leading to the development of the UAE as an education hub. Considering this growth, the UAE established an agenda for its higher education to help with the building of the economy. The higher education sector thus comprised of four main pillars:

(1) "Public higher education institutions for nationals, (2) instruction would be in English as the international language of business and commerce, (3) qualified English-speaking

<!-- page 19 -->

faculty would be recruited, and (4) females would be included and afforded separate facilities as religion and customs call for." (Fox and Shamisi, 2014, p. 65)

Less emphasized, albeit an important agenda for the government, is the preservation of the cultural heritage of the UAE. It was hoped that the higher education sector would play an important role in helping preserve this culture in the face of a growing expatriate population (Baburajan, 2011).

Since the implementation of this agenda, the federal government has established three main higher education institutions to serve the local Emirati population. These institutions offer tuition-free education to both men and women Emiratis. UAE University, serving the male Emirati population, is the Flagship University of the UAE and is followed by Zayed University for Woman and the Higher Colleges of Technology. Subsequently several private and international branch campuses were established leading to the emergence of education hubs a concept that will be discussed in more detail in the following chapter. These private and branch campuses are provided with different incentives to their federal counterparts. Nevertheless, irrespective of whether they are a branch campus or a private higher education institution, all institutions in Abu Dhabi have national accreditation from the Commission for Academic Accreditation (CAA) and hold a license from the Ministry of Education. In terms of branch campuses, Abu Dhabi, has three IBCs namely, New York University (NYU), Sorbonne, and the French business school INSEAD. These universities were invited by the Abu Dhabi government to establish satellite campuses with incentives provided in the form of custom-built facilities, funding of operations and scholarships. Undisclosed financial payments were also made to the home campuses to support research. This was particularly beneficial for NYU as the additional funds allowed the institution to compete with the likes of Harvard and Yale (Tierney and Lanford, 2015). In other Emirates such as Sharjah, Ras Al-

<!-- page 20 -->

Khaimah and Dubai there exist different initiatives and incentives to attract foreign institutions to establish operations.

In Sharjah, the focus on quality higher education has been a priority for the ruling family with the establishment of a purpose-built 'city within a city' dedicated to higher education. Appropriately titled 'University City' the geographic hub houses a federal campus – the Higher Colleges of Technology, and the American University of Sharjah that is an American based private higher education institution that was established in partnership with the American University of Beirut. Also found in University City are a number of academies and the private University of Sharjah, with Arabic instruction and a Centre for cultural preservation (University City, n.d.). Although the City is a self-contained cluster of education institutions, it is not a free zone where institutions are exempt from federal accreditation. As a requirement of the local government, all higher education institutions in Sharjah are accredited by the CAA.

In contrast, Ras al-khaimah, a smaller, northern emirate has built an education zone similar to the Free Zones of Dubai. It was "established to attract and support qualified educational institutions and service providers that are committed to delivering market-driven, educational, training and professional development services" (RAKEZ Academic Zone website, n.d.). Within the free zone are nine branch campuses, five of which are from the UK. One campus from the USA opened its doors in 2006 but closed the campus a few years later due to "differences with the government and low enrollment" (Fox and Shamisi, 2014, p. 71). According to the academic zone, these branch campuses offer programmes under the supervision of the home campus, while adoping the same

<!-- page 21 -->

quality assurance control of the home campus. As a result, these institutions are exempt from any federal licensing and CAA accreditation requirements.

In Dubai the demographics mirrors that of the UAE in that the ratio of citizens (Emiratis) to expatriates is similar and has impacted the higher education landscape of the Emirate. Expatriates who live in Dubai, as with those in other emirates, are not entitled to citizenship but are offered residence visas that allow them to live and work for as long as they are employed. This visa is subject to renewal based on employment status and allows for entire families to live in the Emirate. The Emirati to expat population ratio is greatest in Dubai where over 85 percent of Dubai residents are expats (World Population Review, 2021). This demographic ratio is important when examining the higher education landscape and its impact on economic development.

The Dubai government first published the Dubai Strategic Plan (DSP) in 2015 followed by a more recent Dubai Vision 2030. The establishment of various purpose-built Free Zones was an initiative of the DSP 2015 that recognised the need for development in trade, construction, finance, logistics and transportation and education. To attract the private sector to make financial investments, the Technology, Electronic, Commerce and Media (TECOM) group, provided the main infrastructure. TECOM is a Dubai government entity that is focused on providing free zones that "cater to six vital knowledge-based economic sectors, including design, education, manufacturing, media, science, and technology" (TECOM Group, n.d). These Free Zones included the Dubai Knowledge Park (DKP) and the Dubai International Academic City (DIAC) and have had a significant impact on the economic development of the Emirate, as a means of attracting higher education institutions. In fact, by establishing DKP and DIAC, Dubai has successfully attracted the largest number of

<!-- page 22 -->

international branch campuses in the region. In the latest Dubai Vision 2030 the Dubai government reaffirmed its commitment to the further development of Dubai into a global hub for business, innovation, and research. One of the initiatives stemming from this vision is the recognition of the importance of quality education and research. Quality education is directly tied to the nurturing and development of its human capital and the development of a skilled workforce capable of serving a knowledge-based economy.

## 2.4 Local and Regulatory Environment:

Having established the growing need for human capital and given the jurisdictional oversight of higher education which is a shared responsibility between the federal government and the government of the respective Emirates, a dual regulatory environment emerged in the Emirate of Dubai. IBCs located in a Dubai Free Zone have an educational license from the KHDA and are quality assured by the University Quality Assurance International Board (UQAIB). In 2006 the Executive Council of the Dubai Government established the KHDA as the educational arm of the government with oversight of the K-12, higher education, and vocational sectors in the Emirate. A more recent law issued in 2021 (Law no.2) expanded on the aims of the KHDA to include:

"Attracting the best leading international education institutions, training centres and institutes, and research and studies centres to conduct their business in the Emirate; and encourage them to invest in the education sector." (KHDA, 2021)

The responsibilities of the KHDA, in broad terms, are to regulate education and improve educational outcomes. The creation of the authority was a result of the government's focus on regulating the quality of general higher education for expatriates because of their large demographic population in the Emirate and their only access to higher education through private

<!-- page 23 -->

provision. Therefore, the focus was and still is to regulate this part of the sector where expatriates have few alternative options for higher education. UQAIB was established by the KHDA to ensure that "the quality of such provisions [were] beyond question and the processes to ensure that quality [would] be credible and effective, whether undertaken abroad or locally" (KHDA, 2016). The purpose of this Board was to provide foreign higher education institutions with favorable market conditions "in order not to burden foreign HEPs unduly and duplicate QA [Quality Assurance] processes to which such HEPs have already been subjected" (KHDA, 2016). The UQAIB board reviews IBCs and their programmes and makes a recommendation to the KHDA on the quality of programme offerings by conducting equivalency validation. It recognizes the quality assurance oversight of the home campus and the regulatory structures in the home country.

The CAA on the other hand, is under the Ministry of Education and regulates and accredits the other education institutions in the UAE including those outside Dubai Free Zones. It is the national accreditation body that uses a standard- based, two-phased, accreditation process that involves the licensure of the institution followed by full programme accreditation. During the first phase, visits to the institution are conducted by international higher education experts working as commissioners. Phase two is the programme accreditation process that allows institutions to award accredited degrees. While CAA accreditation is optional for branch campuses in free zones, IBCs can voluntarily apply for accreditation. UQAIB validation on the other hand, is a one-step licensure process in Free Zones. An initial application to offer programmes at an IBC is approved for one year and subsequently renewed for between one and five years depending on the performance of the branch campus. Similarly, programmes are validated for one year initially and approved for

<!-- page 24 -->

periods of between three and five years in the case of renewal applications (KHDA, 2015). IBCs must either be accredited by the CAA or quality assured by UQAIB in order to operate in Dubai.

## 2.5 Conclusion:

Establishing a large-scale higher education system in a country like UAE is complicated when various levels of government have oversight of the delivery and quality of programmes. Ultimately, the degrees awarded, and the quality assurance oversight must be transparent, sustainable, and supplying the market with graduates that can help build a knowledge-based economy. Therefore, all awarded degrees must be accredited, or quality assured by competent, globally recognised bodies that can provide the necessary assurance to students, patents, and employers. Whether an IBC chooses CAA accredited or quality assurance through UQAIB, will depend on the strategic vision of the home campus. In some cases, the shift in strategic vision signals a shift in regulatory processes from UQAIB quality assurance to CAA accreditation.

3.1 Introduction:

In the early 1990s Australia became the first country to identify and distinguish between foreign

students studying at one of its higher education institutions and those that were studying for an

Australian degree offshore. Before the introduction of this distinction, HEIs had been extending

their core activities in teaching and research across national borders for decades. On the receiving

end, host countries have benefited from these activities as the recipients of foreign higher education

provisions. As discussed in chapter two, nowhere is this more prominent than in developing

regions like Dubai where, the need for a rapid transformation to a knowledge-based economy

required a quick response from the higher education sector.

The purpose of this chapter is to reflect on how the emergence of globalisation has impacted

various service sectors, notably higher education. It will examine how the globally interconnected

world has pushed HEIs to diversify their product offerings and seek to recruit international

students. The chapter will explore the various motivations that push institutions to adopt various

internationalisation strategies in order to remain competitive and help position themselves in a

globalised world. One of the key outputs of internationalisation has been the implementation of

transnational higher education (TNE). TNE encompasses many different activities including

recruitment of international students, student and staff mobility, the development of international

curriculum and research and the delivery of programmes in other countries. This chapter will

explore these delivery options and the various TNE frameworks put forward by researchers such

<!-- page 26 -->

as Knight, de Wit, Marginson and van de Wende. Focusing on the international branch campus model as a form of TNE, the chapter with then examine the various definitions of this model and its historic growth and characteristics. Given that IBCs are a form of international start-up venture for HEIs, the chapter will conclude with an analysis of the Uppsala Internationalisation Model that can help HEIs internationalise their product offerings offshore.

## 3.2 Globalisation:

For over thirty years globalisation has been a central focus for both society and higher education. The significance of this has impacted nation states as they have witnessed the flow of trade, investment, and people cross state boundaries and the subsequent erosion of domestic control in favour of a more globally integrated world economy. The result has been the need for a move to a knowledge-based economy relying on the creation of new knowledge and the call for higher education to respond. To better understand this significant global change, it is important to address the nature of globalisation as a concept and strategy for government and higher education institutions.

Globalisation is a multifaceted concept that has, at times, been confused with the concept of internationalisation in relation to higher education. Altbach, et. al. (2009) argue that globalisation is a reality that is shaping an increasingly integrated world economy that is beyond the control of academic institutions. Marginson (1999) attributed the irreversible changes in the politics of nation-states and their regional sectors, domestic classes and nationally-defined interest groups to the emergence of globalisation.

<!-- page 27 -->

Beerkens (2003), on the other hand, argues that globalisation is a process and is attributed to four concepts. Firstly, it is characterised as a geographical expansion from the local to the worldwide. In essence, the nation state becomes part of a larger global system characterised by the movement of people, goods and finances. Secondly, it can be characterised as a concept of authority whereby the authority of a nation-state is threatened by a regional authority. This threat can be viewed as a transfer of authority downward in favour of competition, upward to supranational bodies or to the side, in the form of nongovernmental actors (Beerkens 2003). Thirdly, Beerkens argues that globalisation can be characterised as a cultural concept where an emerging global culture replaces a local nation-state culture. This emerging culture is one that transcends all country boundaries and is free of any ties to any particular nation. Fourthly, according to Beerkens, globalisation can be viewed as an institutional concept that erodes "national identity, commitment and citizenship" in favour of a more cosmopolitan or world citizenship (Beerkens 2003, p. 132).

In summarising globalisation, Knight and de Wit (1997) approached the concept from a more holistic angle. They state that globalisation is defined as "the flow of technology, economy, knowledge, people, values, [and] ideas across borders. Globalisation affects each country in a different way due to a nation's individual history, traditions, culture and priorities" (Knight and de Wit, 1997, p. 6). Supporting this definition, Rizvi (2007) defines globalisation as:

"a concept used to describe almost any and every aspect of contemporary life, from the complex contours of contemporary capitalism, to the declining power of the nation-state system, the rise of transnational organisations and corporations, the emergence of a global culture challenging local traditions, and the information and communication revolution enabling rapid circulation of ideas, money and people." (Rizvi, 2007, p. 256)

<!-- page 28 -->

Tying these various definitions together is an underlying theme that globalisation denotes increasing integration across borders and is perceived as an external force impacting higher education at the national and institutional level. This is a very different concept to internationalisation, a concept that is seen as a means of interconnectedness at both the national and institutional level.

## 3.3 Internationalisation:

If globalisation is seen as the reality that is shaping increasing integration and an external force, then according to Altbach, et. al. "internationalization is defined as the variety of policies and programs that universities and governments implement to respond to globalization" (Altbach, et. al., 2009, p.7). As a concept, internationalisation has been part of political science and government relations for decades, however it only gained relevance to higher education in the 1990s when the term international education became a feature of higher education separate from other terms such as global education and comparative education (Knight, 2004). Van der Wende (1997) cautioned however, that limiting internationalisation to the institutional level did not adequately address the broad nature of the concept and its relationship to globalisation. The author proposed that internationalisation is "any systemic effort aimed at making higher education responsive to the requirements and challenges related to the globalization of societies, economy and labour markets" (Van der Wende, 1997, p.18). In 2002, Söderqvist introduced a third dimension to the definition insisting that internationalisation must focus on the change process occurring at institutions and defined internationalisation as:

"A change process from a national higher education institution to an international higher education leading to the inclusion of an international dimension in all aspects of its holistic management in

<!-- page 29 -->

order to enhance the quality of teaching and learning and to achieve the desired competencies." (Söderqvist, 2002, p. 29)

However, this definition is focused on competencies at the institutional level and does not adequately account for the generic applicability of the term across different countries, cultures and education systems. Therefore, Knight (2003) proposed a more comprehensive term encompassing both the international nature of higher education as well as the institutional level and defined internationalisation as:

"The process of integrating an international, intercultural or global dimension into the purpose, functions or delivery of post-secondary education." (Knight, 2003, p. 2)

It is this working definition of internationalisation that focuses on the relationships between various actors as points of linkages and is the term of reference in this research.

## 3.4 Motivations for Internationalisation:

In order to fully appreciate the impact of globalisation on higher education, it is important to explore the various motivations for internationalisation. Motivations are important because they describe the various outcomes expected from internationalisation initiatives and are set in motion at both the national as well as the institutional level. Knight and de Wit, (1997 and 1999) put a framework around the various motivations by focusing on four main categories: political, economic, academic and cultural/social. When internationalisation policies are introduced, they are based on these four broad categories. Internationalisation based on political motivations are mainly relevant at the national level where, international higher education is seen as beneficial for national security and the preservation of national identity (Knight, 1999). With the advent of

<!-- page 30 -->

globalisation and the interdependency of nations, countries began to focus on their economic competitiveness as a catalyst for internationalisation. This need to remain competitive resulted in linkages between economic development and international higher education at the national level. As nations compete to remain economically competitive so does their need for skilled labour to work in a more interconnected, international global environment. In response to these global changes, important international organizations such as the European Union and the World Bank began to place higher education at the forefront of their agenda (Verger, 2010). This is demonstrated by the framework on the General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS) launched in 1995 by The World Trade Organization (WTO). GATS promoted twelve service sectors, one of which is the economic free trade in higher education. By promoting this sector, it created competition among HEIs by encouraging the free trade of higher education as a commodity among its members. The resulting commercialisation of the sector produced a shift in international strategies at HEIs responding to this increasingly competitive market. These strategies include; cross-border supplies of education such as distance education (e-learning) and franchising of programs. It also included transnational student mobility, institutional mobility with the establishment of IBCs and joint venture partnerships. Academic mobility with the movement of faculty and research provided additional educational services and further pushed commercialisation (Altbach and Knight, 2011). With the introduction of higher education as a commodity and a service in GATS, Wildavsky (2010) remarked that:

"The rise of a new kind of free trade, free trade in minds…The academic mobility made possible by our increasingly borderless academic world will, like other kinds of free trade, bring widespread and economic benefits, along with valuable intellectual ferment and tremendous opportunities for individuals." (Wildavsky, 2010, p.10)

<!-- page 31 -->

Cuts in government spending incentivised HEIs to offer services and attract fee paying international students as economic rationales. These educational services have traditionally included study abroad programmes, internationalisation of the curriculum, offering foreign language instructions and sponsorship of foreign students.

From an academic perspective, HEIs are motivated to internationalise their curriculum to achieve international academic standards in teaching and research. If prioritised at the highest level of governance and emphasised through mission and strategy, internationalisation at the institutional level could add value to the quality of education as well as the overall higher education system at the national level. From a cultural and social perspective, efforts aimed at recognising ethnic and cultural diversity among nations is a strong motivating factor in promoting internationalisation of higher education systems that recognise national culture and respect for cultural diversity. Initiatives such as skills development, strategic alliances, promotion of mutual understanding, income generation and nation building through socio/cultural development are all cultural and social motivations for internationalising higher education systems (De Wit, 2013).

Knight (2004) proposes that the on-going changes HEIs and national systems are facing, produce other motivations worthy of consideration. Specifically, Knight argues that at the national level, there are motivations that cannot be categories neatly in the four frameworks outlined above. This include attracting a more skilled labour force capable of addressing the needs of a knowledgebased economy. One way of achieving this is by attracting bright students and researchers in the form of international students through higher education systems. The Singapore government has implemented various policies to attract more students including, revamping immigration and

<!-- page 32 -->

student visa regulations and offering more scholarships for international students. Similarly, in the UAE the government introduced long-term student visas allowing for bright students to remain in country and work after graduation; an incentive that is hoped to attract and retain skilled labour. Strategic regional alliances at the national level are another national motivation that allows for mutual economic benefits as well as importing of higher education to advance nation building (Knight, 2004). In developing nations, transforming to a knowledge-based economy requires importing education in order to attract and retain talented students.

At the institutional level, there are motivations for internationalisation that go beyond the four categories and are independent from the national systems. These include institutional reputation and race to move up in league tables in order to attract the brightest of students and researchers. The underlying premises here is that the stronger the worldwide reputation of an institution is (global footprint), the better the quality of education (Knight, 2004). It is debatable whether rankings and institutional reputation result in quality educational provisions and innovative research. Nevertheless, this remains a motivating factor for institutions to internationalise especially, in developing nations where the global reputation of institutions is an important consideration for students looking for transferable skills.

## 3.5 Transnational Higher Education (TNE):

'Transnational higher education' is a term that has often been used synonymously with crossborder education. Researchers have developed their preferences when approaching this concept; for example, Naidoo (2009) refer to internationalisation of higher education as a form of cross-

<!-- page 33 -->

border higher education while others such as Knight and Marginson refer to internationalisation as a form of transnational higher education. Other terms of reference include offshore higher education referred to by Chapman and Pyvis (2006). Knight (2003) defines cross-border education as an activity that involves the movement of people, programs, or providers across national jurisdictional borders. UNESCO and the Council of Europe defines TNE as education "in which the learners are located in a country different from the one where the awarding institution is located" (UNESCO/Council of Europe 2001). These movements can run simultaneously for example, when a program is offered across national borders it is usually accompanied by some form of institutional outpost or mobility.

As a result of GATs and in response to globalisation, transnational higher education has been at the forefront of changes taking place in higher education today. It is a product of the policies and programmes implemented at the institutional and national level and regarded as an opportunity for both the host and home country to develop their education systems. McBurnie and Ziguras (2007) argue that TNE for developing nations is a means of rapid escalation of the higher education sector through access to the world's most advanced education systems allowing for the development of human capital and economic development. It is regarded as a quick fix, both in terms of quality and quantity, for the education systems in developing nations. Naidoo (2011) additionally states that the provision of higher education through foreign providers alleviates the pressure on domestic institutions and provides educational opportunities to groups of students who do not have access to domestic education for ethnic or religious reasons. This is the case in UAE where federally funded higher education institutions provide free education only to UAE nationals, comprising approximately ten percent of the total population. The larger expatriate community is excluded

<!-- page 34 -->

from this provision and relies solely on private education provision. TNE is also thought to be a response to the educational needs of a growing global middle class in developing nations whose national higher education systems are unable to meet those needs (McBurnie and Ziguras, 2007).

For higher education institutions in developed nations such as the United Kingdom and Australia, TNE is a response to government policies. For example, in the UK, the Browne Report published in October 2010 signalled a shift in higher education spending. It recommended that funding for undergraduate course should be through student fees driven by market forces as opposed to block grants from the government. With these changing shifts in government strategies and cuts to higher education spending, institutions have shifted their focus to diversifying their sources of income through fee-paying international students. These fees are then used to offset cuts to teaching and research. Indeed, in a study conducted by Drew et. al. (2008) it was found that a key motivation for UK HEIs engaging in TNE was income generation. However, in a study by Leung and Walters (2013) of UK TNE activities in Hong Kong, the researchers concluded that aside from the financial capacity gained, some providers also mentioned that their hope was that their TNE activities would "serve as an advertisement for the UK as a destination for potentially mobile students" (Leung and Walters 2013, p. 492).

Knight (2016) developed a conceptual framework that identified the type of TNE activities impacting HE stakeholders such as students, faculty, programmes, and institutions involved in cross-border education. The author classified TNE activities such as twinning programmes, joint degree programmes, cofounded or codeveloped institutions, IBCs, distance education and franchise universities into a framework that is relevant to both sending and host countries. In the

<!-- page 35 -->

framework, Knight describes two types of TNE provisions in host countries: collaborative activities, with shared responsibilities between local and foreign providers, and TNE that is provided solely by the overseas HEI. Knight describes collaborative provisions as foreign providers partnering with local higher education providers in host countries for the delivery of academic programmes. Twinning programmes cofounded or codeveloped institutions and joint degree programmes are commonly offered in these types of collaborative arrangements. The second type of provision is independent with no local institution involved in the delivery of the programmes. Examples of this type of provision include IBCs, franchise universities and distance education (Knight 2016). ## 3.6 The International Branch Campus Model:

Establishing a concise definition of IBCs is difficult because of the changing nature of the model and the way HEIs reposition their IBC activities when faced with economic and regulatory changes at host countries. This proves problematic because data collection on the exact number and scope of influence cannot be accurately presented nor can a sustainable business model be clearly defined. Several organizations concerned with this form of TNE have presented their definition. The British Council (2013), for example, define an IBC as:

"The sending HEI establishes a stand-alone satellite operation known as an international branch campus (IBC) in the host country and is responsible for all aspects of recruiting, admission, programme delivery and awarding of the qualification. In addition to faculty employed from the parent institution, the IBC may employ local and/or international faculty to assist with teaching. Quality assurance of the programme is the responsibility of the sending HEI and is often subject to additional accreditation processes by the host country." (The British Council, 2013, p. 2)

Two other leading authorities on IBCs also have their definitions for this TNE model. The Observatory on Borderless Higher Education (OBHE) from the UK and the Cross-Border Education Research Team (C-BERT) at the State University of New York Albany are the comprehensive source on IBCs. In a report for the OBHE, Becker (2009) defined an international branch campus as an offshore operation of a higher education institution that meet the following criteria:

•The unit should be operated by the institution or through a joint-venture in which the institution is a partner, in the name of the institution, and

•Upon successful completion of the course programme, which is fully undertaken at the unit abroad, students are awarded a degree from the foreign institution.' (Becker, 2009, p.2)

<!-- page 39 -->

This definition is specific to branch campuses and does not apply to several other types of crossborder activities. For example, campuses where programmes are offered through a partnership institution such as Malaysia's Asia Pacific Institute of Information Technology that offers University of Staffordshire programmes through its Pakistan and Sri Lanka campuses are not considered IBCs (Becker, 2009). This is because the institute is not in the name of University of Staffordshire although the institution, through its partnership arrangement, awards degrees. Another example of arrangement not included in this definition are those where programmes are offered in partnership with institutions where the degree awarded is double or joint. The Xi'An Jiaotong-Liverpool University that awards double degrees from the local institution and the University of Liverpool is an example of this type of arrangement (Becker, 2009). The definition also eliminates institutions that may have been established as branch campuses but have since developed into independent institutions with their own awarded degrees and local accreditation (Becker, 2009). Private universities in host countries with local accreditation but academic and curriculum support from foreign providers, such as the British University in Dubai, are also not considered as IBCs according to the OBHE definition. Finally, the American University of Cairo and the American University of Sharjah are modelled on an American higher education system but with locally awarded degrees and no specific ties to any American university. They are also not included in this definition.

In a 2012 OBHE report, the organization broadened its definition of an IBC to include:

a) A higher education institution that is located in another country from the institution which either originated it or operates it, with some physical presence in the host country;

b) And which awards at least one degree in the host country that is accredited in the country of the originating institution. (Lawton and Katsomitros 2012, p. 7)

<!-- page 40 -->

This new broadened definition allows for awarded degrees that are accredited in the host country usually offered in dual-degree agreements and those institutions that offer only a part of a degree at the branch campus.

(C-BERT, 2017)

This definition encompasses a more wholistic and accurate reflection of the types of IBCs explored and is the definition that is referenced in this research.

## 3.7 Historic Developments:

The earliest form of a branch campus was Parsons Fashion School from New York that developed its internationalisation strategy with the intent of expanding its operations by including a campus in France to be closer to the fashion industry. This campus was established in the 1920s and was later followed by Johns Hopkins University that set up a campus in Italy in the 1960s (Garrett et al., 2016). It was also during the 1960s that Schiller International University established campuses in Germany and Spain alongside the University of London campus in France (Garrett et al., 2016).

In the 1970s two higher education institutions from the USA, Florida State University and Webster University, expanded their operations with the establishment of IBCs in Europe. Both Webster University and Johns Hopkins University later built additional campuses in the late 1980s (Garrett et al., 2016). However, the real, rapid growth of IBCs began in the 1990s with 40 establishments forty percent of which were towards the end of the decade (Garrett et al., 2016). This growth continued into the 2000s when a total of 164 IBCs established campuses between 2002 and 2009 and thirty percent of these establishments were between 2006 and 2009 (Becker, 2009). This period of growth is characterised mainly by the movement of campuses from sending nations of the North (developed nations) to host nations of the South (developing nations).

During this period, the sending countries with the largest number of IBCs were USA and Australia. The United States, with 78 campuses, accounted for approximately forty eight percent of all IBCs (Becker, 2009). This was closely followed by Australia with 14 campuses accounting for nine

<!-- page 42 -->

percent and the United Kingdom (UK) with 13 campuses adding a further eight percent to the total number (Becker, 2009). This number increased by sixty six percent bringing the total to 249 IBCs by 2015 (Becker, 2009). What is most striking about this growth is the increased number of students enrolled at IBCs. A total of approximately 180,000 students are enrolled mostly in China, UAE, Singapore, Malaysia and Qatar (Garrett et al., 2016). This number, however, only accounts for four per cent of the approximately five million students studying in foreign countries through other TNE activities and an even smaller percentage of the one hundred and fifty million higher education students globally (Garrett et al., 2016).

In terms of campus closures, according to C-BERT, a total of 58 campus have been reported as of 2023. UAE witnessed campus closures as a result of high admission criteria resulting in poor student enrolments and planning. Changing home campus priorities and lack of commitment to the branch impacted revenues and heightened reputational risks. George Mason University for example, was established in 2005 and decided to close its branch campus operations in 2009 because it failed to hit enrolment targets (Schoepp, 2009). Another closure was the University of Southern Queensland. A branch campus of an Australian University, it was established in 2003 with a full range of undergraduate and postgraduate programme offerings. A year into campus operations, the university announced it was shutting down due to lack of oversight and commitment from the home campus (Schoepp, 2009). To avoid such high-profile campus closures and avert reputational risks, quality assurance measure were introduced by host countries in order to regulate IBC activities. These were regarded as important measures if the IBC model is to flourish and become an important contributor to transnational education and local economies.

## 3.8 Characteristics of IBCs:

Branch campuses vary depending on the strategy of the home campus. This includes variation in physical size, number of programmes and student enrolment. Higher education institutions predominately operate under the same regulatory framework and use the same quality assurance mechanisms found at home. For example, institutions may have degree awarding powers and oversight from one national quality assurance system, however, when establishing international branch campuses, higher education institutions differ in size of operations, legal arrangement, and quality assurance mechanisms. Some institutions offer specialised programmes in purpose-built facilities like the Hopkins-Nanjing Centre for Chinese and American Studies established in 1986 with a focus on one particular field of study despite the comprehensive size of Johns Hopkins University in the USA. Others have larger programme offerings such as the French Business School INSEAD Asia campus established in 1999 with six programmes and approximately 384 student enrolments (Garrett et al., 2016). However, the bigger, more comprehensive IBCs with programme offerings of twenty or more are smaller in number but have been established for longer periods of time with steady growth of student enrolment. They emphasize research in all disciplines although their primary function is teaching. The University of Nottingham with campuses in China and Malaysia has a combined number of 13,000 students (University of Nottingham website). Whereas Heriot-Watt University, at its Dubai campus currently has an enrolment of approximately 4138 students according to the KHDA. The size and variety of programme offerings reflect the strategy of the home campus as well as host government needs.

<!-- page 44 -->

With regards to ownership, the C-BERT definition refers to some home campus ownership of the branch. However, the legal establishment of operations varies depending on host country regulations, the strategic priorities of the home campus and the financial model. Those institutions that have opted to invest directly in their IBC operations may have more flexibility in terms of their programme and research output. Direct investment from the home campus allows for more control and oversight of the activities conducted at the IBC. These include control over internal quality assurance arrangements, the range of programme offerings and faculty recruitment. It also allows for more control over the range and extent of research output at the IBC, if this is included in the strategy.

There are other financial models for IBCs including joint venture partnership where the partners may originate at the home campus and jointly establish the IBC in a host country. The arrangement would allow for each partner to retain their own legal entity at home but share in the financial investment and risks of the branch campus. In this model each partner would invest in the campus, however the campus bears the same name as the home institution and award the same degrees (Garrett et al., 2016). In other models, the financial partner may be in the host country with insights into the local legal regulations and the local higher education landscape. This private equity partner provides a HEI with in-depth knowledge of the local country and could provide an advantage over other IBCs. These types of ownership models "are a means for leveraging scale and differentiation while limiting risks and reducing the financial output needed by a single partner" (Garrett et al., 2016, p.39). Monash University's campus in Malaysia is an example where the university has some financial investment in the IBC with a local real estate development company, Sunway Group. In this partnership, Sunway Group owns 76% of the IBC while Monash owns 24% (Garrett et al.,

<!-- page 45 -->

2016). The most important factors in these types of ownership models are the protection of the academic integrity and the ownership of the academic delivery of programmes at the branch. This is usually prescribed in legal agreements between both parties to ensure that academic oversight rests with the home campus. As with the wholly owned business model, the political, regulatory and socio/cultural environment of the host country can have a direct impact on operations especially if profit estimates are not met (Garrett et al. 2016). In those instances, the home campus may face pressure to compromise on the quality of programme delivery or quality of students thereby risking the reputation of the home institution.

## 3.9 IBC and Home Campus Governance:

A number of research studies have analysed the governance and leadership challenges that exist between HEIs and their IBCs. Lane (2011) and Healey (2015) outline some of the problems IBC leaders face when trying to balance local host country conditions while trying to meet home campus policies and procedures. Hughes (2011) studies the challenges of academic staff teaching at an IBC that are responsible for academic standards and upholding the home campus aspirations. Wood (2011) explores the student experience and the lengths that some IBCs have gone in replicating the home campus student experience. The management of quality at an IBC is also a widely researched area (Hudson & Thomas, 2001; Castle & Kelly, 2004; Coleman, 2003). Indeed, Coleman 2003, highlights that sometimes an IBC is part of the institution's periphery and not a direct part of the academic core of the HEI. This has impact on the reputation of the HEI because it is directly linked to the quality of education and awarded degrees at the IBC.

<!-- page 46 -->

The challenge with operating an IBC lies with the management of the campus in a foreign country. In the first instance, IBC managers are exposed to a unique cultural environment that has impact on staffing, the students and their experience, the curriculum and the quality delivery of programmes. This environment exposes a HEI to conditions and competitions that are very different from the home campus where it may enjoy a well established reputation.

Employment of staff is one of the major challenges that HEIs face when operating a branch campus. According to Healey (2015), there are three different types of staff that have been found to work at IBCs, firstly are seconded staff who are employed by the home campus and are contracted to work at the IBC for a specified period. These staff members retain their home campus employment contracts and enjoy additional allowances while working at the IBC; they include leadership and seconded faculty (Healey, 2015). Secondly are international staff that are typically instructors and teaching staff that are hired at the same rank as academic staff from the home campus. The major differences between them and seconded staff is that their employment contracts are tied to the IBC rather than the home campus (Healey, 2015). Finally, are locally hired staff that are tied to the IBC with no home campus contracts or human resource rights. Typically, the local hired staff are paid less than those that are seconded and this leads to friction and becomes a divisive force on campus, making it difficult to manage (Healey, 2015). The cultural gap between the manager, seconded staff and the locally hired staff can also be a source of friction which can impact the quality of teaching. This leads to issues and allegations of cultural impropriety that ultimately result in high turnover of local staff and impact on the student experience (Healey, 2015).

<!-- page 47 -->

The student body at an IBC is also one that is different from the home campus and can be a source of challenge for the home campus. Students that study at IBCs may not have the same academic background, learning styles or English language abilities as at home. They may struggle with active learning and critical thinking skills that are culturally based. This poses a problem for teaching staff who are responsible for teaching programmes with the same learning outcomes as the home campus. In some instances, these students require additional support or courses to bring them up to the standards of home campus students. These support services or additional courses may not be readily available at the home campus. For instructional staff and faculty, it is also about modifying curriculum to meet local context. Adding more localised context to programmes is a balancing act for instructional staff. On the one hand, the home campus is resistant to modifying the curriculum because of quality assurance regulations at the home country. On the other hand, the host country regulatory bodies may also have specific requirements on the delivery of programmes at the IBC. For example, the host country government may want programmes that do not necessarily attract student interest. This in turn impacts enrollment projections that usually fall short of expectations of the home campus. Indeed, according to C-BERT data, most IBCs that have failed were due to the home campus inability to provide the necessary flexibility for the IBC to adapt to local host country conditions. In other instances, the due diligence in advance of creating the IBC is flawed or based on inaccurate assumptions.

## 3.10 The University as a multinational corporation:

With the advent of globalisation and the push towards internationalisation, universities have taken on characteristics that emulate multinational corporations. Engwall (2008) identifies four

<!-- page 48 -->

characteristics of corporations: firstly, their origins can be traced to the wake of the industrial revolution, secondly, their main purpose is the provision of goods and services, thirdly, is the type of ownership with multiple shareholders and finally, the presence of an internal hierarchy of administration. The author concludes that, by examining these characteristics, and because of the fact that many universities, particularly those in Europe, are public entities that have state charters and receive state funding, they differ from business corporations. The author argues that universities have more historic traditions, are more concerned with their reputation and have a different ownership characteristic. Universities are funded by endowments or government subsidies as opposed to owners who trade on stock markets. However, this conclusion does not sufficiently address universities that seek alternative sources of funding as a result of government cutbacks. It fails to address institutions that engage in transnational higher education activities as a strategy for diversifying sources of income and income generation.

Ahmed and Rao (2011), on the other hand, argue that universities when internationalising, are emulating multinational companies. The establishment of IBCs are comparable to businesses that invest overseas in new markets. The authors recognise that a key distinction between corporations and universities is motivation. Corporations are driven by profits and some universities are driven by non-profit motives such as reputation and the dissemination of knowledge. However, the authors present a compelling argument for why this distinction is gradually fading. With internationalisation, universities have increasingly set up operations in developing countries as main sources of additional revenue. These overseas expansions, including the branch campus model, emulates multinational corporations because they are expected to provide profit for the home campus as well as maintain financial independence. Companies engage in foreign direct

<!-- page 49 -->

investment as part of their product life cycle. Foreign direct investment by firms is motivated by the need to manufacture and market their products overseas. Similarly, universities who invest in facilities to offer their degrees abroad, for example through a branch campus, emulate multinational companies. A university's key products include its faculty, programmes, and research. They invest in IBCs in order to offer their programmes and provide opportunities for their faculty to conduct research in overseas locations. This in turn could help with global rankings and reputation.

## 3.11 The Institution as a Learning Organisation:

The term learning organisations was first coined by Senge (1990) in his book *The Fifth Discipline: * *The Art of Practice of the Learning Organisation *as an organisation:

"…where people continually expand their capacity to create the results they truly desire, where new and expansive patterns of thinking are nurtured, where collective aspiration is set free, and where people are continually learning how to learn together." (Senge, 1990)

In an increasingly competitive world, the capacity to understand the forces that impact an organisation and its ability to respond to these changes will contribute towards the organisation's overall competitive advantage. The type of learning will shift over time as the organisation is able to better understand its competitors and its relative position in market. Senge (1990) advocated for this type of thinking where stakeholder interactions is part of an overall system change. He advanced the notion of a shared vision that could respond to a continually changing environment that produces the necessary competitive advantage (Senge, 1990). Ortenbland and Koris (2014), built on the notion of a learning organisation by defining characteristics of an agile organisation

<!-- page 50 -->

that is able to respond to customer needs in a quick and efficient manner. They define a learning organisation as:

"[. . .] an organization with a flexible, decentralized, informal and organic team-based structure which enables its members to make their own decisions in order to satisfy the continuously changing customer expectations quickly, and which needs continual learning [. . .] to occur in order for it to attain and retain flexibility." (Ortenbland and Koris, 2014, pp. 175-176)

According to Jashapara (1993) there are two main goals of a learning process, one is to be knowledgeable of all aspects of a particular subject matter, and the other, is to learn about one's individual organisational strengths and weaknesses. Argyris and Schon (1978) introduced the notion of organisational learning that is either single-looped or double-looped. The single-looped definition is one where individuals within an organisation respond to changes in the internal and external environment by correcting errors so that the central feature of the organisation remains intact (Jashapara, 1993). Conversely, the double-looped definition of learning is one where the current organisational norms and practices are questioned. The learning is advanced through communication channels and questioning of norms that drive individuals and teams within an organisation to establish new set of norms (Jashapara, 1993).

At a higher education institution, the notion of a learning organisation is one that would be embraced by most staff and faculty provided it has the ability to impact positive change. For faculty, especially, "the attractiveness of their chosen profession lies in the opportunity to explore new territory and to learn from these explorations" (Lewis et. al, 2008 p. 292). Watkins (2005) advanced the notion that a learning organisation has the capacity to learn and change. With respect to higher education institutions, one can implement this model in serval areas. Firstly, in order to implement change, a HEIs must clearly set explicit goals and policies that are flexible in nature.

<!-- page 51 -->

They should have the ability to develop and redevelop when necessary (Watkins, 2005). Secondly, the right individuals within the HEIs would be identified and provided with leadership capacity to implement change and build a new culture that would ultimately transform the system (Watkins, 2005). Thirdly, any change within the HEI system must be built on consensus even if its mandated. The benefits of the change must be clearly communicated to build consensus. Leadership must use the top down or bottom-up participatory change processes to promote change by working together as a team (Watkins, 2005). Fourthly, turbulence in the form of external environmental competitiveness and changes can affect the process. These external environmental threats are beyond the control of the HEI and will need to be managed effectively (Watkins, 2005). They include regulatory compliance measures and accreditation or quality assurance standards. Finally, it is expected that there will be some form of resistance to change as fears of change is a human reaction. Planning for this will remove barriers to learning and build an organisation that is continuously adapting to change (Watkins, 2005). With respect to the planning and implementation of a large-scale transnational higher education initiative such as the establishment of an international branch campus, it is important to put in place policies that build flexibility within the organisation.

## 3.13 The Higher Education sector in the UK:

In a 1963 Robbins Report it was recommended that "courses of HE should be available for all those who are qualified by ability and attainment to pursue them and who wish to do so." (Robbins Report, 1963, p.8). While the report never identified whether the 'those' were UK citizens, it did mark a turning point in the UK HE sector by linking the importance of higher education to the overall prosperity of the country and formalising the idea of widening participation. This report applied equally to all UK nations although at the time Scottish higher education institutions had, at an early stage, established wider participation in comparison to their counterpart. In the late 1970s, and as a result of pressure on public sector funding cuts, the UK government in its White Paper posed the question whether non-UK students should continue to receive education funded by UK taxpayers. This debate was part of a larger government initiative to push for more oversight of universities' funding and strategies. In the 1980s the higher education system faced more public scrutiny that applied equally to all UK nations. This public scrutiny culminated in the Browne report of 2010. As mentioned earlier, this report recommended that funding for undergraduate courses should be through student fees. Students would be supported through government funded student loans that replaced the direct government funding model 'block grant' for undergraduate programmes. This proposal dealt a blow to the higher education sector with a very visible response from students in the form of demonstrations across campuses.

In Scotland, the Scottish National Parliament pledged to abolish tuition fees and as a result no tuition fees have been charged to undergraduate Scottish domiciled students since 2007. It was in that year the Scottish government established the Joint Futures Thinking Taskforce on Universities

<!-- page 56 -->

(the Taskforce) with the mandate of reviewing how the Scottish University sector can contribute to the economy and society. The Scottish Funding Council (SFC) continues to fund higher education institutions through block grants in collaboration with individual HEIs and other government entities.

There exist other variants between the higher education sectors in UK nations worthy of noting. Scottish undergraduate programmes are four-year honors degrees that allow students the flexibility to explore a wider range of subjects that are not afforded to students studying in England. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland undergraduate degrees are three years with some disciplines requiring 4 years. Furthermore, QA oversight of HEIs in England rests with the Office of Students (OfS) established by the UK Government and Parliament in 2017. The framework is risk-based and outcomes-focused thus rendering the process less burdensome on HEIs. In contrast, the QA framework in Scotland is a collaborative endeavor between the SFC, the QAA as the quality assurance agency, Universities Scotland as the representative body for the higher education sector and the National Union of Students (NUS) as the students' representative body. The Scottish Quality Enhancement Framework is based on enhancement where institutions are expected to report on initiatives that are designed to enhance the student learning experience even with the existence of quality measures (UUKI, 2023). This translates into more oversight over the sector and a strong feature of the higher education sector. In Wales the Higher Education Funding Council of Wales (HEFCW) is responsible for oversight of institutions in Wales and QA is supplemented by enhancement-focused outcomes. Finally, in Northern Ireland, the Department for the Economy of Norther Ireland (DfENI) oversees higher education and is also based on an enhancement led model.

<!-- page 57 -->

Despite these variances a saliant common feature in QA across the UK is underpinned by common principles. Regardless of whether a degree is delivered in the UK or through TNE provisions, all four nations are focused on delivering high quality academic programmes that students come to expect, with excellent learning and teaching outcomes and student contribution in the quality enhancement of degrees (UUKI, 2023). Furthermore, a consistent thread that runs through all UK HEIs is the focus on enhancing student engagement, maintaining high quality standards that the UK HE sector has promoted globally and the promotion of international recognition (UUKI, 2023).

With regards to TNE, changing government policies and stringent oversight, international students outside Europe became a focus of HEIs because they provided additional funding through tuition fees. It, therefore, became important for institutions to persuade their international markets that what they had to offer was worth the fees. This introduced the concepts of marketing and recruitment for international students. Historically, competition for international students began in 1992 when the Polytechnics, who were previously denied the 'university' status and branding advantage, began to compete for international students (Humfrey, 2011). Indeed, acknowledging the benefits that successful recruitment of international students had on the sector, the First Prime Minister's Initiative (PMI), delivered by Tony Blair in 1999, set specific targets for increased registration of international students in HE and the further education sector. By 2007 international students who graduated from a UK HEI were granted leave to take up employment recognising the contribution they make to the economy and closely resembling the USA model. With the increase of TNE and the option of delivering UK degrees overseas, international student recruitment through overseas provisions had surpassed traditional student recruitment at the home

## 3.14 International Business Models:

The theoretical framework of this research is based on the Uppsala Internationalisation Model introduced by Johanson and Vahlne in 1977. As discussed earlier, higher education institutions exhibit similar behaviour to multinational companies when responding to globalisation. They are primarily motivated by economic reasons to seek full tuition paying students in new markets to offset government policies at home. Similarly, multinational companies expand their consumer markets through product diversification in order to generate alternative sources of income. However, HEIs differ from multinational companies in that they are also motivated by other factors such cultural immersion and capacity building. Setting up a branch campus involves institutional business operations meeting host country cultural, legal and environmental conditions in order to establish an independent extension of a home campus. This is an endeavour that is more complex than other forms of TNE because the planning and operational needs of a branch campus involves, in the first instance, understanding the needs and conditions of the host country including expectations of the local government. It also involves an appraisal of market conditions, competition, and student needs in order to ensure the appropriate mix and level of programme offerings that will give the campus a competitive advantage. At home, strong leadership at the institution must think strategically and engage with the wider university community including the administrative and academic staff in order to ensure a common vision for the successful operation of an IBC (Mustafa et. al., 2022). Related to engagement of resources is understanding the local

<!-- page 60 -->

socio/ cultural environment of the host country and ensuring that faculty and staff share the same cultural values and establish appropriate hiring and retention policies. Finally, for the IBC to be sustainable, it is important that appropriate budgets and realistic timelines are established for operations to commence and to allow a degree of flexibility so that the IBC may pivot according to host country conditions.

John Dunning (1980, 1981) developed a tool for assessing market penetration and the various rationales and constraints surrounding products diversification across borders. He focused on the theory of ownership, location, and internationalisation (OLI). Using case studies, Dunning concluded that multinational companies would only be involved in overseas commercial activities and diversify their product offering if the following conditions are met in the table below:

> Table 3.6: Dunning's OLI model for assessment of market penetration

1.Ownership advantage: A company internationalize when it possesses specific ownership advantages over other companies in host country. These advantages include unique assets that are internal to the company.

2.Location advantage: There exists specific location advantages in the host country that are favorable and more profitable for the company to exploit.

3.Internationalization advantage: These assets are utilized by the company itself rather than outsourced to a local entity in the host country. This allows the company to reduce transaction cost during market uncertainties.

Wilkins and Huisman (2012) applied W. R. Scott's three analytical pillars (the regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive) as a framework for analysing HEIs who opt for the IBC model. In their analysis, they outlined advantages for foreign market penetration arguing that the

<!-- page 61 -->

regulative, normative, and cultural setting within a host country can present advantages to market entry. The regulative elements emphasise structure, rules, policies, and sanctions for those that break them. Normative elements are the moral norms and habits of people whereas culturalcognitive elements are the beliefs, assumptions and value systems of a nation.

Shams and Huisman (2012), on the other hand, applied the Prahalad and Doz (1987) 'I-R paradigm' as a theory applicable to the key managerial complexities of running branch campuses. They argued that the larger the cultural and regulatory distance between the home country and the host, the more pressure there is to localize production. On the other hand, rational choice theory argues that decision makers are rational people by nature who calculate the costs and benefits of all actions before deciding. In these business theories it is assumed that all relevant information on the host market is available to the decision maker at the initial planning stage allowing for an informed decision prior to implementation. However, changes in host country conditions can impact on both initial strategies and long-term operations. These changes may be new or existing but become evident once operations commence.

## 3.15 Uppsala Internationalisation Model (UIM):

Williamson's Transaction Cost Theory (TCT) (1985, 1998, 2010) is an economic model that approaches market entry with the premises that, until a foreign market becomes familiar to a firm and provides low risks of market entry and penetration, a firm should exercise low levels of ownership. This model assumes that franchising of products in foreign market with low financial liabilities is preferable to over whole ownership of foreign production. Johanson and Vahlne's UIM

<!-- page 62 -->

(1977, 1990, 2009) is a behavioural model that posits the incremental stages of market penetration. While it is not an economic model, its premise is similar to TCT theory. Through empirical observations of Swedish companies, Johanson and Vahlne concluded that firms develop their international operations in small, incremental steps. The premise is that internationalisation is the product of a series of small incremental decisions designed to overcome a key obstacle in the development of international operations. The obstacle, being the lack of knowledge of foreign markets, is only overcome after having established operations. Once operational, companies obtain better knowledge of the foreign market in which they are operating, thus leading to greater commercial commitments and increased integration in a foreign market (Johanson and Vahlne, 1977). Experiential knowledge is knowledge that can only be learned through personal experience and interaction. This is different from objective knowledge, which is knowledge that can be taught and present in the planning stage. Experiential knowledge of markets present firms with information on various factors that may not be evident in the initial planning stage (objective knowledge). These include the changing diversity and size of the market, the type of business practices and ethics as well as cultural and traditional norms. In this incremental process, one-step builds on the previous, where increased market knowledge leads to more commitment and increased market activity.

Healey (2008) applied the UIM to transnational higher education arguing that the four steps to the incremental process are: exporting, licensing production, joint ventures and sole ventures (Healey, 2008). The first step of exporting is the provision of educational services to international students at the home campus. At this initial stage, HEIs actively engage in international student recruitment

<!-- page 64 -->

through face-to-face delivery of programmes at the home campus or through distance and online provision (Healey, 2008). The second step to TNE development is licensing of production, which in higher education terms, is typically the process where a higher education institution partners with a local institution to offer part or all of its degree programmes. A variation of this form of TNE includes the delivery of an undergraduate programme, for example, in a '1+2' to '2+1'. (Healey, 2008). The third step of the Uppsala Internationalisation Model is joint ventures through the establishment of offshore operations. This form of TNE is common in Singapore and Malaysia where local governments recognised the value of building capacity in national institutions through collaborations with Western counterparts. Examples of these types of arrangements include MIT, Stanford and Technical University of Munich collaborating with local Singaporean universities to deliver postgraduate programmes with dedicated teaching facilities at these host universities (Healey, 2008). According to Healey, the final step of the Uppsala Internationalisation Model as it relates to TNE is sole ventures. These are wholly owned branch campuses such as the University of New South Wales's campus in Singapore and the more recently established University of Birmingham in Dubai. The joint venture and sole venture processes are utilised by HEIs who wish to provide access to students in foreign markets who, for a variety of reasons, are unable to access higher education programmes at the home campus. While the Uppsala Internationalisation Model is useful in explaining these various forms of TNE initiatives, limited research is presented on how IBCs behave after the initial planning stage. A 2009 revised version of the Uppsala process could help improve and inform our understanding of how IBCs operate and pivot to remain more adaptable and sustainable.

<!-- page 65 -->

The Uppsala Internationalisation Model describes the process for incrementalism but does not specify a prescribed form which increased commitment may take. Indeed, the authors argue that in some instances, commitment may decline or ceases if performance and conditions at the host country are unfavourable (Johanson and Vahlne, 2009). However, the underlying assumption is that any international operation requires experiential learning of the host environment through established operations. In this case, the model is regarded as behavioural in nature and was subsequently revised in 2009 to include the development of network relationships in host markets. In this revised version of the model, Johanson and Vahlne recognise that internationalisation is understood as a behavioural process of building 'insidership' positions in relevant networks. The success of a company is therefore dependent on what happens within relationships built in relevant networks (Johanson and Vahlne, 2009). More specifically, the authors state that:

Anything that happens, happens within the context of a relationship, and a firm that is well established in a relevant network or networks is an "insider" […] it is to a large extent via relationships that firms learn, and build trust and commitment – the essential elements of the internationalization process." (Johanson and Vahlne, 2009, p.1415)

Networks in this context are generic and can refer to relationships built with like institutions or other stakeholders such as industry or government entities. Compared to the original model, the revised model affirms that the liabilities of being an outsider hinder opportunities for internationalising more than the liability of operating in a foreign environment. In both instances, there lies a certain absence of knowledge of the host country that creates an obstacle to further integration. In the revised version however, experiential knowledge is gained through an interactive process of relationship or network building. This relationship building process is crucial in gaining market and institutional knowledge especially in distant and emerging markets where

<!-- page 66 -->

rules and regulations are not as formalised. The argument is that gaining country-specific knowledge of a host country entails network engagements and building trust through outreach.

In the Arab world trust is founded on relationships that are built based on loyalty and honour. "The relationship hierarchy begins with the immediate family, then relatives and then clan and tribe affiliate" (Houjeir and Brennan 2017, p. 496). In such a traditional environment, the UIM would suggests that international opportunities and success are built by developing insider knowledge, experience and trust gained over time. ## 4.2. The Qualitative Research Methodology:

There is a broad acceptance that scientists create theories to explain complex phenomena. The methodology which a researcher adopts to answer a research question is reflective of their ontological and epistemological world view and is not to be confused with the research method, which is the techniques used for conducting the research. Ontology is the study of the notion of reality and differs for researchers concerned with studies in the natural science, where reality is objective and independent of social actors, and those whose focus is the study of human behaviour. When studying human behaviour, unlike in the natural sciences, life experiences naturally lead to the construct of multiple realities as they are perceived by individuals. The ontological view of this

<!-- page 70 -->

researcher is one that closely associates with human behaviour in relation to how decisions are made when setting up an IBC. Human behaviour is dependent on an individual's world views and how they interact in society. This 'social construction of reality' is institutionalised by the way we interpret and understand events and how we respond to them (Berger and Luckmann, 1966).

Research methodology is concerned with the logics behind a chosen method and why a particular technique is more appropriate than another in addressing the research questions. There are two broad methodologies: quantitative and qualitative. These methodologies are fluid and overlap depending on the research question. They should not be viewed as opposite dichotomies, however (Creswell, 2014). Quantitative methodology tests theories by comparing and identifying the relationship between different variables. Typically, this form of methodology uses numbered data to analyze statistical similarities or variances. A worldview based on foundationalism or externalism would espouse to this type of methodology. Alternatively, qualitative methodology explores the relationships and meanings which individuals assign to a social or human problem. It is individualistic and best defines the processes that describe relationships rather than structures. In qualitative research, the data is collected in the participant's own settings where the researcher will make meaningful interpretations of the data inductively, built on particular observations in relation to general thematic findings (Creswell, 2014). Those who engage in this form of inquiry have a worldview that is internal and anti-foundationalist in its approach. Mixed methods are a middle ground between qualitative and quantitative methods and engages in elements of both methodologies.

<!-- page 71 -->

There are distinguishing characteristics of qualitative research. The research is undertaken in a natural setting with a focus on meanings and perceptions where outcomes are based on the interpretations of these perceptions. It emphasizes the role of values and the interconnectedness of various social elements within reality. The methodology also highlights the close relationship between the researcher and what is studied to establish how experience is created and given meaning. It also accommodates a number of variables which create a strength for investigating the research questions and more particularly the establishment of IBCs.

## 4.3 Critical Realism Theory:

Thomas Kuhn (2009) coined the term 'Paradigm' based on two core notions; firstly, a paradigm is a tool that provides scientists with the means to explain and understand the natural world. Secondly, the community of researchers working within a paradigm seek common knowledge and apply them to an establish theoretical concept (Kuhn, 2009). Therefore, a research paradigm is research conducted amongst researchers that share a common outlook of ontology, epistemology, axiology and their research methodology.

In the field of research paradigms there are two opposing 'worldviews': positivism and constructivism. Positivists, from an ontological view, regard the real world as existing externally and unchanged. Epistemologically, they believe in research that is based on a scientific approach where researchers are tasked with finding reality "rather than to create or interpret it" (Wicks and Freeman, 1998, p. 125). They are concerned with the reporting of empirical facts as natural observers through a descriptive pattern without providing any prescriptive notions or human

<!-- page 72 -->

biases. Axiologically, positivists view research as external and unchanging free of value-laden assumptions. They use qualitative data driven statistical analysis to test hypotheses.

On the other end of the spectrum are the constructivist who believe that ontologically, scientists cannot be independent neutral observers but are rather active participants in the collection of evidence (Morgan, 1980). Epistemologically, constructivists believe that the world is complex and can only be understood from a holistic approach that incorporates social behaviour and, observations of various interpretations of social contexts. Axiologically, constructivists believe that the researcher's own values are intertwined with the research of the phenomena. Methodologically, constructivist use a more inductive, qualitative research methodology based on observations to build their theory. This researcher considered these two opposing spectrums of worldviews in terms of their relationship to the research questions and what constitutes reality. The positivist view was worthy of consideration but a more important interpretation, fundamental to the research questions, lies in the importance of social interpretation of how events happened the way they did. An acknowledgement that a real world exists that is born of social interactions and interpretations is the driving force for this research and the foundation for the adoption of a more intermediate position, a conceptual framework known as critical realism.

Critical realism is a relatively new philosophy that was first coined by the British philosopher Roy Bhaskar (1998). According to Bhaskar, the assumptions made in critical realism is that a real world exists that is independent of our knowledge of it (Bhaskar, 2008). It recognizes the importance of causation, in essence accepting that there is a real world that exists independently of the individual and that it is possible to learn from this world. The objects of study can be regarded as:

<!-- page 73 -->

"neither phenomena (empiricism)nor human constructs imposed upon phenomena (idealism), but real structures which endure and operate independently of our knowledge, our experience and the conditions which allow us access to them." (Bhaskar, 2008, p.15)

Reality is divided into three domains: the real, the actual and the empirical. In the real world there exist mechanisms made up of physical and social structures with capabilities for behavior. These mechanisms may or may not trigger events in the second domain of the actual. In the third domain, the empirical, these mechanisms may or may not be observed (Bygstad et. al, 2016).

Sayer (1992) set out a detailed account of critical realist ontology with eight key assumptions. In summary, these assumptions are firstly, the realization that the world exist independently of our knowledge of it, and that, secondly, our knowledge of the world is fallible and theory-laden, therefore concepts of truth and falsity fail to provide a coherent view of the relationships between knowledge and its objects. Thirdly, knowledge develops neither wholly continuously, nor discontinuously, through simultaneous and universal changes in concepts, and fourthly, objects, whether they are natural or social, have ways of acting and are liable to be influenced. Fifthly, the world is stratified, consisting of events, objects and structures which have powers and liabilities capable of generating events, and social phenomena such as actions and institutions which are concept dependent (Sayer, 1992). While they have to be interpreted by the researcher's own frame of meaning, they exist regardless of the researcher's interpretation of them. Finally, science, or the production of any kind of knowledge, is considered a social practice and must be evaluated critically (Sayer, 1992). Most importantly is the use of language and the way we communicate which are connected to what is known, and if we are to gain a deeper understanding of social phenomena, we must be able to evaluate them critically (Sayer, 1992). In essence, critical realists

<!-- page 74 -->

accept that there are facts, that there exist relationships between these facts and that it is possible to establish a form of generalised theory. There are a number of different views and approaches to realism, however this researcher espouses the vision that is credited to Sayer and his eight key assumptions.

The use of language is key in critical realism because it reflects the language that is adopted and the explanations that are subsequently created. Critical realists espouse the use of causal language with careful thought. According to these assumptions, "critical realism does not aim to uncover general laws that predict outcomes, but to understand the underlying mechanisms that have generated the phenomena of interest and could do so again" (Bygstad et. al., 2016, p. 84). This is particularly relevant to the research questions as critical realism focuses on three main concepts. Firstly, it asks what are the entities that refine the research field, which in this case are the UK higher education institutions. Secondly, it asks what are their relationships? In this research their relationships rest on their internationalisation strategies as they relate to their IBC operations in Dubai. Finally, it asks what power relationships and liabilities exist which in this case, relate more specifically to the IBC operations. The implications are that the research questions are likely to reveal more about the relationship between the establishment of IBCs and the observable events that impact these initial decisions: the 'why' factor as well as any subsequent modifications. In summary, critical realism is well suited for case study research because, regardless of the number of units involved, the process involves thoughtful, in-depth research with the intention of identifying and understanding why things happen the way they do (Easton, 2010).

## 4.4 Case study research:

According to Yin (2009), a case study is defined as "empirical inquiry that investigates a contemporary phenomenon in depth and within its real-life context." (Yin, 2009, p. 18) Case study research has traditionally been associated with qualitative research although it may also be quantitative or contain elements of both approaches. It differs from comparative research methods where a small number of cases are explored for facts in order to find differences or similarities (Starman, 2013). Case study research examines a particular case within the context in which it is carried out (the philosophical assumptions) and is specifically designed for exploring new processes or behavior and is therefore useful for 'how' and 'why' questions (Luck et. al., 2006). It allows the researcher to explore and identify a set of complex factors and relationships by using multiple sources of data on a small number of social entities to develop a holistic picture through a systematic, research approach. This method has built within it a flexibility that is not found in other methods such as surveys, for example, because it allows for a movement between the researcher and the social entities (Easton, 2010). A critical realist case study is particularly useful for this research approach as it tackles patterns of interaction between various connected organisations. In this case it attempts to understand how UK higher education institutions view internationalisation and the rationale for establishing IBCs as a form of TNE. The focus on case studies is not based on the individual but rather on the issues and conditions where the case is set. As Yin stated, "you could use the case study method because you deliberately wanted to cover contextual conditions- believing that they might be highly pertinent to your phenomenon of study" (Yin, 2009, p. 13). In other words, what causes the events to occur.

<!-- page 76 -->

In case study research, the research questions create and develop relationships such as what factors are involved in the initial decision to set up an IBC and, following from that, what subsequent factors may have modified the original set of reasons. The key difference between case study research and other qualitative designs such as grounded theory and ethnography is that case study research is open to the use of theory that guides the research and analysis of data (Meyer, 2001). In this research study, the behavior of HEIs can be interpreted through a previously developed theoretical framework which can be applied as a useful way to understand the data. The Uppsala Internationalisation Model is applied to higher education institutions' behavior when managing IBCs. In contrast, grounded theory or ethnography "presupposes that theoretical perspectives are grounded in and emerge from firsthand data" (Meyer, 2001, p. 331).

## 4.5 Variants in Case Study Research:

There are three types of case study research: the single instrumental case study, the multiple- case study and the intrinsic case study. For those conducting single instrumental case studies, the research is focused on one particular issue or concern and then a single case study is selected to illustrate the issue. In multiple case studies, the researcher is still concerned with a particular issue but selects multiple cases to illustrate it. By selecting multiple case studies, a researcher is able to explore different perspectives on the issue. Multiple case studies use the technique of replication whereby the procedures are replicated in each case. The final type of variant in case studies is an intrinsic one in which the focus is on the case itself because the case itself presents a unique or unusual situation (Creswell, 2014). Whilst all three types of case study can be successful in addressing a particular issue, this research will use the multiple case studies approach to illustrate

<!-- page 77 -->

how UK HEIs establish IBCs and their different perspectives on what defines sustainability. Each of the universities in this research represents an independent case unit of analysis.

Critics of case studies point to the intrinsic nature of the method and argue that a small number of cases drawn from a universe of cases cannot reasonably establish reliable findings or data. However, this is flawed in that it assumes that each case is a single respondent. If the appropriate parameters of the research are established, then the sample, whether it is two or ten or a thousand could be considered acceptable. Other critics point to case studies only as exploratory tools, while still others believe that intense exposure to a limited number of cases may produce biases in findings. However, this research is not aimed at incapsulating or documenting general findings but rather it is aimed at capturing specific institutional responses to a complex issue by adding strength to what is already known through previous research studies.

## 4.10 How data was collected:

## 4.10.1 Semi-structured interviews:

According to Fontana and Frey (1994) there are three ways of classifying interviews on a spectrum from structured interviews such as surveys, to unstructured interviews where group interviews and more in-depth interview models are classified, and questions are unplanned. In the middle are

<!-- page 85 -->

focused or semi-structured interviews. This type of interviews allows for focus on a specific topic but also allows for conversations to flow in a two-way communication setting. This form of interview model was considered most appropriate as the researcher had enough knowledge of the research topic and developed questions to address it but relied on the interviewees' responses to provide the missing answers and relevant insights. Ten semi-structured interviews were conducted with participants, on condition of confidentiality and anonymity, to gain an account of their experiences in the initial discussions leading up to the establishment of the branch campus and the subsequent decisions on managing of operations at the IBC. A further interview was conducted with a representative of the Dubai government working in the higher education sector to provide context on the higher education landscape of Dubai. In order to ensure the data was robust, the same interview techniques and questions were used with every interviewee, who were each given the promise of anonymity. Interviews were interactional discussions that encouraged the participants to be relaxed thus allowing for thought provoking responses. This ensured the interview and the data collected was not compromised.

The difference between semi-structured interviews and other forms of interviews, such as unstructured and structured interviews is in the degree of control over participants' responses. As mentioned earlier, in unstructured interviews questions are unplanned and the participants responses are not controlled but rather allow for their behavior to impact their responses. Whereas in structured interviews, precise data is collected and coded within pre-established categories such as those found in surveys (Fontana and Frey, 2005). Each participant is asked a series of questions with a limited set of responses. For the purposes of this study, unstructured interviews were not considered due to the open-ended, non-standard nature of the interviews. Instead, semi-structured

<!-- page 86 -->

interviews were found appropriate as they allowed for new lines of enquiry to be explored through emerging themes. They were justified by the conceptual framework as they allowed for a basis of comparing participant responses to open ended questions. This was achieved by asking participants the same questions in the same order thus allowing for data to be collected, compared and quantified (Fontana and Frey, 1994; McIntosh and Morse, 2015). To ensure validity of responses, questions were structured in a manner that allows for a natural flow from one topic to the other.

Semi-structured interviews have weaknesses in two main areas. Firstly, the risk in relation to the selection of appropriate questions to address the research question or in the sequence and appropriate language of the interview questions. To avoid this problem, interviewees were asked, to elaborate where appropriate, if they wished to add additional context to the subject. Secondly, the responses could impact the study if it was felt that participants answered in a manner designed to please the interviewer or hide certain factors that might reflect negatively on their institution. In these circumstances, responses may be embellished, or key facts omitted. To address this potential problem, anonymity was guaranteed and more importantly, the fact that the researcher no longer works in a position of influence within the Dubai government allowed for rapport to be established thus minimizing this potential problem. The fact that the researcher was no longer living in UAE added a level of assurance to interviewees.

## 4.10.2 Documentary research:

According to Coffey (2014) documents are produced as a mechanism and a tool for understanding and explaining social and organisational practices. There are two forms of documentary sources

<!-- page 87 -->

that are characterised as either mediate access or proximate access (Scott, 1990). Mediate access is indirect access to documents that describe events and behaviour in the past and must be inferred. This is in contrast to proximate or direct access where the researcher is part of or witness to the activities. There is a range of documents from public, private and personal accounts. Public documents include easily accessible sources of information such as government publications, policy statements, census reports or consultancy reports. Private documents usually originate from private, non-governmental organizations or individuals. They can include minutes of meeting, board resolutions and personal accounts such as household account books and personal letters (Coffey, 2014). For the purposes of this study, data was collected from public documents that included those from the HEIs, such as internationalisation policies, and any other strategic plans as they relate to TNE, as well as government reports and manuals from Dubai and the UK. These included the QAA, UK Government and government of Dubai websites such as the Knowledge and Human Development Authority and the Ministry of Education. Websites from each case study were also used to gain insights into the nature and scope of IBC establishment and the linkages with the home campus.

In each institution, individuals were selected who had direct knowledge of, or participated in, the establishment of the branch campus and its operations. A further three were conducted earlier according to the availability of interviewees. For those interviewees

at the IBC, four were locally hired managers with extensive experience in the UAE higher education sector and one was seconded from the home campus. ## 4.12 Data analysis:

Qualitative analysis is the process by which data is segmented using codes generated from the data. Coding involves subdividing the data and assigning meaning for the purposes of analysis. These categories help understand the research questions or the phenomena that is being studied (Boeije, 2009). The analysis is concerned with identifying patterns between the data collected, the research questions and themes that are generated. Constant reference to the research questions was, therefore, a fundamental exercise of the data analysis process. Case studies generate a lot of data, in this case from two main sources of information. In order not to overwhelm the researcher and risk losing sight of the overall research questions, codes were created through a method Miles and Huberman (1994) call a start list that is created from the conceptual framework. These codes include the research questions, hypotheses and key variables. The data was then assembled to create a picture by identifying emerging patterns and relationships between the cases. The analysis allowed for the creation of two main categories that were grounded in the data. The first contained data on the original decision to set up in Dubai and the motivations for the establishment of the IBC and the second was more related to motivations of each IBC impacting its overall strategic direction of operations.

In preparation for data analysis, interviews were transcribed, and the transcripts were coded according to the two main categories created from the research questions. Each case study was

<!-- page 92 -->

examined as an independent entity and then cross-examined with other cases within their group for major emerging themes. The themes were then noted and cross referenced with reference to the document research so that triangulation could be developed. A report was then generated for each case study that was then cross-referenced and aligned to the two groups of institutions for further analysis. Results from each case are reported in Chapter Four and comparisons are made in Chapter Five for further discussion. Chapter Five will also analyse the cases in relation to UIM, drawing conclusions on the applicability of this model to the research framework. That chapter will also conclude with suggested further research.

## 4.13 Validity:

There is a wide array of definitions of what constitutes validity in qualitative inquiry. However, most perspectives would agree that validity is the process of demonstrating that a study is credible. "Researchers determine how long to remain in the field, whether the data are saturated to establish good themes or categories, and how the analysis of the data evolves into a persuasive narrative" (Creswell and Miller, 2010, p. 125). In this study validity is assured through a set of processes that the researcher uses to validate and instill credibility in the study. It is a process that starts with involving the respondents and their role in the research study. Checking how accurately they had been represented, each respondent received the same invitation letter and description of the research study. Each was guaranteed anonymity and presented with the same questionnaire.

Another layer of validity involved triangulation where the researcher examined research documents and cross-referenced with the interview transcripts looking for convergence and

<!-- page 93 -->

common themes in the study. This added layer of validity allows for the use of multiple sources of datasets as opposed to relying on one set of evidential sources.

## 4.14 Reliability:

Reliability is the consistency in which the generated data can be replicated with the same research at different times and produce the same level of patterns. In this research it was a correlation of the level of consistent responses between the participants. The research questions were explicit, and the questionnaire was designed to answer the research questions. Furthermore, each interview was recorded and professionally transcribed. Respondents were afforded the opportunity to add any extra comments they felt would be relevant and enriching to the research or thought to be providing further insights. The added layer of confidentiality allowed for the respondents to feel comfortable discussing any sensitive topics that might arise, but more important is the position of the researcher, which was considered together with any inherent biases. It must be highlighted that the researcher had been a member of the Dubai government responsible for regulating the higher education sector and had left their position in 2019. That the researcher was now working in Canada in the private sector, removed from any government ties to the region, offered an added level of comfort to the respondents. However, the researcher's knowledge of the region, the institutions and their historic operations in Dubai, coupled with a level of professional relationship with the respondents, allowed for the interviews to flow more freely.

One of the limitations of this study is the limited number of case studies and the limited number of interviewees in each case. This does not naturally lead to a degree of generalisability however,

<!-- page 94 -->

the variety of institutions from a broadly homogenous higher education system and the focus on their IBC operations in Dubai, does capture the main themes across these selected institutions in a particular host country. This may or may not be relevant to other universities outside the parameters of this research. As indicated earlier, information gathered from document research allowed for triangulation wherever possible and thus helped to minimize this limitation.

A final consideration was the selection of the type of interviewees and particularly the lack of students presenting their perspective. This was considered at the onset of the research project but was rejected for a number of reasons. Firstly, it would have been difficult to identify students that represent the viewpoints of an entire student population. Secondly, the question of which student population would need to be presented: the domestic students at the home campus, the international students at the home campus or the international students at the IBC was important. A focus group exercise was considered but this ran the risk of introducing domestic students with limited knowledge or involvement in the internationalisation strategy at the home campus and the risk of international students with a good command of the English language dominating the discussion at these focus groups. This added complexity of an additional data gathering method and sets of research group to account and consider would go above and beyond the scope of the research. It would have veered the research in a different direction or at the very least expanded the scope of the research questions and was therefore rejected as beyond the scope of the study.

## 5.1.3 Diversified programme offerings:

Diversified programme offerings have two objectives for institutions. In the first instance, diversified programmes allow the home campus to offer differentiated programmes at satellite locations. In the second instance, offering the same programmes in different locations allows for seamless mobility of students between campuses. With this strategy an institution is thought to be elevating its global profile. However, the initial regulations set by the Dubai government through the UQAIB, required programmes offered at the IBCs to be broadly the same as those offered at the home campus. As the representative of the Dubai government asserted:

"We look at student outcomes, student grades to make sure that everything is balanced between the curriculum here, the curriculum there…to make sure that…students can take the same programme, they wouldn't want totally different. Students want to have the same reputation in the degree that comes from the home campus." (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 16 August 2019)

<!-- page 103 -->

For institutions that wish to establish their IBC with the view to offering programmes not previously offered at the home campus, the local accreditation from the CAA allows for this flexibility. With accreditation from the Commission, institutions can offer programmes that may not be desirable at the home campus but meet the needs of the Dubai market. KHDA had initially allowed for some programme adaptation, however the programmes at the branch must be offered at the home campus with the learning outcomes being largely the same but can have limited adaptation and local context. Details of programme adaptation are outlined in the KHDA published UQAIB Good Practice Guide for localising the curriculum. It provides examples of localised content that "encourage certain general electives that are aligned with local needs…Minor amendments to core and specialist elective units may also be accommodated" (KHDA, 2016). More recently, the UQAIB Quality Assurance Manual has updated the programme registration requirements to allow IBCs "that have established a strong record of performance…to offer academic programmes at the…Branch that are not offered at the…Home" (KHDA, 2016). In all five cases, the institutions opted for KHDA licensure and UQAIB quality assurance of their programmes initially as part of their strategic plan for the establishment of the IBC. Three have since sought local accreditation from the CAA through the Ministry of Education to allow for customised programme offerings. Motivations for this change in strategy will be discussed in more detail later in the chapter.

Institution C has always had the ambition to establish the IBC given its history of offering distance education programmes completely designed for international delivery. For them the diversification of programme offerings meant distributing those programmes through different locations and

In the last year, the institution obtained CAA accreditation and licensure from the Ministry of Education, a move that signalled a shift from UQAIB quality assurance as a long-standing strategy. The rationale for this change will be discussed in more details later in the chapter.

Institution A has always offered its postgraduate programme as an extension of the programme at the home campus. The strategy for the Dubai campus was to allow local students access to study the same programme as at the home campus through the flying faculty model. This provides students access to the same instructors at home without having to be physically present in the UK. Since the inception of the campus, the strategy has always been to offer postgraduate programmes taught by faculty from the home campus. ## Part 2: Addressing the internal risks and challenges:

Internal risk management allows institutions to make better informed decisions about overseas opportunities and to constructively identify and approach patterns of risks or challenges. According to the Higher Education Funding Council (HEFCE), a number of institutions use risk management to identify and target new overseas market opportunities when appraising key aspects of an institution's strategy (HEFCE, 2005). In each of the five cases, the level of risk management and degree of self-evaluation varied depending on the extent of TNE engagement the home campus

<!-- page 107 -->

had developed. According to the Council, the higher education sector is changing and institutions in turn, are driving to enhance excellence. Factors that are driving risk and presenting challenges at a strategic level include increased competition and over reliance on certain overseas markets, global competition, and involvement in certain partnerships (HEFCE, 2005). The establishment of an IBC in Dubai, a new market for institutions, is considered one of those risk factors. The fact that institution E had an established track record of international collaborations and activities meant the risk mitigation exercise was less intrusive as the institution was already exposed to overseas activities. In others, where the reputational risks were thought to be higher, the risk registry was a more involved exercise that included the governing body and academics to safeguard stakeholders' interest and institutional reputation. Financial risks were considered a high priority when discussing the related threats and opportunities of the IBC project. This was closely followed by the environmental and cultural risks of operating in a foreign market. For some institutions there was additional pushback at the faculty level while in others there was contention from the administrative and marketing departments who found operating an overseas campus challenging to their long-established processes.

During the interview with respondent C1, emphasis was given to institution C's long established ambitious international strategy driven by increased competition. Indeed, the aim to grow outside the UK started as early as the 1980s through a strategic model which allowed for exporting education through partnerships. In fact, the business school set up a distance learning online MBA programme designed for international delivery. This approach to distance education and the partnership model paved the way for the delivery of other programmes. When the opportunity to establish a branch campus presented itself, the institution was able to move quickly because of its

<!-- page 108 -->

established history of internationalisation through various TNE provisions. The project was seen as a low risk and a safe financial endeavor because an AIP would be assuming the financial burden. More importantly, respondent C2 asserted, "we've been able to carry that institutional knowledge and experience into the developmental phases of other pieces of work. So, it has really informed what we've done, [and] how we've done it (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 9 August 2019). However, the Dubai government was keen for engineering programmes. While the institution had a history of offering a broad range of programmes outside the home campus, they had never delivered undergraduate engineering programmes specifically. The challenge with engineering was twofold: first, there was the challenge related to extending accreditation from professional bodies such as the Institution of Civil Engineers and the Institution of Structural Engineers to programmes outside the UK. These professional bodies had specific accreditation requirements for the delivery of engineering programmes which the home campus was approved to deliver and which the IBC would have to replicate in order to maintain. To mitigate these risks, these programmes were launched later after the commencement of operations and once the laboratory space and equipment requirements were in place and enrolments from other programmes were generating enough revenue to allow for the rollout of these programmes. The second risk was related to instructional support and whether the institution could secure the right faculty to teach these specialised programmes. Since the institution did not implement the flying faculty model, finding appropriately qualified faculty to teach on these programmes in Dubai was initially a challenge.

Like other case studies in this research, institution D was also approached by a private equity investor but decided the risks were too high and ultimately decided to turn down the offer. It was important that academic freedom and strategic aims rested within the home institution something the institution felt might be compromised with an AIP. After they declined the offer, the institution

<!-- page 109 -->

then conducted an internal exercise to identify whether there was scope for a branch campus based on a set of criteria that included a location that was English speaking, with commitment to education and local government support. It was again identified at the conclusion of the exercise that Dubai was the right location for the IBC, but the legal establishment of operations needed to work strategically for the institution. The institution opted to finance operations independently and lease facilities that were built to their specifications without the need for relying on a private entity for funding. This was quite important for the institution strategically and this legal model helped tackle questions at home about the perceived risk to academic freedom. The worry was that the quality of programme delivery and freedom to offer the variety of programmes would be challenged with a private partner model. It was important that safeguarding the academic freedom and quality of the programme offerings rested with the home campus. According to the institution, leasing the appropriate space directly from the Dubai government allowed for this degree of control and freedom.

Institutions A reported internal opposition from specific faculty with regards to freedom of travel to the region. Because the model was centred on the flying faculty model, there was certain apprehension from select faculty around travels to the region. Institution B reported faculty challenges of a different nature. Faculty unions at the institution questioned operations in UAE based on human rights records. This was also reported at institution D although they addressed these concerns from the onset of the project. Firstly, they set up human resource policies stipulating that only those who were on a Dubai specific contract would need to travel to the region. Secondly, resistance within the institution surrounding human and civil rights records were approached from a global perspective. As respondent D2 explained:

<!-- page 110 -->

"It felt at times that where we did have pockets of resistance, it was focused on Dubai…. So I tried to lift it up not focus on Dubai…. because it's about being understanding how to operate in a global context." (Anon. (pers. Comm.)22 March 2022)

Institution B reported another internal challenge with regards to its establishment. Initially the IBC was set up as an independent entity of the business school. This posed limitation on programme offerings from other schools within the university. In order to diversity programmes for sustained operations, other schools had to be given the opportunity to offer programmes in Dubai. As a result, management of the Dubai campus was transferred to the central organisation at the home campus.

In all cases the internal risk management process was designed to address several concerns. These included the degree of risks to reputation and academic freedom, resistance to the location of the IBC and the human and civil rights implications, as well as the legal structure of the campus based on the funding model. Once these challenges were overcome, projects were given the green light and the operational phase began. This following phase presented other challenges, some that were unforeseen, or unaccounted for during the planning phase.

## Part 3: International Branch Campus Operations:

As discussed earlier in Chapter Two, one of several motivations for the establishment of an international branch campus is the recruitment of international students, especially when the home campus may not have capacity in certain programmes. Whilst efforts to create an educational hub in Dubai provided a legally attractive proposition for institutions, cultural differences and legal structures posed unforeseen challenges. All five cases had to modify elements of their original

<!-- page 111 -->

assumptions and rationales after commencement of operations. This was partly due to operating and building networks in an environment with unique cultural contexts and regulations that are at times complex to navigate. Once the IBCs managed to develop and build these networks, they were able to successfully tap into the knowledge resources available in the region to achieve sustained growth. These business networks 'insidership' helped shape operations by focusing on the IBC's competitive advantages and have allowed the campuses to be more agile and responsive to opportunities, and challenges. The remainder of this chapter will outline three main areas that were impacted by newly established networks. Areas impacted include programmes and their need to adapt to local market demands, accreditation, quality assurance in response to student enrolment needs and research collaborations that grew organically as a result of collaborations with local government and industry.

## 5.3.1 Programme implementations and adaptations:

A student population that may not have access to the home campus, but nevertheless seeks a UK higher education qualification is a motivating factor respondents emphasised. As stated by the representative of the Dubai government:

"the model was designed to offer to keep the students…it was a great advantage for students to take [their degree] here, they wouldn't want a totally different [degree] they want to have the same reputation [and] degree that comes from the home campus." (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 16 August 2019)

For some students it is also about having the required qualification or skills to work and be successful in the UAE. The Dubai government's vision for creating an education hub is to provide opportunities and "open doors for employers and students who want to succeed in Dubai, even if

<!-- page 112 -->

they come from the Near East, the Far East, the Stans, Russia, [and] North Africa." (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 16 August 2019). Indeed, the recently announced Dubai Economic Agenda 'D33' in early 2023 outlined 100 transformational projects with the aim of increasing foreign trade and direct investment. The first set of projects include "launching a programme to attract the World's best universities, making Dubai a global pioneering hub for higher education." (UAE Government, n.d.). However, recruiting students and providing them with the education and skill sets that ultimately provide industries with relevant skills requires a degree of adaptation at the HEIs. This is evidenced at institutions C and E who have been operating their IBCs for over 15 years. In both cases, the adaptation of existing programmes and the need for new programmes that meet local needs required a balanced approach.

For institution C the adaptation of programmes was two-fold. During recruitment, it became evident that some students looking to accessing undergraduate programmes did not have the necessary academic qualifications for direct entry. This is due to educational systems in countries that are different from those in the UK and students lacking the necessary academic foundation in subjects such as maths, science, and English. To bridge these gaps, university C had to introduce an International Foundation Programme. As respondent C1 explained:

"There's a gap between some education systems and our degrees. And that's why we do need to have a bridging programme to bridge that gap, to make sure that they are up to the standards." (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 5 August 2019)

This foundation programme did not exist at the home campus but rather it was designed specifically to meet local market needs of the branch. It was therefore introduced early in the establishment of operations when it became evident that there was a need for a bridging

<!-- page 113 -->

programme in order to recruit students. Respondent C2 further explained the rationale for the introduction of the programme:

"We had never done that before…I remember we don't do that. We don't teach at that level…And then there is the market. The market required us..." (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 9 August, 2019)

The Programme was necessary to address access not anticipated in the initial planning. According to the respondent, it was a major endeavor for the home campus and has proven successful with hundreds of student enrollments. Institution E and D also followed with similar versions of a foundation level programme recognising the need for a bridging programme to ensure that students entering their undergraduate programmes had the necessary skill sets and foundation to succeed.

Institution C developed two additional undergraduate programmes not offered at the home campus. Architecture and interior design were desirable for a rapidly expanding city that required the skills of architectural design and creative mindsets to help grow the hospitality and tourism sectors. However, at the time, the regulatory limitations set out by the KHDA meant that the university could only introduce programmes that were offered at the home campus. As the representative of the Dubai government explained, "…the [UQAIB] model provides for a number of differences, but not wholescale differences" (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 16 August 2019). Consequently, to meet demands of the local market, both programmes were introduced at the home campus and upon graduating their first cohort, the programmes were introduced in Dubai. In response to branch campus needs, the Dubai government has since modified its programme approval process in the 2015/2016 version of the UQAIB Quality Assurance Manual. Two major processes were introduced, firstly, UQAIB can, "under exceptional circumstances…consider a proposal to register

<!-- page 114 -->

an academic programme that will commence at the…Branch and…the Home at approximately the same time." (KHDA, 2015). Secondly, IBCs that have a long-established record of offering programmes in Dubai and who have been quality assured by UQAIB previously, "may be eligible to offer academic programmes at the…Branch that are not offered at the…Home." (KHDA, 2015).

Programme adaptation was a necessity for institution E at the postgraduate levels because the branch campus addresses a different market segment. A good example is the MBA programme. At the home campus, the programme is offered as a standalone qualification whereas in Dubai it was necessary to offer pathways, or 'specialisations', based on market demands whilst also meeting KHDA requirements. As respondent E2 explained:

"Traditionally, most UK universities don't offer pathways. And in this part of the world…pathways are very, very important in marketing your programme. So, one of the things which we do uniquely is we have an MBA programme with nine pathways, which does not exist in the UK… (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 14 March 2022)

Institution D faced similar challenges with their undergraduate programmes. When the university wanted to offer their computer science programmes, it became evident that the Dubai marketplace is different from the UK. Students wanted programmes such as software engineering and applied computer programming that were relevant to Dubai but not offered at home. To university D, having quality assurance from UQAIB allowed for the institution to offer the programmes from the home campus without additional approvals. During the first two years of operations however, it became evident that students wanted unique programmes that were accredited locally by the CAA. This struggle led to a re-evaluation of the quality assurance model and exploring alternative options with the federal Ministry of Education. As respondent D1 explained:

<!-- page 115 -->

"The market was very different. The programs that the market wanted were not quite what we expected…and we had to do that adaptation." (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 9 March 2022)

At the time, this degree of adaptation could only be supported through accreditation from the CAA and licensure through the Ministry of Education.

## 5.3.2 CAA Accreditation and the KHDA Quality Assurance framework:

All five case studies developed their internationalisation strategies with the intent of setting up in a Dubai free zone with KHDA licensure and UQAIB quality assurance. The flexibility of UQAIB afforded a degree of control over operations and the ability to offer programmes expeditiously through the equivalency model which some respondents recognised as a less onerous alternative to CAA accreditation. However, there are drawbacks to the model. While it is desirable to have degrees that are awarded by the home campus, the public sectors in UAE, except in Dubai, recognise only CAA accredited degrees for the purposes of employment. Dubai government, through Article 9 of the Executive Council Resolution No.(21) of 2011 makes an exception for Dubai, it states:

"Academic Qualifications granted by the Academic Institutions and certified by the KHDA shall be recognised in the Emirate by all public and private entities for all purposes." (KHDA, 2011)

This allows graduates to work in the public sectors of Dubai but not in other Emirates where this law has limited applicability. This restricts an employee's ability to transfer within a government department in another Emirate. Complicating matters further, some countries in the region also require students who studied in Dubai to have local, CAA accreditation if they wished to work in their public or private sectors. This is based on mutual recognition agreements among countries in

<!-- page 116 -->

the region. It has posed a challenge for some of the branch campuses who seek to attract UAE nationals who wish to work in the federal government or students who want to stay and work in the region after graduation. Three institutions in this study have opted for CAA accreditation as a result of this limitation.

Institutions develop their marketing and recruitment strategies by identifying the target market segment that can best benefit from their programmes. Marketing and recruitment activities at the branch, exposed one institution to local market conditions. They realised through experiential learning, that the process of marketing and advertising programmes in Dubai was unique. Marketing and recruitment practices at the home campus may not align with the competitive market of Dubai. An institution may have an established brand and reputation in its domestic market but not necessarily enjoy the same in a foreign market. Building the brand awareness may take a different marketing strategy such as radio advertising, as respondent D2 explained:

"we've got people in market who understand their market saying, that's how you raise profile of the universities on local radio [so I said] let's set some metrics of success. But we've got to try it." (Anon. (pers. Comm.)22 March 2022)

Adapting to new marketing strategies brought about a measured degree of success, however, the institution quickly discovered it was still difficult to recruit students. Students who wanted the added flexibility of working for the federal government or other Emirates and countries in the region was still a barrier. As respondent D1 explained: "There were certain international markets that look to see whether or not you were listed on a federal website" (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 9 March 2022). Being included on the list of institutions that have local accreditation means that students are able to have their degrees attested in their home country. For Emirati students it meant the

<!-- page 117 -->

opportunity to work in the public sector. The representative of the Dubai government recognised this challenge and concluded that "the answer here would be a mutual recognition between the Emirate degrees and the Federal government. But that has been long and coming." (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 16 August 2019)

This challenge is the main reason why institution D ultimately sought CAA accreditation to attract more students. Thought to be an onerous two-step process, CAA accreditation requires institutions to adhere to 11 standards that include a detailed governance structure and prescriptive requirements for programme structure. Through a well-established network of individuals in the UAE, the institution was able to secure a real commitment from the federal government in support of its application to the CAA. Evidence of this commitment can be found in the updated standards that grants the Commission discretionary powers in deciding the depth of review needed for branch campuses established in the UAE. These discretionary powers are thought to provide the Commission with flexibility when accrediting branch campuses.

For institution A, the decision to transition from the KHDA quality assurance framework to CAA accreditation was under consideration for several years. As respondent A1 explained:

"We are very proud to be here and I'm sure the country and the Ministry shares that as well. We want to be here in the longer run and that was...a reason why we wanted to get accredited." (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 26 September 2022)

Their motivation for accreditation was driven by the need to attract UAE nationals. In fact, a significant portion of their student market are Emiratis and students already working in the region.

<!-- page 118 -->

It was therefore important for students that their qualifications are recognised by their employers and this recognition is primarily offered through CAA accreditation. Meeting CAA standards meant that they have to demonstrate that sufficient faculty were based in Dubai to support the programme. However, the other attraction for students, a unique selling point of their programmes, is access to senior faculty from the home campus. This is achieved through the flying faculty model which is challenging for programme accreditation. For the institution to have faculty based in Dubai would signal a change to operations. Having local faculty could take away from the value proposition of their programme offerings. Instead, the institution is looking to offer faculty from the home campus opportunities to conduct research in the region, increasing the number of faculty based at the IBC who can also teach. It is thought that this could satisfy CAA standards requirement.

According to the QAA, in 2013-14 academic year, institution C branch campus was already 30 percent ahead of its targeted student enrolment plan (QAA, 2014). Nevertheless, it recently applied for CAA accreditation and was ultimately successful in 2023. With only a small percentage of Emirati students enrolled in its programmes, a key motivating factor for the institution was expanding its student diversity with a focus on Emirati nationals. Institutions B, on the other hand, with a long history of operations in Dubai, regards UQAIB QA as meeting their priority for their long-term sustainability. Their target market continues to be focused predominantly on the expatriate community.

## 5.3.3 Research Collaborations:

Research is identified as new knowledge and skills imparted by faculty members to help guide and address societal challenges (Murad and Rajesh, 2010). The UAE government launched the National Research Foundation in 2008 with the aim of establishing more research leadership in the country. The catalyst for the Foundation is to help the country support a knowledge economy and address future challenges with three interdependent components: (a) knowledge production; (b) knowledge application; and (c) knowledge diffusion (NRF, n.d.). Research funding by the NRF is provided to projects from CAA accredited higher education institution that "survive international peer review, that contribute social and economic benefit to UAE, and enhance the development process in the country." (NRF, n.d.). The NRF recognises that "a strong research base in UAE universities with high quality doctoral programs in place will produce the next generation of national researchers who will help build the competitive knowledge economy." (NRF, n.d.). Branch campuses face several hurdles when they want to conduct research or collaborate on research projects. Firstly, international branch campuses do not have access to the NRF research funding unless they are CAA accredited institutions. Funding research must come from the home campus or from private entities in UAE. Secondly, UQAIB restricts the offering of PhD programmes at IBCs until the "campus demonstrates appropriate research support from the faculty and from the home campus over the students." (KHDA, 2015). This could take several years after the establishment of the IBC. In fact, the process for approval to offer PhD programmes at an IBC requires a separate application to the UQAIB and each proposal is quality reviewed individually. Research is predicated on the availability of research-based postgraduate programmes which under UQAIB standards are permitted at the branch campus once the IBC has graduated at least one cohort of undergraduate students. All five cases studies reported that research was an

<!-- page 120 -->

initiative considered at a later phase of operations. Indeed, for one institution, research was not part of the business plan for the first four years of operations because they recognised that this initiative required networking and building relationships. This would only be possible after operations had commenced and 'insidership' was built in market.

For institution D research is less a financial gain, but more about helping solve some of the problems that Dubai is facing and ensuring they can add value towards a knowledge-based economy. However, not having access to federal funding meant that research was slow to develop at the IBC. This was exasperated by the pandemic because several intended activities the institution was keen to establish with local industry and faculty from the home campus had to be paused.

For institution C, the buildup towards more research at the branch was only made possible after the scale up of the IBC attracted more home faculty interested in conducting research. As respondent C2 explained:

"Once you've reached a certain scale, it's much easier to attract people, retain people…but it takes a while to get the critical mass to have functioning departments and colleagues who are interested in the same thing, research in the same areas that takes time. But it's certainly developed. And the sort of more recent phase of development is about research and the relationship with business and industry." (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 9 August 2019)

One of the key successes factors with having engineering programmes has been the extent of research and industry collaborations in the construction industry. Dubai continues to experience rapid economic growth with big construction projects that have allowed the institution to tap into and contribute to this growing industry. Consequently, the institution was successful in partnering

<!-- page 121 -->

with like-minded organisations and government entities for innovation in construction and the built environment. Research is part of the home campus strategy to build on the international globally connected university and to becoming a world leader in specialised disciplines. Research at the branch was made possible with the introduction of several postgraduate programmes that are research-based in Dubai. In the next growth phase, the institution is hoping to expand research into areas such as robotics and data science with the recently acquired CAA accreditation.

For institution E research features as a prominent agenda at the IBC. Since its establishment, the university, has implemented "a deliberate strategy to develop research activities that underpin curricula and enhance staff performance and the student experience." (QAA, 2014). The QAA stated that this has resulted in "significant internationally relevant scholarly work." (QAA, 2014). According to respondent E2, research and enterprise is extended in several disciplines on campus. This includes innovation and entrepreneurship, sustainable development, and social psychology. Central to the institution's research agenda is collaboration with local industry. Working with the Dubai government on data protection insights and hosting conferences in applied psychology are examples of faculty driven research. Emphasising the vision of the IBC, respondent E2 stated that an integral part of the branch campus has been working in partnership with local industry and government entities to "transform outcomes for individuals, communities and organisations and to empower people to change their lives" (Anon. (pers. Comm.) 14 March 2022). This statement is reflected in the institution's strategy.

Research has developed further at institutions C and E because they have a larger postgraduate programme offerings at the IBC and because their focus on industry relevant research was part of

<!-- page 122 -->

their long-established history in the UAE. With independent work supported by industry and under the supervision of qualified local faculty, research has grown. For institution D building capacity has been the focus with research growing naturally after IBC operations. Seeking federal accreditation is thought to help to accelerate research activities and unlock funding potentials in UAE. For the smaller institutions there has been limited research because of the flying faculty model where most of the research conducted rests with faculty at the home campus. This model has limited the outreach with local industry. However, for institution A there is a push for conducting research in the region in support of their recent application to the CAA.

## Part 4: Factors that facilitate or inhibit IBC development

The establishment of an international branch campus is a large endeavor that has the potential to create new opportunities for students and staff as well as generate additional revenue for the home campus. However, overseeing the operations of an IBC requires the HEIs to adapt, and in some cases adopt, institutional business operations and policies to meet cultural, legal and regulatory environmental conditions at the host country. This endeavor is far more involved than other forms of TNE that involve the offering of programmes through partnerships. HEIs are required to establish operations in a foreign environment that has its own set of unique legal, cultural and economic systems. The operating of a branch campus requires the formation of operating systems, hiring of personnel and the establishment of the appropriate legal and quality assurance frameworks. During the planning process, there needs to be clarity on how the institution will protect its students and faculty at the IBC, the management of operations in a remote location,

<!-- page 123 -->

adherence to local regulatory frameworks and ultimately the protection of the name and reputation of the HEI.

One of the challenges with IBC operations is with regards to the relative positioning of the branch within the overall governance structure of the home campus. It is important for the success of the IBC that the branch is seen as part of the home campus and not an entity that sits on the outskirts of the institution. It must be integrated in the policies and procedures of the academic core of the higher education institution and afforded due consideration. For example, applying existing academic and governance structures from the home campus may not be suitable for the branch campus. Leaders at the home campus must be aware of the existence of a different cultural, legal and economic conditions at the host country that require a delicate balancing act between meeting host country requirements and upholding home campus standards. For example, understanding the degree of programme customisation and need for local accreditation, this responsibility rests with the IBC manager.

In order to facilitate the successful establishment of operations, the home campus must have in place certain success strategies that can be implemented during the initial planning stage and adapted once operations commence. These include understanding that local regulations have an impact on the legal establishment of the IBC initially but may require adaptation once operations commence and stakeholder engagement reveal new market intelligence that have the potential to impact IBC competitiveness in market. Further, recognising that the establishment of existing employee hiring policies, retention and professional development at the home campus, may need

<!-- page 124 -->

to be adapted to address cultural and local hiring practices in the host country. This could impact the ability of staff to work cohesively and the ethos of the overall campus.

Cultural conditions at the host country can also impact the marketing and recruitment of students as well as the quality of students' academic background. Marketing practices maybe distinct at the host country where the global reputation and name of the institution is not as well established in the higher education landscape. Students enrolling at the IBC may not have the same academic qualifications as those at the home campus, this may require additional support in the form of foundation programmes. The replication of the student experience at the home campus might be difficult at the IBC and may require a new institutional ethos for the IBC that is separate from the home campus.

## Part 5: Conclusion

This chapter has reported on the results of 10 semi-structured interviews with respondents of five IBCs in Dubai and the corresponding home campus leads who helped spearhead the project for the establishment of the branch campuses. It also included results of interview with a Dubai government representative providing insights into the Dubai higher education landscape. The data was supplemented by documentary resources such as institutional websites, government websites, published reports and regulations. The collection of data provided for themes that were then defined and categorised to produce findings and interpretations. Results were then presented into two focus areas, the international aspirations that led to the establishment of an international branch campus and the subsequent challenges during operations that modified the initial strategy and

<!-- page 125 -->

approaches to operations. The institutions were divided into two main groups that were identified by the breadth of their programme offerings in Dubai. Two are focused primarily on postgraduate programmes while the other three offer more comprehensive undergraduate and postgraduate programmes at the branch. The institutions were divided into these two categories with each category having enough similarities to allow for comparable data analysis but being sufficiently different in nature from those in the other category to allow for a broad range of findings to be captured.

All respondents identified a diverse student body and the ability to provide students with alternative access to the home campus as motivations for the establishment of the IBC. In some cases, the IBC was regarded as an extension of the home campus allowing students the flexibility to study at either the home or branch campus or other established IBC locations. This strategy is thought to help elevate the institution's global footprint and profile. With limited government support, all case studies have identified that funding operations were important considerations in the initial planning phase. Some were given limited funding from school budgets while others opted to seek opportunities for funding operations through private equity academic infrastructure providers. Finally, a diversified programme offering was also identified as a motivating factor for some institutions to allow for more access when limited capacity in certain programmes at the home campus restricted further enrolments. The exceptions to this were institutions who were initially established to offer only postgraduate programme at the IBC.

Challenges to the establishment of the branch came in the form of resistance to operating in the UAE and the Middle East region from a cultural and human rights perspective. These challenges

<!-- page 126 -->

were more prevalent at one institution where the IBC campus was the first such transnational education initiative of its scale and therefore considerable due diligence was conducted around potential reputational risks. For other institutions with an established history of offering transnational education, this was less of an onerous process. For one institution, the business school that funded the campus was international in its outlook with a global mindset. This helped bring the IBC project to fruition. One institution was able to address similar concerns at the home campus with evidence of its successful track record through previous experience having established a network of partnerships overseas.

The second focus area addressed operations of the branch campus and the challenges that institutions faced during the initial years of operations. Programme adaptation and recognition were cited as hurdles to the growth and sustainability of the campus. Three of the case studies addressed these challenges by seeking CAA accreditation. A departure from their initial strategy, they pursued local accreditation to attract Emirati students and students from the wider region as well as unlocking potential research funding from the NRF. One institution managed to build its research agenda through a long history of established networks with local industry and government entities building on their specialised programme offerings. Finally, one case study hopes that having home campus faculty conducting research in Dubai will meet the requirements of their accreditation application with the CAA.

As the IBCs matured from start-ups to daily operations, the IBCs became more embedded in the higher education landscape of Dubai. The extent of the home campus's ability to adapt and adopt policies that are distinct to the IBC is key to its competitive advantage in the host market. The

<!-- page 127 -->

relationship between the home campus and the IBC evolves over time to allow for operations to be sustainable. The implications of this as it relates to the research questions will be discussed in more detail in the following chapter.

## 6.1.1 External environmental factors:

As discussed in Chapter Three, the UK higher education system faced significant changes during the 1980s and 1990s which culminated in the Browne review of higher education in 2010. This resulted in deep cuts to university budgets, increase in student fees and cuts in international student numbers through evolving immigration policies. Parallel to these policies were the forces of globalisation that were impacting nations from the flow of trade, investment, and people across boundaries and the subsequent erosion of domestic control in favour of a more globally integrated world economy, which, has resulted in internationalisation of the higher education sector as defined by Altbach, et. al. (2009). It is represented by the "variety of policies and programs that universities and governments implement to respond to globalization" (Altbach, et. al., 2009, p.7). UK HEIs responded to these national and global factors by seeking alternative sources of revenue in an effort to becoming less dependent on government funding and at the same time, gain a larger global footprint. They began to implement policies and internal reform in response to these shifting national and international realitiesthrough transnational higher education. These included a variety of initiatives such as partnership programmes and independent provisions in the form of distance education and branch campuses.

All five case studies in this research had engaged in some form of transnational higher education activities prior to the establishment of branch campuses in Dubai. In two of the case studies, the establishment of a campus in Dubai led to the set up of other campuses in other regions. However, not all cases identified cuts to government funding or a cap on student numbers as the primary motivating factor for their established operations in Dubai. Indeed, one institution established their

<!-- page 130 -->

operations in response to an invitation by the host government. Ultimately, in responding to these factors, UK HEIs have provided international students alternative access to their degrees through offshore operations. Three of the case studies reported stronger internal connectedness between the home campus and the branch. Conversely fees from fee-paying students have allowed institutions to become more financially independent and less reliant on government funding. Finally, having a global footprint and the opportunity to serve the economic development goals of Dubai have been identified as motivating factor in all five case studies. The IBCs in this research have all engaged in dialogue through networks established in Dubai as a result of the experiential learning process. This process has identified the economic needs and aspirations of the host city and ways in which IBCs can contribute.

## 6.1.2 Host country factors:

McBurnie and Ziguras (2007) discuss several stakeholders impacted by transnational education, including host countries. For developing nations transnational education is a tool for the development of a higher education sector that is required to respond to and keep up with a rapidly developing economy. For economies in need of human capital, developing countries such as the UAE accelerate the process by importing higher education from advanced countries with more established higher education systems. This in turn, accelerates the process of developing human capital, economic development and compliments the existing HE sector.

As discussed in chapter two, Dubai lacks petroleum resources found mainly in Abu Dhabi. This has affected its development shifting the focus to diversified sectors such as trade, tourism,

<!-- page 131 -->

banking, finance, shipping and away from reliance on petroleum. In order to achieve this rapid economic growth, Dubai needed a well-functioning higher education system which serves as a catalyst for economic development. However, investing in local institutions would require time to develop the needed curriculum, enhance the quality of teaching and develop research in order to serve the economic needs of Dubai. Dubai, through the KHDA, proposed a solution whereby foreign institutions would be invited to establish IBCs that would in turn, bring economic activity and growth. This would effectively side-step the years needed to develop local institutions that were, in large part, less than fifty years old. It was a quick fix for Dubai's higher education system both in terms of quality and quantity. The strategy to invite foreign universities to establish IBCs would also provide expatriate students, already in country, with access to foreign HE degrees.

As highlighted, the population ratio of Dubai is over 85 percent expats (World Population Review, 2021). This large group provides Dubai with available human capital that could be developed to meet the economic needs of the Emirate. However, expats do not have access to publicly funded higher education which is reserved for Emiratis. Therefore, in order to ensure economic development, the higher education sector needed to provide access to all expatriates through a variety of educational offerings and tuition price points. Providing expatriate students with choices in higher education, ensures that human capital development is available to support economic growth. Consequently, it was the Dubai government's goal to bring to Dubai other types of institutions to fulfill this need and provide access and opportunity for students. Rather than enrolling in overseas campuses, expatriate students can continue their higher education journey in Dubai and upon graduation, contribute to the economic development of the city.

<!-- page 132 -->

The result was a higher education system that was fit-for purpose, serving a diverse mix of students that reflected the vast number of nationalities in the Emirate. The presence in Dubai of the largest number of UK institutions is no coincidence. These institutions serve not only British expats but, because of their reputation, attract other nationalities that view UK education as prestigious and valuable. According to McBurnie and Ziguras (2007) this demand is part of the prestige and appeal of a transnational programme, which in this case is provided by UK higher education institutions. Given the colonial ties to the region and the 'brand status' of UK education, it is not surprising that the largest offering of IBCs in Dubai is from the UK.

As discussed earlier, in Abu Dhabi, government motivations for establishing international branch campuses and the subsequent funding model differs from those found in Dubai. The Abu Dhabi government selects HEIs and invites them to establish campuses in the national capital. These institutions are selected based on ranking and global brand name, which in turn is thought to bring prestige to the higher education sector. This is a strategy that can also be found in other countries in the region such as Qatar. Invitations like these mean institutions have CAA accreditation and in turn, have access to research and infrastructure funding by the government. Examples include New York University in Abu Dhabi.

## 6.1.3 Internal organisational factors:

A mark of globalisation is the increased competition across borders that transcends country boundaries. As Beerkens (2003) argues, it is a force that can be categorised as an emerging global culture that replaces a local nation-state culture. It is not bound by country borders and emerges as

<!-- page 133 -->

a global force fuelled by rapid communication and circulation of ideas. It is marked by money and people moving across boundaries thereby impacting higher education at the national and international level. For the HEIs in this research, global competition and cuts to government funding have impacted their strategy. More specifically, government cuts have impacted on research capabilities, staffing and ultimately pose risks to the quality of education. To bridge these gaps in funding, HEIs have relied on international student tuition fees. There are internal organisational considerations involved with this strategy however, namely the increased exposure and reliance on overseas markets and global competition. For example, if tuition fees are high, price sensitive student are more likely to go to lower-fee institutions in other countries. In the case of China, over reliance can lead to market vulnerabilities if recruitment numbers fluctuate.

Competition for international students is also driven by institutional reputation and rankings. As respondent D2 explained, "having a global footprint that is greater than the UK is essential for being a truly global institution" (Anon. (pers. Comm.)22 March 2022). Being a global institution means having a significant international presence that includes partnerships, research collaborations and, in the case of the institutions in this research, international branch campuses. Rankings reflect the global presence of an institution and are used by students, faculty, and governments to assess and compare the quality and reputation of universities globally. Having a large and diverse population of international students enhances the reputation of an institution and its global footprint. The geopolitical location of Dubai provides a diverse student population and favorable local government regulations. It, therefore, becomes the ideal location for the establishment of an IBC.

<!-- page 134 -->

Another important factor for those institutions considering an IBC is the financial implication. To circumvent government cuts and to offset the more regulated domestic tuition fees and caps on enrolment at home, the IBC model of transnational education provides for a diversified source of income. It allows for institutions to access new markets and provide programmes that international students may not be able to access at the home campus. The additional financial revenues from IBCs are then used to grow research, support in the recruitment of international faculty and build collaborations both at the home campus and the branch. For institution E the increase in student enrolments at the IBC, while generating revenue for the home campus, also provided access for students to transfer to the home campus as conventional international students. Students can enrol at the IBC in their first year and transfer to the home campus for the remainder of their degree serving as a feeder of international students to the home campus. They can also move between multiple campuses in different regions gaining a real richness in culture that comes from studying abroad. For institution C the success of the IBC underpinned the establishment of another campus that built on the success of Dubai operations. Having multiple campuses allowed the institution to build a globally connected university where students are able to study at any campus for the duration of their degree. However, financial revenues are never guaranteed and must be balanced against reputational risk.

## 6.1.4 Market entry factors:

Chapter Two outlined the Dubai government strategy for higher education implemented through the KHDA, by inviting foreign universities to establish IBCs as a goal linked to Dubai's national priority for rapid economic development and building human capital. The intention was that IBCs

<!-- page 135 -->

would expedite the development of human capital and present a workforce that is ready to contribute to the overall economic development and aspirations of UAE. Providing expats access to higher education and opportunities to contribute to the economic development of the city became a key government strategic priority and a market entry factor.

Providing a supportive regulatory environment would attract institutions that are invested in helping build the future of Dubai. At the same time, foreign institutions that establish IBCs in host countries with favorable government policies, are likely to invest in the sustainability of their operations in the long term. For one of the institutions, the requirements for establishing operations in a foreign location included finding an English-speaking country with a government that was supportive and committed to education. Based on these requirements, Dubai was the right fit for their IBC operations.

Aside from a favorable regulatory environment, the Dubai government also provided favourable economic conditions with free zones or economic zones, that helped jumpstart the economy with foreign direct investment. These free zones allowed for foreign companies and higher education institutions to establish operations without having to pay government taxes, thus effectively retaining control of their profits. They also allowed HEIs to set up campuses without having to establish local governance structures or share ownership with an individual investor or company. Consequently, control of the IBC operations would be retained with the home campus. These favorable regulatory and economic policies provided HEIs with market conditions that supported the establishment of operations. Every case study in this research operates their campus in a Dubai free zone. Outside economic free zones are areas that are considered Federal territories where

<!-- page 136 -->

institutions are subject to accreditation from the CAA and shared ownership with a local individual investor or company.

For the delivery of quality programmes, it was important for the Dubai government to ensure the quality of provisions. Achieving this would require quality through a framework that would ultimately protect students, provide assurance to employers, and recognise the foreign degrees that IBCs awarded. This UQAIB framework provided favorable academic conditions for market entry. All five case studies were initially QA through UQAIB, however, through experiential knowledge and networks development, three of the institutions opted for federal accreditation. This shift in strategic policy was a deliberate means of expanding enrollment and research capabilities and will be discussed in further detail later.

The choice of locations, while impacted by economic and regulatory conditions, is also influenced by non-commercial factors. Setting up an IBC in a foreign country is more complex than other forms of transnational education because it requires a degree of familiarity with economic market conditions, student needs and regulatory requirements. For institution B having alumni with established networks in the region was a motivating factor. Through these networks, the institution was able to successfully navigate the regulatory environment and address a gap in the market. Three case studies were approached by private equity partners interested in financing operations as academic infrastructure providers through a profit-sharing model. Garrett et. al. (2016) refers to this model as a joint venture partnership where the arrangement would allow for the partner and the HEI to retain their own legal identity at home but share in the revenues and risks of the branch campus. This type of partnership can be advantageous because of the extensive knowledge and

<!-- page 137 -->

networks that private partners can bring. However, a key consideration with this type of partnership model is the need to establish mutual interests and shared goals for the IBC that safeguards the academic delivery of programmes at the branch. Private partners can be motivated by profit which can run contrary to academic quality of programmes. One institution admitted that being approached by a private equity investor was successful in drawing their attention to the region. However, through further discussions with the partner, it was decided that strategic visions for the branch campus were not aligned thus posing a potential institutional and reputational risk and that it would be best for the institution to explore other alternatives. In the end, they opted to establish their campus independently through a phased approach whereby phase one would entail renting existing facilities, followed by phase two when a separate campus was built through a direct leasing model.

Institution A was asked to help set up a local HEIs specialising in business management. Through negotiations, the HEI decided on the establishment of an IBC of the home institution. A joint venture to establish a local HEI would have allowed the institution to familiarise itself with the local student market and the manner in which business is conducted in Dubai through support from the partner. However, the chances that the institution would then be able to establish its own IBC in the future is less likely given the nature of this type of venture and the long duration of these kinds of arrangements.

Analysis of market conditions as well as non-commercial motivations are part of the initial strategies and risk management exercise that HEIs conduct before considering a location for their campus. In all five case studies, the strategic planning and risk mitigation exercises that brought

<!-- page 138 -->

about the eventual establishment of the IBC were born from ideas and proposals brought forth by those with an international mindset. While some institutions established a joint venture with a private equity partner familiar with the local Dubai market, others opted to establish their own presence by taking on the whole risk of operations.

## 6.2.1 IBC networks:

As established, the vulnerability of being an outsider in a foreign market is attributed to the lack of local knowledge afforded to those already operating in market. Networks built with other IBCs present insights and guidance on how to avoid 'blind alleys.' In the case of institution D, networking and consulting with other more established IBCs in UAE, produced insightful groundwork that helped navigate and bridge the psychic distance between the institution and the host country. Discussions around freedom of speech, for example, helped address ways in which these discussions can be addressed in a meaningful manner without infringing on local cultural norms.

Networking with other IBCs in UAE also provided insights into the regulatory environment of the country that can at times seem complex. ## 6.2.2 Government networks:

Local government networks refer to networks established with both the Dubai government and the Ministry of Education. Having strong local government networks is a key success factor to establishing a successful IBC in a foreign market. These relationships help build trust and provide access to information that include regulations, legal requirements, and cultural norms. Government networks can help navigate unfamiliar terrain and avoid costly regulatory mistakes. Establishing these networks ensures that IBCs are meeting all necessary regulatory requirements and avoiding any potential legal challenges. On the flip side, government networks with IBCs have helped provide input that have resulted in amendments and updates to regulations in order to address the changing landscape of higher education in UAE. For instance, in one case study, the growth in student numbers is attributed to the strong ties with the Dubai government through the KHDA and the flexibility that the government had provided. With a large number of expatriate students, the institution attributes its success to the flexibility of the QA framework of KHDA. More specifically, the institution has been able to develop pathways into its postgraduate programmes that allow students to specialise without compromising on home campus quality assurance requirements. As discussed earlier, in the latest version of the UQAIB quality manual, an institution can apply for programme adaptations to better suit the Dubai context. This is a development that was introduced as a result of the Dubai government responding to and addressing the changing needs of IBCs in Dubai.

One IBC case study developed strong network relationships with the Dubai government during the initial stage of licensure and phase one of operations. This resulted in an expanded second phase of operations when the free zone built a campus to their specifications. Another IBC, through its relationship with the KHDA, introduced engineering programmes in the region that helped support the economic growth of Dubai.

Since all the cases studies in this research established IBCs in a Dubai free zone where the KHDA had regulatory oversight, the relationship with the KHDA was critical and was developed early in the process. Accreditation from the Ministry of Education were not a prerequisite for the establishment of operations in Dubai free zone, as a result, getting a 'seat at the table' with the Ministry grew organically and over time through strong alumni and professional networks because these relationships needed to be fostered with several outreach initiatives that included the UK government and other ties in the region.

## 6.2.3 Alumni and Professional networks:

Alumni and professional networks in a foreign country help develop transnational activities that include the establishment of IBCs. Networks of this kind help institutions connect with stakeholders and students. They provide cultural understanding of how business is conducted and how relationships are developed. In some cases, they can provide valuable insights into the local market that help institutions tailor their programme offerings to meet local market needs. Alumni can also become brand ambassadors, promoting the institution to their networks in the market

thereby building credibility and reputation in the host country. In some cases, alumni members may be influential members of society with powerful connections that can help an IBC grow and flourish in a foreign market. They have the potential to facilitate access to key stakeholders.

Respondent B2, who spearheaded the project for the establishment of the IBC case B, was an alumnus of the business school. He was succinct when describing his overall experience by saying "this business, in my opinion, is all about people." (Anon. (pers. More specifically, they pointed to the local advisory board created with business professionals, engineers and lawyers that proved very helpful because of their influence in the region.

As discussed earlier, three of the case studies ultimately sought CAA accreditation to access funding and attract Emirati students as well as students from the wider region. Accreditation unlocked potential research funding from the NRF and the public sector. One case study pointed out that having access to the Ministry of Education was important to the overall sustainability of the IBC and the future development and expansion of operations. It was this relationship that ultimately paved the way for local accreditation without having to compromise on home campus QA systems. The CAA has a working relationship with the UK QAA. This is part of mutual government collaborations between the UKA and the UAE that includes areas of cooperation relevant to branch campuses. More specifically, a memorandum of understanding between both the CAA and the QAA states that they shall be:

"Providing relevant data points that institutions may wish to consider when establishing new higher education programmes or extending existing ones, for example through branch campuses, in the UK and UAE." (QAA, n.d.)

The main reference point is the Ministry of Education website that lists all CAA accredited institutions. Being on this list means that local recognition from neighboring countries in the region allows for seamless mobility and expanded work opportunities for graduates which in turn, allows for more student recruitment.

## PART 3: The evolution of partnerships and IBC typologies

Throughout the evolution of Transnational Higher Education one of the salient drivers for the establishment of IBCs has been the financial pressures from reduced government spending on tertiary education in home countries such as the UK. With the introduction of higher education as a trade commodity through WTO and GATS, HEIs have been provided with the impetus to explore international trade of higher education. As it becomes an established commodity capable of being sold for financial gains, HEIs have pursued transnational goals in search of financial independence and reputational growth. While HEIs are not for profit entities at home, when establishing IBCs, the underlying drivers include revenue generation and the provision of an alternative source of

<!-- page 148 -->

funding for the home campus. However, branch campuses, which are considered a high-risk form of TNE typology, push the boundary of higher education institutions' practices with the potential for big financial loses (Wilkins and Huisman, 2012). With IBCs, HEIs begin to exhibit the characteristics of multinational organisation where branch campuses become an extension of the home campus with autonomous features existing in a foreign environment. They become a form of foreign direct investment. For a HEI, the success or failure of a branch campus becomes a gain or loss of revenue and impact to global reputation.

Branch campuses as a form of TNE have been the source of many research studies. Some have focused on the student learning experience at IBCs, while others have focused on the managerial challenges of operations and the relationships with the home campus. Chapter Three discussed some of these challenges in more detail. This research has explored the sustainability of IBC operations by applying the business theory UIM as a model of evaluating human behaviour and interactions through experiential learning. It posits that irrespective of the size of the IBC, sustainability of operations is linked to market knowledge and degree of integration in the host country. It is governed by the relationship between the home campus and the IBC. The ability to understand external environmental forces that exist in a host country and their ability to impact the HEI as an organisation will contribute to the IBC's overall competitive advantage in the host country. The type of learning will shift over time as the IBC is able to better understand its competition and its competitive advantage through experiential learning. The UIM theory supports this type of learning and provides a framework for the establishment of relationship networks that build the social construct of insidership advantage. In return, the home campus must respond with

<!-- page 149 -->

policies that have built flexibility within the organisation that would allow the IBC to better respond and compete in a host country.

## PART 4: Uppsala Internationalisations Model

## 4.1 A framework for integration:

The UIM model is built on the premise that multinational corporations internationalise in order to generate additional revenue or provide alternative revenue generating sources. It is a tool that explains the holistic method of internationalisation as a continuous process marked by gradual increases in international market commitment (Johanson and Vahlne, 2017). The characteristic of integration influences the pace and form of the internalisation process. Pace is dictated by gradual integration of the firm into the foreign market through experiential leaning thus impacting the form of operations that lead to increased market commitment (Johanson and Vahlne, 2017).

Higher Education has been the beacon of national identity and a boost of national efficiency. It is the one sector that is regarded for playing a pivotal role in the greater good of a nation and a symbol of national identify. However, with government cuts to funding, HEIs have been engaging in activities that have sought to secure additional funding in the form of research and development working collaboratively with commercial sectors, contracting of services and rental of facilities as well as engaging in international student enrolment. Indeed, the expansion of undergraduate international enrolments earn institutions direct financial funding. However, the recruitment of international students to the home campus is susceptible to competitive forces that can impact

<!-- page 150 -->

financial revenues, especially with the increase of online and distance learning programmes. With the introduction of higher education as a global commodity under GATS, these global activities are no longer limited to for-profit educational groups such as the Apollo Group or Laureate. Rather, a growing number of public and private HEIs are now engaging in TNE and more specifically the establishment of IBCs. This is because of the need to expand revenue through the delivery of programmes overseas and foreign research collaborations capable of generating research monies. In this regard, HEIs that engage in offshore campuses exhibit similar behaviour to multinational companies when responding to globalisation. The applicability of the UIM is a useful tool in exploring the potential sustainability of IBC operations by analysing human behaviour and interaction in a host country. As a business theory, UIM can lead to better strategic planning that could lead to better resource allocation and improved competitiveness in foreign markets.

## 4.2 The nature of experiential learning:

Increased market commitment of an IBC is informed by relationship networks and are observed in several areas. These include the pace and development of programme offerings, the evolutionary change in regulatory and quality assurance frameworks, the establishment of research and in some, the physical expansion of campus premises. The conceptional design of UIM assumes that once a firm is able to close the gap on psychic distance, it is able to establish increased commitments in the host country (Johanson and Vahlne, 2017). That increased commitment is driven by the need for local market knowledge and by connections developed amongst individuals who have that insider knowledge.

<!-- page 151 -->

The Dubai market is built on personal connections embedded in cultural norms. The UIM calls for experiential learning that is born from insider knowledge learned through personal experiences. In the case of IBCs, this is developed through networks and relationship in market. The insider knowledge resulting from stakeholder engagement creates decision interdependencies such that the outcome of one decision directly impacts the outcome of the next. For example, networks with other IBCs in country shed light on cultural norms that informed student recruitment practices. It revealed that some students lacked the necessary academic requirements for certain programmes and thus necessitated the development of foundation programmes to close the gap. The need for tailored programmes that allow students access to local and regional markets necessitated the change in regulatory frameworks. These relationships have impact on the IBC and the home campus' capacity to evaluate and assess the competitive forces impacting operations at the branch. Three of the five case studies changed from UQAIB quality assurance to local CAA accreditation recognising the importance of local accreditation for students looking to stay in the region. Whilst still holding KHDA licensures and operating in Free Zones, these institutions responded by offering foundation programmes as well as locally accredited programmes to meet student demand. The networks with alumni, professionals and industry, informed research activities and other types of tailored programmes relevant to Dubai industrial growth through access to senior government officials and industry.

A key feature of the UIM asserts that the more resources allocated to a market, the greater the market commitment (Johanson and Vahlne, 2017). Resources located at the home campus deployed in the development of the IBC demonstrate an increased commitment to Dubai. For cases A and B, these resources include flying faculty from the home campus. In cases C and D, the senior

<!-- page 152 -->

leadership at the branch were seconded from the home campus to support IBC operations. In the case of E, the head of the IBC is a member of academic committees at the home institution representing the IBC interests.

The UIM business model also asserts that the more specialised the resource allocations are to the international outpost, the greater the degree of commitment. This is further evident by those case studies who successfully integrate the IBC into their home campus systems allowing for seamless movement of students between campuses while studying the same degree. The resulting 'globally connected' university, signals a greater degree of oversight and commitment from the home campus. However, the IBC model is not without its challenges. A deeper analysis of some of the operational and academic challenges have been explored in greater detail in chapter Three.

As learning organisations, HEIs adapt policies and shifting priorities as more increased experiential knowledge is made available. A key to experiential learning is its ability to provide the framework for identifying and formulating opportunities in market (Johanson and Vahlne, 2017). This in turn, reduces market uncertainty allowing for institutions to address internal challenges and increase integration. For most of the cases in this study this is demonstrated by the expansion of programme offerings and research development. Given the rapid change in market conditions and increased competition, the more integrated an IBC becomes in Dubai and the more flexible the home campus is in its ability to respond to these changes, the more sustainable the branch campus is likely to be.

<!-- page 153 -->

Utilising the UIM as a theory demonstrates incremental commitment- commitment from the home campus to its IBC operations, to local government, industry, students and employers. For HEIs it is also about presenting an integrated strategy that prioritises the branch campus. In all cases there was a shared vision for long-term sustainable operation, whether this was through expanded facilities, new foundation programmes, adapted programmes, or change in QA systems and the offering of locally accredited degrees. There was a real commitment from the home institutions to be more flexible, exhibiting features of learning organisations, to maintain their IBC's overall competitive advantage. By making incremental changes as operations matured, these HEIs were able to overcome challenges and support the growth and sustainability of operations in order to gain competitive market advantage.

## PART 5: Long-term outlook

The demand for higher education continues to grow especially in developing countries such as the UAE. Investment in developing a higher education system that can support rapid economic growth requires quality higher education institutions: institutions with international standards which support local industry by providing skilled workers ready to contribute their skills and with a global mindset. This creates opportunities for UK HEIsto expand and thrive, particularly in regions where there is a shortage of established local institutions. However, the challenges of operating an IBC as a form of TNE are twofold. On the one hand, there is the challenge of ensuring that the home campus has sufficient oversight of operations and maintains quality standards that safeguards the degrees awarded. On the other hand, it is also about the need to respond to local markets and stakeholders that differ from those at the home campus. For example, establishing an IBC through

<!-- page 154 -->

a joint venture partnership can have its advantages, especially if the partner has deep roots in the host country. However, balancing partners' profit expectations and maintaining quality over the academic delivery of programmes can be difficult. These are challenges that the home campus needs to address through balanced considerations for establishing IBCs.

The result of this exploratory research proposes that establishing an IBC should be an incremental process. The chosen position is that of the UIM, which is a useful conceptual tool for thinking about IBCs and their long-term sustainability. As demonstrated, IBC operations grow through experiential knowledge and through the development of networks in host countries. This gained knowledge can inform decisions at the home campus and challenge previously made assumptions at the project planning stage. There is a push from IBCs to localise programmes, research, and recruitment practices. For example, the decision to offer certain programmes from the home campus may not be the right fit for the economic market of the host country. The need for the development of foundation programmes not offered at the home campus in order to recruit students and provide access could be unique to the branch. Furthermore, recruitment practices conducted at home may not work in a developing market with a different type of target student. The reputation of an institution at home may not be the same in a foreign market. In the host country it may be that local and regional recognition of degrees is a priority for local and regional students. By analysing the different types of networks developed during operations, it is argued that the original set of reasons for establishing and managing the IBC evolved. Networks shape IBC operations, challenge initial assumptions at the home campus and call for more flexibility in order to maintain competitive advantage in a foreign market. They challenge the complexities that lie with strategic planning and the subsequent need for alignment with conditions at the host country.

## PART 6: Limitations

The IBC model is a risky form of TNE because of the significant planning and investment required. Assumptions made during the initial planning stage may have limitations when it comes to the operational stage. Specifically, planning for the establishment of an IBC based on existing knowledge of a host market may not provide a full picture of market conditions. For example, a well-known, research-intensive institution in its home country may not have the same reputation in a host country. Building that reputation and knowledge within a foreign market can be time consuming and expensive. By adopting an exploratory research methodology through case studies, this research has attempted to explore the initial planning and impact of subsequent operations of IBC on the home campus. This was done through interviews with ten senior managers at five UK IBCs and a representative at the Dubai Government. Chapter Two laid out some of the inherent limitations of case study research in terms of liability. This research has sought to limit these liabilities through its research design by identifying five very distinct cases, thus offering a credible evidence base to addresses the research questions. However, the depth of the research may have been limited by the small sampling size. Originally the plan was to interview all UK IBCs in Dubai, but it was difficult to gain access to senior leaders at the home campus to participate on the grounds that they had limited knowledge of operations at a branch campus that in some cases had been established prior to their appointment. However, although the research focused on five case studies, the access to senior leaders with knowledge and history of the strategic planning for IBC operations at the home campus as well as operations at the branch revealed a wealth of data that, on this basis alone, provided for a robust finding.

## PART 7: Potential themes for future research

This research has focused on the incremental development of operations of IBCs in Dubai and the factors that challenge strategic planning managed initially at the home campus. It highlighted the changing realities of operations in a host country as evidenced by experiential learning brought about by network development. The results of this research can inform future directions for further exploration. For example, one area of future lines of enquiry might be the internal decision-making process at HEIs and the role of champions, such as senior leaders, faculty, alumni, and professional staff supporting the IBC vision. A strong and robust internal decision-making process that involves consultation with stakeholders, can ensure that the IBC strategy is aligned with the institution's mission and values, and that it has the necessary resources and buy-in from all relevant stakeholders. Exploring this further could add value to the IBC research literature.

A second possible line of enquiry is the COVID-19 pandemic and its impact on various sectors including higher education. The impact on IBCs may be far reaching both at the home campus and the branch. However, there is a need for further inquiry into the extent of this impact on transnational higher education. For example, the disruption to mobility made it difficult for students, faculty, and staff to travel. This may have had a larger impact on those IBCs dependent on the flying faculty model than others. Anecdotal evidence suggest that the financial impacts of the pandemic were challenging for IBC, especially if student recruitment is drawn from neighboring countries who must fly to Dubai to attend classes. Finally, the pandemic may have impacted on teaching and learning and pushed the boundaries of local accreditation. With many

<!-- page 157 -->

HEIs pivoting to online or hybrid models of instruction, students with limited access to technology struggle the most. Furthermore, for a host country that requires accredited programmes to be offered in-person, online or hybrid models challenged this requirement. Understanding the impact of the pandemic on IBCs and host governments can help better study vulnerabilities in the model and inform future strategies to cope with challenges such as global pandemics.
