Most articles appearing in top management journals come from scholars based in developed and especially Western schools of business and management [(Byron,](#page-4-0) [2024;](#page-4-0)[ Swanson, Wolfe & Zardkoohi, 2007)](#page-5-0).[ Nagaraj](#page-5-0) [and Yao (2023](#page-5-0): 16) analyzing six top management journals (Academy of Management Journal, Academy of Management Review, Strategic Management Journal, Administrative Science Quarterly, Organization Science, and Management Science), found that "only 4.72% of the publications have at least one researcher affiliated with an institution in a mid- or low-income country." In the specific case of Academy of Management Review (AMR), publication data from 2014 to 2023 reveal that only 2.7% of papers had authors from emerging market institutions, while 73.2% of the accepted papers were authored by scholars from North America and the United Kingdom. Therefore, authors from the "periphery" outside schools especially in the Western world are severely underrepresented.1

The absence of that plurality, however, suggests systemic inequities between scholars in peripherybased versus traditional (especially Western) academic networks. Indeed, given the salience of conflicts and societal polarization along multiple fault lines, encouraging a generative plurality of global voices is particularly important for our times.

Some remedies have been proposed—for instance, helping those on the periphery reposition their papers and write in a style that the editors and reviewers of top journals usually expect. Nevertheless, even though some periphery-based scholars have followed this route, an underlying assumption of the existing conversation is that the Western way of theorizing is superior—an assumption that only discourages periphery-based scholars from engaging in theory writing. Hence, we seek to explore a range of barriers to publication that have not been readily seen, understood, or emphasized in previous discussions. To do so, we decided to listen to periphery-based scholars and learn about the challenges they face, the implications of their exclusion from outlets like AMR, and their suggestions for how we can be more inclusive. In this sense, our piece not only identifies some potential initiatives to help periphery-based scholars publish theory papers, but also invites Western scholars in editorial academic positions of leading journals to understand the constraints that periphery-based scholars face and to embrace initiatives to foster their inclusion and participation in theory-building efforts.

## KEY CHALLENGES TO PUBLISHING IN AMR: A VIEW FROM THE PERIPHERY

## Challenge #1: Empirical Papers Are a Safer Bet

As a theory journal, AMR requires scholars to be familiar with the process of theory building and development—a complex process for which "there are no straightforward formulas or templates" ([Cornelissen, 2017:](#page-4-0) 1). It is already well known that many PhD programs do not train their students to develop new theories, and not many journals invite or are focused on theory papers. Although this problem also affects scholars in Western schools, it is particularly relevant for periphery-based scholars.

Universities in many periphery-based markets are pushing their professors to focus on publishing their work in certain high-profile journals to boost school rankings, and those who publish in these journals are more likely to be rewarded with additional funding, good performance reviews, and tenure. Given this system, publishing empirical work is likely to be a more popular choice than writing a theoretical paper. This point is noted by one of our informants, a full professor, who stated:

The academic culture in China tends to emphasize quantity over the depth of theoretical contributions,

which does not align well with AMR's focus. Scholars are often under significant pressure to publish frequently, which makes the time-consuming process of writing theoretical papers less attractive … if you submit a paper to Academy of Management Journal and get rejected, you can still try Journal of Applied Psychology, or Personnel Psychology, or Journal of Management. There are many alternative outlets.

Three scholars from Latin America also expressed the view that empirical papers may be better received. One even said that scholars from established circles prefer periphery-based scholars to submit empirical papers to leverage data from their unique local contexts. "So, we go empirical," our informant said.

To further illustrate this point, imagine having to allocate effort to two alternative projects: (a) a theory paper that requires substantial learning on how to develop and position new theory and (b) an empirical paper that builds on local databases that are not readily accessible to scholars in Western schools. The general perception is that the latter option tends to have not only a greater perceived return but also a lower risk, as empirical papers can be sent to a broader range of journals and can build on the local advantages that those scholars have in their home countries. In contrast, developing one or two ideas for a theoretical paper can be time consuming and involves significant rewriting based on colleagues' feedback. In addition, should the theory paper be rejected, authors must choose to either revise for another journal submission or convert the paper into an empirical study. This extended process can be perceived as risky and overly time consuming. Hence, if empirical papers are generally seen as a safer bet compared to theory papers, this is particularly true in the case of periphery-based scholars who are already severely time and resource constrained.

## Challenge #2: Cultural Factors Constrain Theory Development Efforts

In many cultures on the periphery, students are trained to not challenge hierarchy. Yet this also unintentionally leads to periphery-based scholars often focusing on "extending" or "filling a gap" in the literature, instead of "challenging existing theories" in the way AMR is often looking for. This sentiment is echoed by an assistant professor from China who noted that:

There is a cultural emphasis on modesty and respect for authority … which can inhibit scholars from proposing bold or controversial ideas. This cultural context can lead to more incremental rather than revolutionary contributions, which might not meet the novelty criteria of AMR.

<!-- page 3 -->

As such, the norms of challenging established wisdom may not be as widespread, and the concept of "novel contributions" may appear in a different form than those expected by Western editors and reviewers.

Cultural factors may also manifest in the difference in language and writing styles between English and the languages of those based in periphery-based academic institutions. For example, as an informant noted:

English, being a low-context language, demands precise and clear arguments, whereas Chinese often relies on context, which may lack logical precision. This difference is not adequately addressed in formal training, even though the structure of doctoral programs in China is similar to that in the United States. Consequently, Chinese scholars might face challenges in articulating their theoretical arguments with the required precision for AMR.

Even though AMR welcomes theory-building theory with mathematical modeling or simulations—perhaps mitigating some of the challenges of translation using those techniques still requires positioning the theory and communicating its key contributions in verbal language.

More generally, periphery-based scholars face the problem of "double translation," where "[academics] translate words into English and interpret a new writing style" but they also have to pursue "a change in style and how to produce knowledge" ([Barros &](#page-4-0) [Alcadipani, 2023](#page-4-0): 579). This not only increases the costs to navigate the submission and review process in top journals, but it also begs the question of whether the current system is discouraging fresh and innovative theorizing.

## Challenge #3: Imposing Standards and Impostor Syndrome

The preceding roadblocks of perceptions about publishing theory reflect structural obstacles embedded in patterns of training, incentives, and national cultures. Our informants also cited more personal and interpersonal barriers, such as feelings of inferiority, unclear or potentially discriminatory standards, and short-term, transactional relationships. First, most of our informants cited experiencing an "impostor syndrome," whereby individuals experience self-doubt even with considerable accomplishments. An African lecturer said that the local community often perceives international schools as "superior." A full professor from Chile similarly stated: "In emerging markets, we also perceive ourselves as 'second tier' … How can a person from an

emerging market compete with top theorists? The perception is that the journals 'prefer' established authors." The lack of a role model may exacerbate this feeling. As a Chinese professor suggested, "There are few successful examples of scholars from China publishing in AMR, which means there are limited role models." The lack of a role model, in turn, creates a mental barrier that discourages even attempting to write a theory paper despite having the capability to do so.

Second, the sense of inferiority was tied to painful experiences of interactions with editorial teams in top journals like AMR. For example, an associate professor from Latin America suggested that:

There are historical and cultural conversations … about people here feeling that they are not given a chance … Both at the Academy [of Management] and in many international journals, there is a need to justify our sampled population. It appears that the only things that we can contribute to are specifically about Brazil (Amazon, Favelas) and not general management topics.

This is also related to the previous point that empirical papers may be perceived as a safer bet, if scholars in Western institutions prefer data-driven contributions.

Third, our informants pointed to the persistent challenge of establishing high-quality relationships with those already associated with top journals. As one full professor from Latin America put it, scholars outside those circles do not have access to the rich network interactions, seminars, and personal conversations that may increase the odds of successful publication. In "how-to-publish in journal XYZ" workshops, informants described a sense of receiving information from the "top down": "Top scholars come, present the journal, and then leave; or they are just interested in the parties." Without long-term, developmental engagement on such occasions, these workshops could be perceived as "a waste of time" for our informants.

## BRINGING THE PERIPHERY IN: CREATING HOLDING ENVIRONMENTS TO INVITE NEW THEORY

These issues are highly structural and require committed, long-term effort. To that end, AMR editorial teams have implemented initiatives to address some of these problems [(Byron, 2024)](#page-4-0). The "From the Editors" series has provided guidelines on how to develop and position theory papers [(Thatcher & Fisher,](#page-5-0) [2022](#page-5-0)), explanations behind rejections [(Campbell &](#page-4-0) [Aguilera, 2022](#page-4-0)), guidance on options after a rejection

<!-- page 4 -->

[(Methot & Kaul, 2024](#page-5-0)), and suggestions on how to develop theory based on local phenomena and novel contexts [(Morris et al., 2023)](#page-5-0). AMR has also held in-person and virtual "idea development workshops" (IDWs) ([AOM, 2023](#page-4-0)) in periphery-based countries, as well as information sessions and panels in existing conferences. Distinct from a "paper" development workshop, IDWs are designed to introduce participants to AMR's developmental resources and publication expectations while they are still formulating their ideas, prior to constructing a paper. AMR also recently launched the "Ask an Associate Editor" series of virtual conversations [(AOM, n.d.b.](#page-4-0)), wherein any prospective AMR authors can directly present questions to members of the editorial team about publishing in AMR, or about specific special topics that the journal is considering. In addition, AMR introduced the Bridge Reviewer Program [(AOM, n.d.a.](#page-4-0)), in which participants at any career stage interested in reviewing for the journal (a stepping stone to Editorial Review Board (ERB) membership and future editorial team opportunities) can complete learning modules and be placed in a mentoring relationship with an ERB member. There seems to be enthusiastic participation across these initiatives, particularly from periphery-based scholars.

Although these initiatives may help reduce the perceived risk of developing theory papers, addressing deeper psychological obstacles to inclusion is critical. To improve inclusion, informants suggested designing supportive structures that can serve as holding environments, or interpersonal and group settings defined by active attention, inquiry, empathy, compassion, and help [(Kahn, 2001)](#page-4-0) that demonstrate a genuine commitment to including diverse perspectives through practices such as listening (e.g., [Kluger & Itzchakov, 2022;](#page-4-0)[ Yip & Fisher, 2022](#page-5-0)).

## Build Local and Long-Term Relationships Focused on Theory Development

Beyond one-shot workshops and events at conferences, there is an opportunity to craft longer-term, developmental mentorship programs in which experienced authors can provide guidance on how to develop promising theory-building ideas. A pilot program along these lines is being planned as a follow-up of the IDW in Colombia in December 2024. As one research fellow from the Caribbean noted, "Potential authors for AMR may need a bridge co-author sort of approach" that is similar to the Bridge Reviewer Program. He cited the Academy of International Business' approach of organizing through local chapters, rather than only interest

groups or divisions, where co-located prospective and published authors could form connections. A doctoral student in Latin America similarly suggested that Western journals could develop partnerships with local universities and professional associations to develop local reviewers and authors. A full professor from China, in turn, suggested that "highlighting papers by Chinese scholars that have already been published in AMR could go a long way in inspiring others—seeing examples from people in similar situations is always motivating." Another assistant professor from the same country also thought that "cultivating a few key 'activist' scholars, who actively promote theory writing and submitting to AMR, can lead to a shift in the overall perception of AMR." These sorts of spaces and connections could facilitate the longer-term, developmental relationships needed to promote the perception that "writing theory is safe," expose Western scholars to the value of different cultural norms regarding what constitutes a "contribution," and provide the role models and confidence needed to combat impostor syndrome.

## Provide Inclusive Training and Publishing Opportunities

Our informants also emphasized the need to sustain publication and training opportunities that welcomed their perspectives. For example, scholars from different countries cited the importance of articulating issues that were locally relevant to peripherybased scholars, but with broadly relevant theoretical importance. A possibility would be to assign special issues dedicated to topics that are relevant to those regions or that might invite constructive dialogue. A Chinese scholar, for example, suggested that, if AMR were to "encourage submissions on topics like crosscultural issues, non-Western perspectives, or universally relevant issues, [periphery-based scholars] might see the relevance and be more willing to try." There are forthcoming special topic forums at AMR that touch on issues highly relevant to periphery contexts—for example, inviting new theory on polarization and marginalized groups.

Apart from publication opportunities, journals could also structure a "phased" series of trainings for periphery-based scholars before the submission of their papers. For instance, they could start by participating in the Bridge Reviewer Program, then submitting a short Dialogue piece commenting on a recently accepted paper, or collaborating with the Review Essays section of AMR. Workshop and mentorship program participants can also help reframe the

perceived costs and benefits of writing a theory paper. For example, even if a paper is rejected, authors can get rich developmental feedback on how to move the ideas forward [(Methot & Kaul, 2024)](#page-5-0). Discussion forums that follow from workshops and training programs can also sustain dialogue about theory writing across a scholar's career. Topics could include the pros and cons of crafting a theory paper earlier or later in your career. The visibility of these opportunities to publish, train, and learn about the scholarly career could help periphery-based scholars feel more confident about their legitimacy and capabilities—thereby addressing issues of cultural differences around language, and the sense of imposing standards.

## Interact Respectfully

Mentoring, publishing, and training opportunities all ultimately involve human-to-human interactions. For those interactions to be successful and inclusive, they need to be respectful. As a professor from Latin America noted, proposing special issues focused on unique theoretical insights—beyond the issues commonly discussed in traditional centers—needs to involve editors and reviewers who truly appreciate diverse points of view. Moreover, editors and reviewers need to be more receptive to submissions that do not fit established standards yet are culturally informed, and creative ways to write and position papers. Editorial pieces providing guidance on how to write theory papers should be more sensitive to the various ways in which new theory can be proposed, positioned, and written. For instance, theorization does not need to be only predictive or explanatory; it can also be "emancipatory," whereby scholars challenge existing beliefs and systems (Cornelissen, Hollerer & Seidl, 2021). Conversations and training € sessions to avoid biases in the review process are already in discussion (Byron, 2024), but baking respect and appreciation into our exchanges is also a necessary start for welcoming diverse voices. This would be foundational to addressing all three of the challenges outlined above; namely, helping periphery-based scholars feel that "writing theory papers is safe," that their particular cultural perspective is valued, and that their voices deserve to be heard.

There are, no doubt, other necessary actions that could potentially prompt changes in the organizational context in which periphery-based scholars are embedded. A journal like AMR cannot influence existing practices and incentives at institutions that constrain scholars' ability to allocate time and effort to novel and impactful research. Yet, as evidenced by our informants' suggestions and our own experiences,

much can be done to include new voices and perhaps use their theorization efforts to unchain their ability to change the prevailing system. Attracting and engaging periphery-based scholars also requires a change in the mindset of Western scholars in leading positions and a genuine engagement to embrace diversity. A crucial step, as we argued and tried to implement in this piece, is to let those voices be heard as they are. Let us welcome them and learn from their fresh and exciting theoretical perspectives on management and organizations.
