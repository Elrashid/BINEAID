## A B S T R A C T

Big Data Stream processing engines, exemplified by tools like Apache Flink, employ windowing techniques to manage unbounded streams of events. Aggregating relevant data within Windows is important for event-time windowing due to its impact on result accuracy. A pivotal role in this process is attributed to watermarks, unique timestamps signifying event progression in time. Nonetheless, the existing watermark generation method within Apache Flink, operating at the input stream level, exhibits a bias towards faster sub-streams, causing the omission of events from slower counterparts. Our analysis determined that Apache Flink's standard watermark generation approach results in an approximate 33% data loss when 50% of median-proximate keys experience delays. Furthermore, this loss exceeds 37% in cases where 50% of randomly selected keys encounter delays. In this paper, we introduce a pioneering approach termed *keyed* *watermarks* to address data loss concerns and enhance data processing precision to a minimum of 99% in most scenarios. Our strategy facilitates distinct progress monitoring by creating individualized watermarks for each sub-stream (key). Within our investigation, we delineate the essential architectural and API modifications requisite for integrating keyed watermarks while also highlighting our experience in navigating the expansion of Apache Flink's extensive codebase. Moreover, we conduct a comparative evaluation between the efficacy of our approach and the conventional watermark generation technique concerning the accuracy of event-time tracking, the latency of watermark processing, and the growth of Flink's maintained state.

## **1.** **Introduction**

The exponential growth of Internet of Things (IoT) interconnected devices, exceeding a count of 70 billion, introduces an array of formidable challenges and substantial considerations within the domain of data stream processing and computational infrastructure [[1]](#page-11-0). In response, we witnessed the emergence of several distributed stream processing systems, often referred to as stream processing engines (SPE), owing to the escalating complexities associated with the rapid processing of extensive data volumes. Illustrative instances of these systems encompass Apache Spark Streaming [[2]](#page-11-1), Apache Flink [[3]](#page-11-2), and Apache Samza [[4](#page-11-3)], to name but a selection. These systems offer scalable and distributed processing capabilities for data streams, demonstrating fault-tolerance and operating in a near real-time fashion aligned with the data's generation speed.

Given the virtually boundless nature of data streams, SPEs must partition them into finite segments. Within this context, the system continuously generates updates on the outcomes associated with this segments [[5](#page-11-4)]. Chunking the stream takes place by applying so-called time-windowing [[6,](#page-11-5)[7]](#page-11-6) on the data stream. As an example of stream

processing applications, the computation of average temperature values provided by deployed sensors situated within designated geographic regions. To accomplish this objective, SPEs continuously update their computation by slicing the data into, e.g., five minutes window of temperature readings and computing a new average on the window content. The slicing of arriving temperature events is based on socalled *event* *time*. That is by tracking a timestamp attribute in the event payload.

Following an event-time based processing model [[8]](#page-11-7), SPEs keeps buffering the arriving data until it gets a degree of certainty that no further arriving stream events will belong to the current window. That is, the SPE believes that the event-time clock has progressed beyond the window end. The common approach to track the event-time progress is so-called watermarks [[8](#page-11-7)[,9]](#page-11-8). Newly arriving records with a timestamp earlier than the current watermark are considered late and are basically ignored by SPEs.

Upon receiving a watermark, the process involves concluding all windows that terminate prior to the watermark and employing their contents for updating analytical results. The advancement of watermarks has a dual impact on stream processing metrics: latency and the tardiness of data item arrivals, ultimately influencing accuracy. The balance between watermark generation frequency and accuracy manifests in a trade-off scenario. Inadequate watermark generation elevates latency, while excess watermark generation leads to the tardy arrival of data items. The computation of watermark progress is essentially a heuristic approach that depends on domain knowledge. In general, the application developer chooses a fixed delay time which she believes that data will never arrive later to. Each arriving record's (event) timestamp is compared to the current watermark. If it is greater than or equal to the watermark, the event is added to the buffer and its timestamp is used to update the watermark value, by choosing the maximum between them. Otherwise, the record is considered late and dropped from further processing. Some SPEs provide more sophisticated watermarking policies. However, they are variations of the approach described above. The majority of open-source stream processing engines (SPEs) offer support for watermarks and equip application developers with a range of policies to regulate their advancement. However, the progress of watermarks exerts its influence on the stream at a more nuanced level. In instances such as that of a temperature stream, numerous physical sensors contribute their readings to a common SPE through a unified logical stream. Consequently, the subsequent watermark value (timestamp) holds sway over the data emanating from all these individual physical streams. Here, the overall temperature stream is the logical stream, whereas the temperature readings from each sensor, distinguished by an ID a.k.a key, is considered the physical sub-stream. Consequently, measurements from sensors with slower reporting rates are discarded due to their delayed arrival. Therefore, slower physical sub-streams are punished and analytical results based thereon are flawed.

This paper presents a novel approach for tracking and generating watermarks at a finer level of granularity, referred to as *keyed* *watermarks*. This method involves creating and monitoring distinct watermarks for each distinct physical sub-stream (See the bottom part of [Fig.](#page-1-0) [1](#page-1-0)). Our proposed approach is realized in Apache Flink, a leading-edge platform in the realm of distributed stream processing. - As navigating and understanding the huge codebase of Apache Flink took a considerable amount of time in this work due to lack of documentation [[10](#page-11-9)], we report about the technical contribution of extending Flink's to realize keyed watermarks. ## **2.** **Background**

Data stream processing [[11](#page-11-10)[–13](#page-11-11)] is about analyzing data as soon as they are generated. Therefore, it follows a continuous form of query processing [[5](#page-11-4)]. A query can be as simple as computing aggregates, all the way to training an online machine learning model. A key challenge is the virtually unbounded nature of the stream. The results of the query is continuously updated as new data arrive. The objective is to get fresh results with the least delay (Section [2.1](#page-2-0)). As we live in the big data era, we need to maximize the number of processed records per unit time (Section [2.2](#page-2-1)). Big data systems achieve low latency and high throughput by utilizing a distributed processing architecture where the data is partitioned into small chunks that are processed in parallel.

<!-- page 3 -->

In data stream processing, partitioning is two-fold. First, the logical stream, e.g., the temperature stream from Section [1,](#page-0-4) is split into a number of physical streams using the sensor identifier (key). Second, the virtually unbounded physical stream is further subdivided into finite subsets of the stream using windowing [2.3.](#page-2-2) Usually, windowing is temporal. The chosen notions of time (Section [2.4](#page-2-3)) used to split the data into windows has implications on the accuracy and reproducibility of results.

As Flink will serve as the SPE to realize and implement our approach, Section [2.5](#page-3-0) will give an overview of Flink's pipelines for data stream processing.

## *2.1.* *Latency*

Latency [[14]](#page-11-12) is a key performance metric in data stream processing engines. It measures the time taken for each event to be processed. SPEs compete to offer lower latency as this directly affects the speed of getting updates of the continuous queries. Latency could be measured at a macro or micro level. That is, the end-to-end latency, or latency at a specific operator in the processing pipeline, e.g., latency in the windowing and aggregation operators. Latency is negatively proportional with the scalability of the operators. That is, using operator fission (replication) [[15](#page-11-13)], the load can be distributed among more operator instances therefore reducing the share of an individual instance and reducing the overall latency [[16]](#page-11-14). In this paper, we are interested in measuring the latency of the watermarking operator. As our approach, as will be discussed in Section [3,](#page-4-0) will apply operator fission to the watermarking operator, we expect a positive effect on reducing latency.

## *2.2.* *Throughput*

Throughput [[17](#page-11-15)] is another performance metric for data processing systems. It is a legacy from offline data processing systems. However, it is still relevant for SPEs performance evaluation. Throughput measures the processed records in unit time. The higher the throughput the better. However, in SPEs, low throughput does not always mean that the system's performance is bad, because it depends on the arrival rate of events which depends on the source generation rate. Similar to latency, in this paper, we want to study the effect of the watermarking operator fission on throughput.

## *2.3.* *Windowing*

As the stream processing engines work with boundless data streams, in order to produce transformations from these streams, the system processes the unbounded incoming streams as bounded defined intervals called *windows* [[18](#page-11-16)]. A window [[19](#page-11-17)[,20](#page-11-18)] is a defined either time-based or count-based interval, that allows grouping events in a defined finite bucket. Windows enables computations of bounded data streams over boundless data streams.

Time-based windows are the most common in stream processing [[18](#page-11-16)]. They can be either of fixed or variable width and overlapping or disjoint content. In the following, we cover the most common time-based window operators, a.k.a assigner in the context of Apache Flink.

## *2.3.1.* *Tumbling* *windows*

The tumbling window [[21]](#page-11-19) is a fixed, non-overlapping, time or count window. The tumbling window operator chunks the stream into fixed-size windows. The size of each chunk is an operator parameter that is specified at application definition time. [Fig.](#page-2-4) [2](#page-2-4) [1](#page-2-5) visualizes how tumbling windows chunk the stream.

The sliding window [[23]](#page-11-21) is a fixed, overlapping, time, or count window. The sliding window operator chunks the stream following two parameters, the window size, same as the tumbling window, and the window slide. If the window slide is equal to the window width, the sliding window reduces to a tumbling window. As usually the window slide is less than the window width, the windows overlap. That is, the same event can belong to one ore more windows. [Fig.](#page-2-6) [3](#page-2-6) visualizes how sliding windows chunk the stream.

## *2.4.* *Notions* *of* *time*

In the previous section, we have discussed basic forms of *windowing* the stream based on time. However, there are different notions of time [[25]](#page-11-23). Processing and event time are the common notions of time ([Fig.](#page-3-1) [5)](#page-3-1).

In processing time [[26](#page-11-24)] mode, the SPE uses its internal clock to track the time progress and to decide opening and closing of temporal windows. In this mode, the SPE does not require a timestamp in the arriving data or simply ignores it. This mode is easier to implement.

## *3.1.* *Watermarking*

In order to allow low-latency processing in stream processing engines the system may have late events that arrive out-of-order which will affect the windows so that they will be kept open until receiving the late events. A technique found in most stream processing engines to solve this issue and allow the window to be closed in order to emit its results is a watermark [[9,](#page-11-8)[34](#page-11-32)[,35](#page-11-33)]. A watermark is a timestamp injected into the stream of events ([Fig.](#page-4-1) [8](#page-4-1)). When the watermark is observed by a stateful operator in the pipeline, e.g., a window operator, it knows that there should be no more events with timestamps less than the watermark are expected to arrive in the future. Therefore, the operator can, e.g., close all open windows whose end timestamp are less than the watermark and deliver the window content for further processing, e.g., computing an average.

A watermark is monotonic. That is, it is a non-decreasing value of the timestamp. This can be achieved by taking the maximum of the current watermarks' value and the timestamp of the newly arriving record on the stream. Periodically, a new watermark is emitted on the stream to be observed and processed by the rest of the operators in the pipeline.

## *3.3.* *Scaling* *watermarking* *(keyed* *watermarks)*

As discussed in Section [1](#page-0-4), the conventional watermark generation architecture detailed in the previous section has several drawbacks. First, as SPES, e.g. Flink, maintains a singleton operator instance, this goes against the scalable nature of such big data systems. Second, as the watermark value is maintained on the logical stream level, slower physical streams are punished by design as their data will tend to be dropped by the SPE as the records arriving from other faster physical streams will advance the watermark at a higher pace. Last but not least, failure and recovery in the conventional approach is slower and more costly. In case the node hosting the watermark operator fails, all downstream operators will stall, waiting for the watermark operator's recovery.

Our contribution of *keyed* *watermarks*[2](#page-5-1) solves the above-mentioned limitations. However, we allow the application developer to configure watermark generation using either the conventional (logical) or the keyed approach (physical). Within the context of Flink, our contribution is a fundamental change in the APIs.

With keyed watermarks, the event time progress of the different physical streams is tracked independently. Therefore, fewer records will be dropped from the slower physical streams. Our experimental evaluation in Section [4](#page-6-0) empirically confirms our claim. To track keyed watermarks, we allow the scaling of the watermark operator. That is, deploy several operator instances. As a result, the latency of watermark processing drops as all operator instances are working simultaneously, a.k.a data parallelism. Our experiments empirically confirm this claim as well. Finally, the recovery from failure should be faster as only downstream operators to the failing watermark operator instance will be stalled. All other operators should continue working normally. [Fig.](#page-5-2) [11](#page-5-2) visualizes the proposed keyed watermarks architecture.

## **4.** **Experimental** **evaluation**

of watermark processing?

We have implemented our approach on a fork of Apache Flink. To evaluate the value of keyed watermarks generation over the vanilla (granular) watermark generation, we conducted several experiments to answer the following research questions:

- **RQ1**: Do keyed watermarks reduce the number of dropped tuples?
- **RQ2**: How does keyed watermarks generation affect the latency
- **RQ3**: How does keyed watermarks generation affect the growth of the maintained state size?

**RQ1** evaluates if we fulfilled the ultimate objective of minimizing the number of dropped records caused by out-of-order processing. A direct effect thereof is to increase the accuracy of whatever computation is done on these elements, e.g., windowing them and computing an aggregate. It is expected that the keyed watermark tracking will generate a much higher number of watermarks. **RQ2** is concerned with measuring the effect of processing this larger number of watermarks on their processing latency, whereas **RQ3** is concerned with the potential increase in state size as the engine is expected to retain more open windows compared to the granular watermark tracking.

## *4.1.* *Experimental* *setup*

To answer the above-mentioned research question, we have developed a simple pipeline where we read from a streaming source and count the number of elements in a 10 s window. We run the pipeline for the original Flink watermark tracking and our keyed watermarks.

We have run our experiments on a Google Cloud Platform virtual machine with 8 CPU Intel Cascade Lake cores x86/64-bit architecture with 32 GB of main memory running Ubuntu 22.10 and disk size of 100 GB. We used Docker to build an Apache Flink cluster. We created a docker image from the Flink build modified by adding keyed watermarks, and from that image, we created three containers. One serves as the job manager, with 4 GB memory and one CPU core, and the other two as task managers with 12 GB and four CPUs each.

We used the dataset of the linear road benchmark.[3](#page-6-2) Linear Road is a simulation of a large metropolitan city that is 100 miles wide and

long and consists of 10 parallel expressways. Each tuple in the data set describes a car ID, speed, road, direction, and the record's timestamp. The data set contains around 6 records. For our experiments, we project only the car ID (as the data source key), the record's timestamp, and the speed. The data set contains over

Linear road data is ordered. To introduce out-of-order data, we prepared six variants of the data set. In the first and second variants, we randomly picked 20% and 50% of the unique car IDs and deliberately delayed their timestamps by 10 seconds. In the third and fourth variants, we introduced a global delay of 10 seconds within 5% and 50% of the records, respectively. In the fifth and sixth variants, we sorted the data by the number of elements present for each key and then selected 20% and 50% of the keys around the median and deliberately delayed their timestamps by 10 seconds to introduce the delay.

We also used the DEBS 2013 challenge [[36](#page-11-34)], which contains an unknown form of out-of-order. The dataset contains about 50 records. We also projected only the key, the timestamp, and the value of the event's payload.

We run the experiments using different parallelism configurations for the watermark and the window operators. We use 2*,* 4*, * 8 degrees of parallelism. Our GitHub repository[4](#page-6-3) contains all the modified and extended Apache Flink code along with experimental pipelines.

## *4.2.* *Results*

## *4.2.1.* *RQ1:* *Do* *keyed* *watermarks* *reduce* *the* *number* *of* *dropped* *tuples?*

As a starting point, we used the ordered linear road dataset to check that vanilla and keyed watermarks compute the same number of windows with the same number of elements. Windows computed following the two approaches have processed 6*,* 178*,* 736 elements with an accuracy of ∼ 99*.*99%. The result shows that on ordered data, the keyed watermark operator acts seamlessly, similar to the vanilla watermark operator. We use these results as the baseline, when we start processing the different unordered data sets.

[Table](#page-7-0) [2](#page-7-0) summarizes the accuracy results for the out-of-order arrival. Accuracy is computed as the ratio of the total number of records captured within generated windows to the total number of records in the data set.

For the unordered data sets, the keyed watermark operator drops fewer late records in almost all cases. An exception is the 5% global delay data set. The difference in accuracy in this dataset is negligible compared to the vanilla case. Analyzing the data, we found that the delay is spread amongst all keys in an almost uniform pattern. Therefore, the keyed watermark generation is affected by this uniform out-of-order arrival.

Another interesting observation is the effect of parallelism on accuracy. For the unordered data, Flink's handling of windows in response to watermarks is generally negatively correlated with the degree of parallelism, whereas keyed watermarks handling of windows gains from the increase of parallelism. This is because Flink deploys only one watermark operator per source. On the other hand, keyed watermarks can deploy as many watermark operators as the degree of parallelism. However, both techniques allocate as many window operators as the degree of parallelism. In Flink's case, the same watermark is communicated to all downstream window operators. Therefore, at the end of the stream processing, more windows per process (thread) are left open, waiting for the next watermark. In the keyed watermark case, Flink allocates the watermark operator and the window operator to the same process, so there is almost no inter-process watermark

## *4.2.2.* *RQ2:* *How* *does* *keyed* *watermarks* *generation* *affect* *the* *latency* *of* *watermark* *processing?*

Turning to **RQ2**, the vanilla watermark operator generates fewer watermarks than the keyed operator. Flink's default behavior is to check for a new watermark value every 200 milliseconds. Assuming 10 unique keys per source arriving continuously, the keyed watermark operator will generate 10 watermarks every 200 milliseconds compared to one watermark only from the vanilla watermark operator. On the other hand, upon receiving a watermark, the vanilla window operator is expected to process, and not necessarily close, a larger number of window instances than a window operator in the keyed watermark case. Against this observation, we conducted our second set of experiments to track the latency in processing watermarks. We compute the processing time latency [[37]](#page-11-35). We compute the difference between the timestamp when a watermark was generated at the respective watermark operator and the timestamp when the receiving window operator processed the last window in response to receiving the watermark.

[Table](#page-8-1) [3](#page-8-1) contains the latency in seconds for all datasets with parallelisms 2, 4, and 8. We calculated the results by logging the timestamp when the watermark was generated and the timestamp of the last use of the watermark. In the vanilla Flink case, we log the watermark value and timestamp. We log the watermark value, key, and timestamp for the keyed watermark. Therefore, we distinguish between

the minimum and maximum for the keyed watermarks because the same watermark value might show for different keys. Finally, we calculated the *., ., *., and (90*,* 95*,* 99) latency percentiles for Flink by grouping the results by watermark value, and for Keyed watermarks, we grouped results by watermark and key and produced the . and . latency found for each watermark. As shown in [Table](#page-8-1) [3](#page-8-1), keyed watermark processing imposes less overhead. That is less latency. Moreover, the latency reduces as the parallelism increases. Compared to the vanilla case, the latency percentile improves by over 50% in most of the cases. In very few cases, the latency of the keyed watermark processing, the maximum case, is a little over the vanilla case. Nevertheless, KMINWM is always below the vanilla case. This means that the pipeline will start producing results earlier than the pipeline for the vanilla case.

## *4.2.3.* *RQ3:* *How* *does* *keyed* *watermarks* *generation* *affect* *the* *growth* *of* *the* *maintained* *state* *size?*

Turning to **RQ3**, similar to **RQ2**, the vanilla watermark operator keeps the elements of the same window in a state. i.e., in memory, and when the event time passes the window's end timestamp, the window operator cleans up the window state. Keyed watermarks are supposed to keep more elements in the state because we have more than one watermark for unique keys. Based on this observation, we conducted the third set of experiments to track the window operator state size across timestamps during the pipeline run-time. At the arrival of each new element, we store the number of elements kept in the state and send this value to the side output which is written to a file. Finally, we compute the difference between the size of the state in the Flink watermark and the Keyed Watermarks. Our speculation is that keyed

watermarks is, in general, going to maintain a larger state as more windows will be kept open. The plots in [14](#page-10-1) generally confirm our speculation. For the ordered dataset, [Fig.](#page-10-2) [14(a)](#page-10-2), we can see a horizontal line at 0, indicating that both approaches maintain the same state size. For the datasets with delays for specific keys, we can observe that keyed watermarks maintains a larger state, [Figs.](#page-10-3) [14(b)](#page-10-3), [14(c)](#page-10-3), [14(d)](#page-10-3), [14(e)](#page-10-3). Obviously, the state size increases as the percentage of out-oforder increases. Nevertheless, the largest state size maintained by keyed watermarks is around 70K tuples more than the vanilla watermark, which, overall, is not a significant overhead. We observe a different state size growth for the datasets with global delay, [Figs.](#page-10-3) [14(f)](#page-10-3) and [14(g).](#page-10-3) In some cases, the pipeline for the vanilla watermark approach maintains a larger state than that for the keyed watermarks. To answer **RQ3**, we can conclude that keyed watermark processing imposes a larger state overhead compared to the vanilla approach. This is expected, as we discussed earlier. However, the overhead in most of the cases is not significant. It is interesting to study the effect of out-oforder distribution within and across keys on the growth of state size. However, this is a subject for future research.

## **5.** **Related** **work**

Handling late and out-of-order data on a data stream caught the attention of researchers a long time ago. Generally, there are three basic techniques to account for late data: punctuations [[33]](#page-11-31), slacktime [[38]](#page-11-36), a.k.a buffering, monotonic watermarks [[8](#page-11-7),[39–](#page-11-37)[41]](#page-11-38), orderagnostic processing [[42]](#page-11-39), ordered processing [[43](#page-11-40)[–46](#page-12-4)], [[47]](#page-12-5), timestamp frontiers [[48](#page-12-6)].

Punctuations can reason completeness by assuming that no more events fulfilling a given condition or predicate will come in the future using the punctuation technique. Punctuations, on the other hand, can combine the usage of watermarks and timestamp borders with other functions such as establishing constraints. The SPE can reason about completeness by observing the existence or absence of conditions, markers, and so forth. Punctuations cannot be generalized as they depend on the semantics of the data.

Buffering is rigid. An inaccurate guess of the buffering time can either introduce unnecessary delays, when the buffer is large, or severely affects accuracy when the buffer is small.

Monotonic watermarks are employed to address the issue of outof-orderness in the events received by the SPE. The SPE can use watermarks to ensure that no more events with timestamps older than the watermark's timestamp occur in the future. So that the SPE can broadcast the contents of the window for further processing.

Order-agnostic processing the SPE can handle the events without regard for their order using this strategy. This is an important capability for reasoning about the completeness, because determining that all relevant events are processed, this technique could be achieved using several methods such as buffering, event-time stamping, and windowing, so the SPE should collect and arrange the events before processing ordering the events according to their timestamps then grouping them into a specific window. As a result, in the case of outof-order occurrences, the SPE can process the events and reason about completeness.

Ordered processing the SPE assumes that the events are ordered in this manner, ensuring completeness and excellent accuracy of the results because no unprocessed prior events are expected.

The timestamp frontiers concept is derived from the Timely Dataflow model, which employs a multidimensional and flexible approach to completion reasoning. It goes beyond watermarks to allow the independent usage of different time conceptions. As a result, each temporal dimension can advance at its own pace, allowing for more coordination and control over incoming events.

Nevertheless, the keyed approach introduced in this paper can be applied to both punctuations and buffering, virtually increasing accuracy.

## **6.** **Conclusion**

In this paper, we propose a novel method called ''keyed watermarks'', which enhances the accuracy of data processing in Big Data Stream systems. By introducing distinct watermarks for each logical sub-stream, we enable separate progress tracking for various data sources while mitigating the data loss problem seen in Apache Flink's default watermark generation method. Our analysis shows that when 50% of the keys around the median are delayed, the standard approach caused a significant data loss of around 33%, which increased to more than 37% when 50% of random keys are delayed. Our technique is able to reduce data loss to less than 1%. To implement keyed watermarks, we outline the necessary architectural and API changes and discuss our experience extending Apache Flink's large code base. Future research should concentrate on assessing the scalability and performance of keyed watermarks across diverse datasets, as well as their effectiveness

in handling larger volumes of data. Also, exploring adaptive techniques for dynamically adjusting watermark generation interval based on the unique characteristics of each sub-stream has the potential to improve the accuracy and efficiency of event-time processing in Big Data Stream systems even further.
