## **Abstract**

Debate continues on the effects of the global proliferation of private higher-educational institutions, especially forprofit institutions. We examine two related questions for Peru using mixed methods: Who attends private institutions and what are their perceived advantages/disadvantages? Longitudinal quantitative data suggest higher-educational segmentation starting early in life, whereby young people from wealthier households attended private institutions and those from poorer households attended public ones. Interviews with teachers, students, and staff provide insights on perceived strengths and drawbacks of higher-educational marketization and highlight the importance of family background in higher-educational choices, governmental regulation, and close monitoring of highereducational quality.

## **1 **|** INTRODUCTION**

In recent decades, higher-educational access has increased worldwide (Schofer & Meyer, [2005](#page-19-0)), including in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) (Ferreyra et al., [2017)](#page-18-0). Pressure has increased to 'produce' competitive individuals (Pang, [2016)](#page-18-1) and market practices and higher-educational privatization have become more common. This is commonly referred to as educational 'marketization', the adoption of market-oriented practices in running schools, e.g., deregulation, competition, stratification, cost cutting, abandoning offerings not in demand, producing only popular products, and advertising to increase sales and profits (Bartlett et al., [2002](#page-17-0); Kwong, [2000](#page-18-2)).

Competition between private and public institutions could induce more productive efficiency (Friedman, [1962](#page-18-3); Levin, [2002](#page-18-4)). But the evidence is mixed (Hsieh & Urquiola, [2006](#page-18-5); Joshi, [2020](#page-18-6); Thapa, [2013)](#page-19-1). Inequality may be exacerbated. Although some claim that higher-educational institutions have social responsibility to provide education to historically marginalized groups (Vessuri, [2008](#page-19-2)), the more education is marketized, the bigger the inequality issue (Aponte-Hernandez, [2008](#page-17-1)). Furthermore, a recent World Bank study highlights the need for stronger regulation of private institutions (Ferreyra et al., [2017](#page-18-0)).

Peru is of great relevance for studies of educational marketization for two reasons. First, in 1996, Legislative Law 882 promoted the liberalization of the educational market, including higher education, by allowing profits in educational services, which in turn substantially increased the number of private institutions. The proportion of higher-education students attending private institutions (universities or technical institutes) in 2020 was 67.3% (Ministry of Education, [2020](#page-18-7)). Second, Peruvian educational access is very unequal, especially for higher education as individuals with more-educated parents (Guerrero, [2013)](#page-18-8), from the wealth's top tercile, and with a better performance in cognitive assessment tests during adolescence (Sánchez & Abhijeet, [2018](#page-19-3)) are more likely to access higher education. By 2019, 22.3% of the non-poor aged 22–24 years old had concluded higher education, compared to 2.1% (9.4%) amongst the extreme (non-extreme) poor (Ministry of Education, [2022](#page-18-9)). Third, studies that explore the returns to higher education in the labour market show a higher premium for graduates from higher-quality institutions (Lavado et al., [2015;](#page-18-10) Sanchez et al., [2021](#page-19-4)).

Therefore, the Peruvian government needs to give attention to higher education and the market dynamics with economic and equity-related implications. This study examines two important related research questions:

RQ1. What are the key predictors of attending a private/public higher-educational institution at age 19? RQ2. What are the perceived advantages/disadvantages of higher-education marketization?

<!-- page 3 -->

The first question is investigated quantitatively with Young Live[s1](#page-17-2) data, an international longitudinal study that covers 15 years of respondents' lives. We are not able to tackle the advantages/disadvantages of marketization explicitly in the quantitative analysis due to data limitations. Therefore, we explore these dimensions in the second question through semi-structured qualitative interviews with students, teachers and staff of higher-educational institutions.

Although previous studies analyse the predictors of Peruvian higher-educational enrollment (Guerrero, [2013](#page-18-8); Sánchez & Abhijeet, [2018](#page-19-3)), to our knowledge, this is the first study that uses a mixed-methods approach to explore the dimensions of Peruvian higher-educational marketization that are addressed in our research questions. Thereby it aims to explore the characteristics, implications and complexities of the marketization of higher education in Peru. Using quantitative data from Young Lives, we explore key factors that predict the type of attending a higher-educational institution. And, using a qualitative approach we obtain perspectives from key stakeholders on the perceived benefits and drawbacks of higher-education marketization in Peru. The following sections provide a literature review, information on Peruvian higher education, details on the data and methods used, results and a summary and discussion.

## **2 **|** LITERATURE REVIEW**

The literature provides arguments for both advantages and disadvantages of higher-educational marketization. Some argue that market-oriented approaches lead to educational decentralization, providing access where public systems are failing (Oketch et al., [2010](#page-18-11)), increasing efficiency and quality (Heyneman & Stern, [2014](#page-18-12); Kingdon, [2020](#page-18-13)), reducing costs (Xiong, [2012](#page-19-5)), and improving faculty retention with competitive market salaries (Lee, [2000](#page-18-14)).

Privatization and marketization approaches are criticized for overstating the learning gains (Klees, [2008](#page-18-15)), widening educational opportunities gaps (Carnoy, [2002](#page-17-3); Natale & Duran, [2012](#page-18-16); Srivastava & Noronha, [2016](#page-19-6)), and undermining the idea of 'education as learning' by focusing on preparing students for labour markets (Natale & Duran, [2012](#page-18-16)).

*Evidence from Low and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs)* is mostly focused on primary and secondary education, and possibly increasing educational inequality (Gill, [2017](#page-18-17); Hsieh & Urquiola, [2006](#page-18-5)). But marketization has expanded throughout educational systems and is becoming increasingly common in higher education given its flexibility and faster adaptation to changes. Such developments are rampant in Asian countries (e.g. Pang, [2016](#page-18-1) for Vietnam and Ahmed, [2016 ](#page-17-4)for Bangladesh) and African countries such as Tanzania and Kenya including costsharing policies for higher education (Provini, [2019)](#page-19-7). Similar trends of expanding coverage of higher-educational institutions, particularly growth in private universities, occurred throughout LAC during the late twentieth century (Ferreyra et al., [2017](#page-18-0); Garcia, [2004](#page-18-18)). In most of LAC (except for Cuba and Venezuela) there are more private than public institutions (Ferreyra et al., [2017)](#page-18-0). Evidence from Chile and Peru (Hastings et al., [2013](#page-18-19); Yamada & Oviedo, [2017](#page-19-8)) suggests that higher-education students that attended newer private institutions have lower labor market returns. This may reinforce the need for stronger regulation, a point also made by World Bank economists (Ferreyra et al., [2017](#page-18-0)).

## **3 **|** PERUVIAN HIGHER EDUCATION**

The Peruvian higher-educational system consists of universities and technical institutes. There has been a substantial increase in gross enrolment: from 38% in 2001 to 67% in 2015 according to official statistics for the population aged 17–21 years old, though completion rates are considerably lower (22.3% in 2019 for those aged 22–24). Historical statistics are scarce, especially for technical institutes, but according to Diaz ([2008](#page-17-5)), by 1970

<!-- page 4 -->

approximately 7% of the population aged 17–24 years old was enrolled in higher education, in a market then dominated by public universities, with 21 public universities located in different regions of the country and 10 private non-profit universities located on the coast, mainly in the capital, Lima. Since then: (i) higher education enrolment increased, (ii) technical institutes became more relevant (share of students in technical institutes increased from 17% to 40% between 1970 and 2005) and (iii) the proportion of university students enrolled in private universities increased (Diaz, [2008](#page-17-5); SUNEDU, [2019](#page-19-9)).

Higher-educational marketization deepened in 1996 with Law 882, which allowed profits (previously only public and private non-profit institutions were allowed) and explains the massive increases in private universities (including 50 for-profit universities) and technical institutes. Regulations and control mechanisms were very lax, creating disorganized and heterogeneous growth (Diaz, [2008](#page-17-5)). There are more technical institutes than universities in the country (1094 technical institutes compared to 143 licensed universities); however, enrolment in higher education is still dominated by universities: By 2019, 73.9% of students were enrolled in universities, versus 26.1% in technical institutes (Ministry of Education, [2020](#page-18-7)). In both cases, enrolment is dominated by private institutions. Enrolment in universities is dominated by private for-profit (39%) followed by private non-profit (34%) and public universities (27%), reversing what was observed in the 1970s (adapted from SUNEDU, [2019)](#page-19-9). On average, public universities remain the most selective in the ratio of admitted students to applicants (Diaz, [2008](#page-17-5); SUNEDU, [2019](#page-19-9)).

Following Law 882, the market is segmented into the three types of higher-educational institutions currently observed: public (free tuition, small fees, and mainly funded by the government), private non-profit (tuition and fees vary by institution; revenues have to be reinvested), and private for-profit (tuition and fees vary by institution; profits are allowed and do not have to be reinvested). Each institution has its own admission procedure, typically entry exams. Despite the existence of public institutions, according to official statistics, access to higher education remains elusive for those coming from poor families (gross enrolment for the population aged 17–21 in 2019 is 81% for non-poor, compared to 27% (16%) for non-extreme (extreme) poor). This is explained by the role of shortterm and long-term financial constraints (Castro et al., [2016](#page-17-6)); the former relates to the cost of fees (even if they are small), food, transportation, rent, etc., whilst the latter refers to the impact of financial constraints on earlier investments made by the family, such as school quality. It appears the arrival of for-profit universities increased access to higher education mainly amongst the non-poor. For those without financial means, private institutions have their own scheme of scholarships and loans for students, whereas the Peruvian government has different programs that provide students (mainly from public universities) with scholarships and low-interest rate loans, however, the coverage of these schemes is very small: in 2020 the number of loans and scholarships provided by the government was 2554 and 40,815, respectively (SUNEDU, [2021](#page-19-10)).

Law 882 had important consequences. First, admissions selectivity declined in universities (Castro & Yamada, [2013)](#page-17-7). Diaz ([2008](#page-17-5)) suggests this decline is driven by a combination of increased demand for higher education and decreased admission selectivity in private universities. Second, there have been shifts from full-time to part-time professors, leading to reduced research activities and undermining educational content (Castro & Yamada, [2013](#page-17-7)).

Therefore, there is concern about Peruvian higher-educational quality. Decreases in admission standards in universities led to suspicions that students from the new universities are not prepared for the academic rigour of universities (Castro & Yamada, [2012)](#page-17-8). Furthermore, from the passage of Law 882 in 1996 to 2010, the probabilities of being underemployed for graduates from 'low-quality' universities increased from 19% to 30% (Lavado et al., [2014)](#page-18-20). In fact, despite the increased number of higher-educational institutions, employers face difficulties finding employees who have the necessary competencies to perform their duties (OECD, [2016](#page-18-21)). This reinforces the importance of analysing Peruvian higher-educational choices, students' characteristics and how recent highereducational marketization is perceived.

There is an on-going effort to alleviate the potential negative consequences of marketization. In 2014, Law 302020 replaced Law 882 and created the Superintendencia Nacional de Educación Superior Universitaria (SUNEDU) to regulate university educational quality—this Law was supported by the National Constitutional

<!-- page 5 -->

Court stating in 2016 that both public and private universities provide public services, which means that the government regulates the service provision of all universities to guarantee their quality.

In 2021, SUNEDU finished the accreditation process of all universities. As of 2022, 95 of the 143 Peruvian universities/graduate schools were licensed, and 48 had been denied licences and, thus, must close. Of the 51 public universities in Peru, only two were denied licences; amongst the 92 private universities in Peru, 50% were denied (65% amongst for-profit, 35% amongst non-profit). Higher education includes technical institutes as well, which face similar marketization challenges. The 2016 Law 30512 regulates the educational quality of technical institutes; however, there is no specialized institution such as SUNEDU. Instead, technical institutes are regulated by the Ministry of Education, which started to give licences in 2018.

## **4 **|** DATA AND METHODS**

## **4.1 **|** Quantitative analysis**

### 4.1.1 | Data

We first analyse data from the Young Lives Study (YLS). YLS collects comparable longitudinal data in Ethiopia, India, Peru and Vietnam for two cohorts of children: the younger cohort (YC), born in 2000–2001, and the older cohort (OC), born in 1994–1995. Currently, there are five rounds of in-person data collection in 2002, 2006, 2009, 2013, and 2016—which we refer to as R1, R2, etc. YLS collects data inter alia on children's education and family background. We use the Peruvian older cohort to explore the correlates of their higher-educational enrolment. OC members were approximately 8, 12, 15, 19 and 22 years old in the respective rounds.

### 4.1.2 | Sample

In Peru, YLS sampled 714 children for the OC in R1 (2002). The households were selected from 20 randomly selected clusters. The study over-sampled the poor by excluding the richest 5% of districts (Escobal & Flores, [2008](#page-17-9)). OC attrition by R5 is 14.1%. There is evidence of selection on observed variables for this sample (males, rural residents and the bottom wealth tercile more likely attrited) (Sánchez & Escobal, [2020](#page-19-11)). Whilst some selection on observed variables is not surprising after 15 years, this does not necessarily mean that estimates of the nonattrited sample are biased. Indeed, there is no evidence of bias in estimates for the analysis of educational outcomes once characteristics of the household during the first visit are controlled (Sánchez & Escobal, [2020](#page-19-11)). In our study, the analytical sample (participants that we observe in each round of data collection with full information) is 517, smaller than the non-attrited sample (714). However, our estimates suggest that using this sample allows unbiased estimates for relationships of interest. We ran attrition probits, regressing a binary variable (1 if attrited in the analytical sample, 0 otherwise) separately on two educational outcomes observed in R1 (reading and writing abilities), in all cases controlling for child and household socio-demographic characteristics. Conditional on these controls, we found no relationship between educational outcomes and the likelihood of attrition.

### 4.1.4 | Variables

The dependent variable took three possible values based on self-reports at age 19 in R4: zero if a child has never been enrolled in higher education; one if a child has been enrolled/has completed higher education in a public institution; two if the child has enrolled/has completed higher education in a private institution (see Table 1). We present our results as marginal effects. Independent variables were grouped into three categories: child and family background, socioeconomic indexes, and skills and aspirations.

Child and family background included children's sex, ages in years, native tongue (Spanish vs. rest), numbers of siblings, public versus private school attended in their last year of secondary school, height-for-age z-score (HAZ) at age 8, mothers' schooling attainment, fathers' schooling attainment, urban or rural residence at age 8, the household wealth index at children's age 8, and household expenditures per capita at the children's age 12 (the earliest ages at which wealth and expenditures, respectively, were collected). A complete definition of each variable is provided in Table 2.

Skills and aspirations variables were test scores in vocabulary (PPVT) and math at age 12, educational aspirations at age 12, and mothers' educational aspirations for children when they were age 12. Aspirations partially account for the role of self-efficacy (Bandura et al., 2001). In all cases, we used information from the earliest round possible to exploit the long-term effects and take advantage of the longitudinally of the database (educational aspirations and expenditures were not available in R1). The included variables reflect well the richness of factors that drive higher-education enrolment decisions (Sánchez & Abhijeet, 2018). However, to our knowledge, there is little evidence about how these factors' roles differ according to whether institutions are public or private.

### 4.2.2 | Sample

We interviewed 27 individuals in November 2018. Institutions were selected based on these criteria:

- **•** Two public and two private institutions.
- **•** Not the highest quintile institutions (not quintile five).
- **•** In diverse places in our around Lima city.

We included districts from quintiles one to four in order to have some diversity in the sample. Based on the 2013 poverty map ([https://www.inei.gob.pe/media/MenuRecursivo/publicaciones_digitales/Est/Lib1261/Libro.](https://www.inei.gob.pe/media/MenuRecursivo/publicaciones_digitales/Est/Lib1261/Libro.pdf) [pdf)](https://www.inei.gob.pe/media/MenuRecursivo/publicaciones_digitales/Est/Lib1261/Libro.pdf), for each province, we ranked the districts from the poorest to the richest to obtain the quintiles. After that, we initially selected four institutions to invite to participate in the study: two in Lima and two in Cañete, two public and two private. In each province, one of the selected institutions refused to participate. In Cañete, we quickly replaced it with a similar one. In Lima, we had to contact several additional institutions to find a replacement because many took too long to reply to our invitation (out of 15 institutions contacted, 11 did not reply, two refused, and two accepted).

### 4.2.3 | Procedure and instruments

The semi-structured interview included some demographic questions and eight questions on higher-educational marketization. Each interview lasted approximately 30 minutes. The interview questions are presented in the [Appendix](#page-19-12).

We explained to the participants that their answers would remain confidential and asked for their oral consent to record the interviews. Three of the 27 were not recorded as these participants did not consent to the recording. Additionally, we explained the definition of marketization as 'private institutions that are for-profit'. Most interviews were conducted in Spanish, except two that were in English (as per participants' preference).

### 4.2.4 | Analysis

The analysis was based on notes taken during the interviews and the review of the audio recordings, allowing us to triangulate the information on the same theme across participants. Additionally, this information was systematized using matrices for each theme of the study, classifying the similarities and differences of participants' opinions. The results reported are based on all 27 interviews.

## **5.1 **|** Quantitative analysis**

### 5.1.1 | Descriptives

Table [3](#page-8-0) shows descriptive statistics of the independent variables (participants from the YLS). Gender is balanced (48% girls, 52% boys). Most children had Spanish as their first language; only 9% reported using an indigenous language. Participants on average had two siblings and were second-born. were one SD below the median for well-nourished populations so the average child was short but not stunted. Seventy-seven per cent of the children lived in urban areas.

At approximately age 12 (R2), over 90% of children and parents aspired for their children to have higher education. Parents' aspirations for their children were slightly higher than those of children. We standardized the children's scores at age ~ 12 with a mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Lastly, children were more likely to have attended public than private middle schools (79% vs. 21%).

### 5.1.2 | Regression analysis

To answer RQ1, "What are the key predictors of attending a private/public higher-educational institution at age 19" we estimated a multinomial logistic model with data from R4 (and R5 in an alternative regression at age 22) for higher-educational enrolments (dependent variables) and R1 and R2 for the independent variables. Standard errors were clustered by the R1 sites. Table [4](#page-9-0) presents the marginal effects of this multinomial model. The key predictors of attending a higher-educational institution vary across categories.

Higher HAZ at age 8 has long-term predictive power in increasing the probability of attending a highereducational institution at age 19. Children from families with wealth in the top tercile are more likely to be

<!-- page 10 -->

<!-- page 11 -->

enrolled in higher-educational institutions than those in the lowest tercile. Children's higher aspirations and previous skills (both math and PPVT) are associated with an increased probability of attending highereducational institutions.

The probabilities of being enrolled in public institutions are higher for children that had better HAZ at age 8, higher aspirations at age 12, higher math scores at age 12, and are from households with expenditures and wealth in the lowest tercile.

For attending private higher-educational institutions, the variables that are significant (although only at 10% level) are wealth in the top tercile, HAZ at age 8, children's aspirations, and math and PPVT test scores at age 12. Notably, the probabilities of being enrolled in private institutions are 27 percentage points higher for children in the top wealth tercile than in the lowest tercile.

Conditional on controlling for other factors, the results indicate that the wealth index and household expenditures are important predictors of the probabilities of attending higher education in both private and public institutions. This is similar to the results of Castro et al. ([2016](#page-17-6)) as they found that concurrent family incomes were strongly correlated with Peruvian university enrollments, although they used cross-sectional data in contrast to our exploration of longer-run associations with household expenditures and wealth. Our results also extend the evidence about the importance of cognitive skills accumulated earlier in life for predicting enrolment in higher education (Sánchez & Abhijeet, [2018](#page-19-3)). A higher math test score predicts enrolment in public and private institutions, whilst a higher PPVT score only predicts enrolment in private institutions.

We also find that the relationship varies between private and public higher-educational institutions. The probabilities of attending private higher-educational institutions are higher for individuals in the top terciles of household wealth and household expenditures compared with those in the bottom terciles.

Some participants (9%) were still enrolled in high school at age 19. This might bias our previous results. At age 22, the proportion enrolled in higher education increased from 46% to 65%. Amongst those enrolled, 25% were in public higher-educational institutions and 40% were in private higher-educational institutions. Table [5](#page-11-0) presents additional results using enrolment at age 22 (R5, 2016) to estimate the multinomial model.

The results are generally similar to those for age 19, but with some differences. Children from the top tercile of household expenditures are less likely to attend public higher-educational institutions. Math test scores significantly predict public higher-education and no-higher-education attendance, but not private attendance, as it did at age 19. No role for PPVT scores is observed to predict private higher education, as it had at age 19. Children's and their parents' aspirations are associated with an increased probabilities of attending public higher-educational institutions. However, the point estimates appear to differ only for parental aspirations, which appear more associated with the enrolment decision in public institutions at age 22. Finally, for child and family characteristics, higher HAZ at age 8 and higher maternal schooling are associated with an increased probabilities of attending higher-educational institutions.

## *Advantages of higher-educational marketization*

Participants mentioned that it is beneficial because institutions will have more resources that can be invested in technology, equipment, and infrastructure, in having more alliances with prestigious institutions, which in turn will improve education. For example, one participant said:

A positive point is the fact that there is more investment. […] like more alliances with renowned institutions.

(Staff, public institution)

(Punto positivo es el hecho que haya inversión. […] El tema de las alianzas con instituciones de renombre.)

Likewise, it was mentioned that students from these institutions are better prepared for labour markets, compared to those graduating from public universities. Additionally, interviewees perceived that the increase in private for-profit institutions promotes competition and provides more opportunities for students in terms of variety of programs and in terms of attending higher education. For example, one participant said:

<!-- page 14 -->

Well, about for-profit institutions, the contribution they can have, the ones that care about student's capacity, is that related to economic resources, they can bring the latest and cutting-edge technology.

(Faculty, private institution)

(Bueno las instituciones con fines de lucro, el aporte que pueden tener, las que toman como importancia la capacidad de los alumnos es que, con el recurso económico, puede traer tecnología más actualizada, más de punta.)

It is important to mention that participants believed that these advantages can only occur if institutions focus on students and their learning but not on money and revenues. Also, there were three participants who stated that there are no benefits from higher-educational marketization.

*Disadvantages of higher-educational marketization*

Participants argued that marketization can decrease educational quality if for-profit institutions rise in numbers without any regulation. One participant shared:

The appearance of so many low-quality [institutions of higher education] where at the end students will not do anything with that education. […] The government has kind of withdrawn from the quality control of higher education […] The disadvantage is that private universities have been created without any control, they are recently being put under control by the government. (Faculty, public institution)

(Que aparezcan tantas [instituciones de educación superior] con baja calidad, que al final a los muchachos no les sirva de mucho los estudios. […] El Estado un poco que se retiró del control de la calidad de la educación superior. […] La desventaja es que las universidades privadas han nacido sin ningún control, recién se están poniendo bajo control por el Estado.)

Additionally, it was reported that for-profit institutions care about their own benefits and interests, putting aside students and society, which can cause higher tuition, more low-quality staff, more corruption, and less rigour with students. The other main concern was about inequality, interviewees mentioned that not everyone has the means to access private institutions, which causes the higher-educational system to be more elitist and discriminatory. One participant stated:

They would focus more on making money and the level of education would decrease and generate incompetent professional in the labor market.

(Student, public institution)

(Se enfocarían más en ganar dinero y el nivel de educación disminuiría, y generaría unos profesionales incompetentes en el campo laboral.)

We also asked participants if higher-education marketization is good for educational progress at the student or institutional level, and if it improves efficiency. One faculty member pointed out that for-profit institutions pay higher salaries to instructors. So, faculty need to be better prepared and only the best ones keep

<!-- page 15 -->

their jobs. Interviewees that believed that marketization is good for educational progress argued that the increase of for-profit institutions increases competition by reducing the tuition at the non-profit institutions and improving infrastructure and equipment, as long as the government regulates institutions. For example, one participant stated:

When we marketize education we have a different range of services among which equipment is important. Students will have more access to these tools. […] Projects will be done in less time, it makes it more dynamic and that also leads to innovation. (Faculty, private institution)

(Al comercializar la educación vamos a tener una diferente gama de servicios entre la cual el equipamiento es importante, los chicos van a poder ser más asequibles a estas herramientas. […] Se hacen en menos tiempo los proyectos, lo hace más dinámico y eso también conlleva a la innovación.)

Nevertheless, they pointed out again the importance of governmental participation and regulation of higher education. It is interesting that out of the 27 interviews, 11 mentioned that the marketization of higher education is not good for educational progress because for-profit private institutions are low-quality and mostly focus on income/ money, not on students. Out of the 11, five were from private institutions and four were students, which means that 38% of the participants were from for-profit higher-education institutions and half of the students interviewed made this observation. For example, one participant said:

No, [it does not promote educational efficiency] because for-profits want to earn [money], not to give more to students.

(Student, private institution)

(No [no promueve la eficiencia educativa] porque fines de lucro lo que va a querer es ganar, no darle al estudiante más.)

Finally, in regard to inequality, 19 participants stated that marketization contributes to inequality because not everyone has the means to attend for-profit institutions. For example, one participant shared:

Look, I come from a humble family, my family's monthly income does not exceed 600 soles […] and it is not enough for me to study at a for-profit university, those that charge you.

(Student, public university)

(Mira yo vengo de una familia humilde, el ingreso mensual de mi familia no pasa de los 600 soles […] y a mi no me alcanza para estudiar en una universidad con fines de lucro, que cobren.)

This potentially generates inequality not only in access to higher education but also in students' knowledge and labour market access since interviewees thought that private institutions that focus on students and their learning are in a better position to provide a better education than public institutions and that employers are more likely to hire students from private rather than public institutions. For example, one participant stated:

I think so [educational inequities have increased]. A graduate of a private [institution] is familiar with the most advanced technology, right? […] Unlike a [graduate from] public, although not always. (staff, public university)

<!-- page 16 -->

(Me parece que sí [se han incrementado las inequidades educativas]. Un egresado de una privada está con la tecnología más de avanzada, ¿no? […] A diferencia de una pública, aunque no siempre.)

Interviewees also highlighted the importance of regulations, arguing that the government should promote alliances amongst institutions to give students more opportunities and supervise curriculum and instruction to assure that educational quality is the same across higher-educational institutions. Four interviewees mentioned that higher education marketization does not increase inequality because inequality in the educational system already exists.

## **6 **|** SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION**

This is the first study that examined Peruvian higher-educational marketization with a focus on educational choices and educational inequality using mixed methods. The quantitative method used longitudinal data from the Young Lives older cohort (R1-R5). Multinomial logit analyses revealed predictors of enrolment amongst private and public institutions. The quantitative analysis shows evidence of segmentation in higher education that is linked to differences observed earlier in life. Results show that having higher aspirations and better math test scores at age 12 are associated with higher probabilities of attending higher-educational institutions at age 19 and higher probabilities of attending public higher-educational institutions at age 22. The latter result is consistent with the fact that public institutions are more selective than private ones, on average, especially considering the proliferation of low-quality for-profit public universities.

The findings on aspirations are interesting, particularly because there is mixed evidence on associations between child aspirations and attending higher education (Harrison & Waller, [2018)](#page-18-22). Experts in the field argue that aspirations for higher education are complex and multidimensional and this could reflect socio-emotional skills (Baker et al., [2014;](#page-17-11) Bandura et al., [2001)](#page-17-10). Further studies on this, particularly in the context of developing countries, would be valuable.

Individuals from households with expenditures in the middle or top tercile at age 12 are more likely to attend private higher-educational institutions than those from households with lower expenditures. This result partially captures the role of short-term financial constraints (Castro et al., [2016)](#page-17-6) in a context where access to scholarships and loans is limited (SUNEDU, [2021)](#page-19-10). However, since expenditure was measured many years before enrolment this also suggests that parents' capacities to invest in their children's education throughout childhood and adolescence have continuing effects after 10 years. The wealth gradient persists even after controlling for cognitive skills accumulated during childhood. This complements the findings from the literature on the privatization of higher education in developing countries by showing that segmentation by wealth combines short-term and long-term factors (Ferreyra et al., [2017;](#page-18-0) Yamada & Oviedo, [2017](#page-19-8)). The fact that at age 22 being in the top tercile expenditurewise predicts enrollment, and cognitive skills do not predict enrollment in private higher-education, suggests that admission selectivity might have been relaxed for those able to pay tuition. This strategy is likely to be unfair even for those that are able to afford it; as recent evidence shows, returns to higher-education in the labour market are higher (and, in some cases, only observed) for those that attend high-quality higher-educational institutions (Lavado et al., [2015](#page-18-10); Sanchez et al., [2021](#page-19-4)).

The qualitative study suggested that the main perceived strengths of higher-educational marketization are related to better opportunities for students. According to the participants, 'for-profit' institutions can invest in technology and innovation and can partially absorb higher-educational demand. The results also showed that the negative aspects as perceived by the participants are institutions being too profit-oriented at the expense of students' education, the introduction of low-quality professionals in labour markets and higher educational costs.

The interviews provided valuable insights relating to educational efficiency and inequality. We heard that educational marketization could increase efficiency by competition, which in turn, leads to better infrastructure, better instructors, and materials. However, another insight was that educational efficiency was possible only with

<!-- page 17 -->

good governmental supervision. The interview results showed the perception that 'for-profit' institutions could increase inequality because not everyone has the means to pay for private education, and this, in turn, generates disparities before and after graduation, in the labour market. This is aligned with the results from the quantitative analysis.

Although this study explores a relevant and understudied topic in higher education, it is important to mention some challenges and limitations. The samples of the quantitative and qualitative analyses are different so we cannot link students' perceptions in the qualitative survey with information from the Young Lives database. Also, the Young Lives data were collected before SUNEDU started the accreditation process, and the qualitative data were collected when this process had started but no decisions had been made. Our results do not show causality, but only what factors are associated with attending higher education. Additionally, we differentiate between public and private institutions, but the Young Lives data do not specify if the institution is for-profit or not. Therefore, we were not able to conduct additional quantitative analysis by the status of the institutions (for or not-for-profit). On the qualitative data, in addition to the shortcomings in the participants' recruitment process, limiting the data collection to the Lima region could potentially make the results biased. Also, the term 'Marketization of higher education' is not common in Peru. Therefore, even when we provided participants with a standard definition, it was challenging to make everyone understand the topic in a consistent manner, especially students. Furthermore, most of the participants from the two for-profits did not know that the institutions were for-profit.

The findings of this study contribute to the ongoing debate on the marketization and privatization of higher education in developing countries. The study's findings are helpful to educational policymakers in Peru and also in other developing countries with similar circumstances. For example, higher education, specifically, has been given increasing importance for its role in enhancing economic prosperity at both individual and national levels. Though data from many countries show that the returns are highest at the primary level, the returns to higher education are also very high (Patrinos & Psacharopoulos, [2010)](#page-18-23). Peru has one of the lowest returns to higher education in LAC (Ferreyra et al., [2017](#page-18-0)). As highlighted by this study, the introduction of for-profit universities without regulation might be one of the factors behind this result in Peru despite a consistent increase in higher education over the years. Interestingly, similar observations have been made in other developing countries where economic returns are low due to unregulated growth in the higher-educational sector (e.g., see Thapa & Maharjan, [2019](#page-19-13) in the case of Nepal). This also points to an important finding of the economics-of-education literature that education alone cannot do the job, and that countries need to have proper economic institutional frameworks (including good governmental supervision) to experience economic development (Pritchett, [2001,](#page-18-24) [2006)](#page-18-25).

Finally, we hope that the results of the study contribute to the debate and implementation of educational policies since they emphasize the possible disequalizing effects of the marketization of higher education and the key roles of governments in regulating and monitoring higher-educational quality. Whilst there is a Peruvian institution overseeing universities' educational quality (i.e., SUNEDU), there is an urgent need to create a similar entity that can oversee technical institutes as well, particularly considering that there are more technical institutes than universities. More empirical evidence on this topic would help better inform the public as well as educational policymakers in Peru and around the world.
