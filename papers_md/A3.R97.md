## ABSTRACT

The circular economy (CE) has been identified as a possible catalyst for sustainable development by business, academics, and policymakers. To aid company developers in creating and improving business models that incorporate circularity, a variety of tools for circular business model innovation (CBMI) have been proposed. Nevertheless, the existing tools failed to consider sustainability or CE in their advancements. Currently, there is no research that has presented a complete dataset including all potential tools that may be created based on the CE' sustainability performance attributes. Moreover, there has been a dearth of research conducted to assess and model these tools in order to determine the most efficient ones, which has resulted in a research gap. This paper constructs a decision matrix of CBMI tools by intersecting 100 CBMI tools with 10 CE' sustainability performance attributes. The modeling of CBMI tools falls under Multiple Attribute Decision Making (MADM) due to the presence of many attributes, varying importance levels of these attributes, and the and variation in data. Thus, the fuzzy weighted with zero inconsistency (FWZIC) method is reformulated under neutrosophic bipolar fuzzy sets (NBFS) to determine the weight of CE's sustainability performance attributes. The matrix that has been constructed and the resulting weight values are fed into the CODAS method in order to model CBMI tools and identify the most sustainable tool. The results indicate that the NBFS-FWZIC method gave a weight value of 0.1031 to A_7, which is the greatest weight value. On the other hand, A_3 had the lowest weight value of 0.0944. The CODAS method modeled the 100 CBMI tools, with Tool<sub>39</sub> being identified as the most sustainable tool and Tool<sub>26</sub> as the least sustainable tool. The robustness and durability of the proposed method are evaluated using a sensitivity analysis. Spearman's rank correlation test, and comparison analysis.

## *1.1.* *Problems and research gaps *

According to ([Bocken et al., 2019](#page-17-0)), sustainability performance has a direct influence on CBMI tools. The CE' sustainability performance attributes for the potential development of the CBMI tool are presented in Table 1, as stated by ([Bocken et al., 2019)](#page-17-0). Thus far, no research has introduced a comprehensive decision matrix encompassing all potential tools that can be derived from the aforementioned attributes.

Furthermore, there has been a lack of study undertaken to evaluate and model these tools in order to identify the most optimal ones, which has considered a research gap.

However, there are concerns associated with the process of modeling and selecting the most sustainable CBMI tool, which is based on multiple CE's sustainability performance attributes. There are three key concerns that need to be addressed when modeling CBMI tools: the multiple CE's sustainability performance attributes ([Mahmoud et al., 2022)](#page-18-0), the level of importance of these attributes [(Ibrahim et al., 2023)](#page-18-0), and variation in data ([Qahtan et al., 2023)](#page-18-0). Hence, it is imperative to consider a multiple CE's sustainability performance attributes in modeling CBMI tools. The integration of the CE's sustainability performance attributes into the development of CBMI tools can complicate the evaluation process. These disparities indicate that the CBMI tools should never be evaluated just on a single attribute. In addition, the importance of the evaluation attributes must be taken into account, as not all attributes carry the same weight and influence on the CBMI tools. In addition, when certain CBMI tool outperform others in terms of specific CE's sustainability performance attributes, they may be selected as the superior ones. Conversely, different CE's sustainability performance attributes may favor other tools over the former ones, resulting in data variation. Consequently, when considering the aforementioned concerns, the complex problem of making decisions with multiple attributes arises in the context of modeling CBMI tools. Hence, a Multiple Attribute Decision Making (MADM) solution [(Albahri et al., 2022; Dhalmahapatra et al., 2022](#page-17-0)) is necessary to address the identified problem, concerns, and bridge the existing research gap.

## *1.2.* *Theoretical MADM challenges *

MADM is a highly dynamic area of interdisciplinary research within the fields of operations research and management science ([Zayat et al., ](#page-19-0) [2023)](#page-19-0). Researchers have proposed numerous MADM techniques and methods over the past few years [(Dhalmahapatra et al., 2022; Mardani ](#page-18-0) [Shahri et al., 2021; Zayat et al., 2023, Kazemi et al., 2024, Wang et al., ](#page-18-0) [2024; Bouraima et al., 2024; Dagıstanlı ](#page-18-0)˘ and Kurtay, 2024). Some of the most well-known methods are the weighted sum model (WSM), weighted product model (WPM), weighted aggregated sum product assessment (WASPAS), technique for order of preference by similarity to ideal solution (TOPSIS), ELimination Et Choix Traduisant la REalit´e (ELECTRE), preference ranking organization method for enrichment of evaluations (PROMETHEE), VIseKriterijumska Optimizacija I Kompromisno Resenje (VIKOR), complex proportional assessment (COPRAS), multi-objective optimization by ratio analysis plus the full multiplicative form (MULTIMOORA), evaluation based on distance from average

<!-- page 3 -->

solution (EDAS), and additive ratio assessment (ARAS) ([Keshavarz ](#page-18-0) [Ghorabaee et al., 2016; Zayat et al., 2023](#page-18-0)).

All these MADM methods have their own advantages, strengths, and weaknesses for certain applications. However, this work does not focus on evaluating their accuracy. Overall, selecting the optimal CBMI tool remains a problem that lacks a clear definition. It typically depends on ambiguous data, which is challenging to represent and is derived from the expertise of professionals. This paper adopted a newly developed method proposed by ([Keshavarz Ghorabaee et al., 2016)](#page-18-0), known as distance-based assessment (CODAS), to address MADM problem. CODAS possesses some characteristics that have not been taken into account in previous MADM methods [(Kausar et al., 2023; Keshavarz ](#page-18-0) [Ghorabaee et al., 2016](#page-18-0)).

The CODAS method employs the Euclidean distance as its main criterion for evaluation. If the Euclidean distances of two options are in close proximity, the Taxicab distance is employed to compare them. The threshold parameter determines the level of proximity of Euclidean distances. Furthermore, [(Keshavarz Ghorabaee et al., 2016)](#page-18-0) conducted a comparative sensitivity study to prove the stability and validity of CODAS. This analysis compares the outcomes of the CODAS method with those of five other MADM methods, namely WASPAS, COPRAS, TOPSIS, VIKOR, and EDAS. The authors determined that CODAS is effective in addressing MADM problems. Thus, CODAS has been applied in various domains to model a logistics service provider, model a tanker vehicle, select potential hospital locations, prioritize technology aspects in the industry 4.0 for improving the adoption of CE in the supply chain network [(Agarwal et al., 2022](#page-17-0)), and model the important CE practices that contribute to achieving the sustainable development goals in circular supply chain management (Lahane & [Kant, 2022](#page-18-0)).

Based on the given description, the CODAS method can be utilized to model the most sustainable CBMI tool. Nevertheless, the CODAS method is limited by a basic drawback, specifically its inability to provide weights for attributes based on their level of importance. This necessitates the use of an additional method for attribute weighing. Traditional analytic hierarchy process, analytic network process, and best-worst method have been widely utilized in the academic literature for this purpose ([Zaidan et al., 2023)](#page-19-0). However, the inconsistent weight results of these methods remain unresolved. Recently, [(Mohammed et al., ](#page-18-0) [2022)](#page-18-0) proposed the fuzzy weighted with zero inconsistency (FWZIC) method that can determine the importance of the attributes in the modeling process with no inconsistency. Nevertheless, the initial version of the FWZIC was characterized by a lack of clarity and the utilization of triangular fuzzy numbers. Therefore, the FWZIC method has been broadened to include various types of fuzzy sets (FS), such as Pythagorean [(Qahtan et al., 2023)](#page-18-0), neutrosophic ([Alamoodi et al., 2022)](#page-17-0), Fermatean ([AlSattar et al., 2022)](#page-17-0), and Pythagorean m-polar ([Albahri et al., ](#page-17-0) [2022)](#page-17-0). Despite these efforts, there are still unresolved concerns regarding uncertain and ambiguous information.

([Zadeh, 1965)](#page-19-0) is credited with the founding of FS theory. The concept of FS theory has been broadened to encompass other types of sets such as vague FS(Alhazaymeh & [Hassan, 2015](#page-17-0)), interval-valued FS, intuitionistic FS ([Alhazaymeh et al., 2012)](#page-17-0), L-FS, Q-FS (Adam & [Hassan, ](#page-17-0) [2015)](#page-17-0), probabilistic FS, and others ([Alsattar et al., 2024; Ghailani et al., ](#page-17-0) [2023; Truck, 2015](#page-17-0)). Each of these versions exhibited limitations in various scenarios. Then, [(Smarandache, 1999](#page-18-0)) introduced the concept of neutrosophic set, which serves as a generalization of all earlier versions of FSs. Unfortunately, all the mentioned FSs exclusively addressed issues related to positive opinions and preferences, disregarding the fact that the human mind operates in both positive and negative directions when making decisions. Thus, in order to address this lack, [(Zhang, 1994)](#page-19-0) proposed the concept of bipolar FSs. The characteristics of bipolar FSs were thoroughly examined and explored by [(Yaqoob et al., 2014, 2016](#page-19-0)).

Neutrosophic set theory finds applications in diverse domains such as engineering, computer sciences, medical sciences, physical sciences, and social sciences (Otay & [Kahraman, 2019)](#page-18-0). Neutrosophic sets is a broader and more comprehensive sets for addressing uncertain and ambiguous

information issues, in comparison to other versions of FSs ([Hashim et al., ](#page-18-0) [2020)](#page-18-0). Bipolar FSs are proven to be useful for dealing with ambiguous situations that involve both positive and negative aspects ([Deli et al., ](#page-17-0) [2015)](#page-17-0). Recently, [(Hashim et al., 2018)](#page-18-0) introduced the concept of neutrosophic bipolar fuzzy sets (NBFS) which is a general extension of the FSs, bipolar FSs, intuitionistic FSs and neutrosophic sets. The NBFS involves making decisions by taking into account both positive and negative preferences. Therefore, FWZIC method is formulated with NBFS to determine the importance of each CE's sustainability performance attribute.

## *1.3.* *Motivation *

At this point, there is no existing research that has presented a comprehensive decision matrix that includes all possible tools that may be developed based on the CE's sustainability performance attributes. Moreover, there has been a dearth of research conducted to assess and model these tools in order to determine the most efficient ones, as mentioned earlier. To our knowledge, there have been no previous research that have extended FWZIC under NBFS. More so, the integration of NBFS-FWZIC and CODAS methods have not been implemented in any case study. Thus, we are motivated to propose novel NBFS-FWZIC and CODAS to model CBMI tool and identify the most sustainable ones. The NBFS-FWZIC is formulated and developed to compute the weight of the evaluation CE's sustainability performance attributes. The CODAS method is adopted to model CBMI tools using the derived weights and the constructed decision matrix.

## *1.4.* *Research main objectives *

Based on the preceding sections, the objectives of the present paper can be simply summarized as follows: (i) To construct a decision matrix for CBMI tools which demonstrates the intersection between CBMI tools and CE's sustainability performance attributes. (ii) To reformulate a new extension of the FWZIC method within the environment of NBFS, which incorporates a weighting mechanism for the CE's sustainability performance attributes and addresses the shortcomings of existing methods. (iii) To integrate the proposed NBFS-FWZIC approach into the CODAS method for modeling CBMI tools and determine the most sustainable ones.

## *1.5.* *Research contributions *

The paper's contributions to knowledge can be simply summarized as follows:

- 1. This paper constructs CBMI decision matrix through the intersection of CBMI tools and CE's sustainability performance attributes. Constructing such a matrix can resolve the issue of evaluating and modeling CBMI tool based on multiple evaluation attributes.
- 2. This paper reformulates and develops a new extension of FWZIC method, named NBFS-FWZIC method, which is designed to assign weights to CE's sustainability performance attributes. The NBFS-FWZIC method addresses the importance level issue of the evaluation attributes by assigning a weight value to each attribute based on its influence on the developments of more sustainable CBMI tools.
- 3. This paper adopts CODAS method to model CBMI tools in order to address the data variation issue.
- 4. This paper proposes a decision-making methodology that combines the NBFS-FWZIC and CODAS methods to model CBMI tools and determine the most sustainable options based on the constructed decision matrix.

## 2. Towards tools for CBMI

Various tools and technologies have been created in the fields that support the concept of CBMI in literature. Recently, there has been a rise in the popularity of work related to eco-design and innovative tools (Bocken et al., 2019). This includes many contributions that focus on the development of diverse methods, such as treatment and recycling (Chiu & Chu, 2012). Over time, more methods and tools for facilitating the creation of sustainable product service systems became widely accepted (Keskin et al., 2013). Several other "sustainable tools" for the design of business models have been offered in recent years (Bocken et al., 2019; Pigosso & McAloone, 2015). The predominant types of tools include guidelines, such as sustainability standards established by the International Standards Organization (ISO), as well as checklists and more analytical tools like life cycle assessment. These tools offer comprehensive and systematic analysis at various phases of the product development life cycle (Vallet et al., 2013). They can have either a qualitative or quantitative structure (Bovea & Pérez-Belis, 2012) and can thus cover one or multiple phases during the lifespan of a product (Rossi et al., 2016). A common objective of these tools is to address stakeholder, customer, and managerial concerns, with a particular focus on conceptual design, ideation, and supply chain participation (Bocken

Despite there are many tools available, research indicates an absence of tools that adequately fulfill business needs and expectations (Rossi et al., 2016). Several well-known fundamental methods and tools, such as the business model canvas (Johnson, 2012) or the lean startup method (Ries, 2011), appear to be employed in a flexible manner in practice, without a specific focus on CBMI. In the absence of adequate support, the widespread use of these basic tools may weaken the focus on sustainability or the circular economy (Bocken et al., 2018) and result in the adoption of further traditional business tools that do not offer any clear advantages to society or the environment.

In summary, a wide range of tools designed for circular innovation and sustainability are unfortunately not being used to their full potential (Bocken et al., 2019). This may occur due to the following reasons: (i) The empirical testing of these methods has not been conducted, and the CBMI technique has not considered the needs of users, particularly those of business developers in organizations (Bocken et al., 2019). (ii) Tools may be excessively demanding in terms of the duration and complexity (Kubzansky, 2013). (iii) Tools are typically built within certain domains, such as engineering, business, or design (Bocken et al., 2019). However, they often fail to leverage valuable multidisciplinary expertise that may enhance their functionality. Design science has become increasingly important in the development of sustainability-focused business tools. It aims to integrate various disciplines by adopting a stakeholder and user perspective (Heyes et al., 2018). This is achieved through the use of design science methods such as prototyping (Geissdoerfer et al., 2016). Additionally, tools are regularly tested and refined with input from consumers in practical terms (Baldassarre et al., 2017).

The recent methods (Bocken et al., 2017; Weissbrod & Bocken, 2017) have been developed CBMI based on established innovation methodologies such as effectuation (learning from entrepreneurial practice) (Bocken et al., 2019) and lean startup (emphasizing rapid and iterative testing of new ideas in practice). Examples of several tool categories that focus on generating ideas for CBMs, such as serious games (Whalen, 2017; Whalen, Berlin, et al., 2018), case databases (CIRCULATOR, 2018), and typologies (Bakker et al., 2014), are always expanding. In

order to effectively adopt and further improve the CBMI tools, it is essential to first have a thorough understanding of their current state and how they might contribute to the process of business model innovation. Several reviews of eco-design tools were conducted (Bocken et al., 2019; Bovea & Pérez-Belis, 2012; Pigosso & McAloone, 2015). These studies focus on evaluating tools based on two criteria: (i) The purpose of the tool, which specifies its type and objective (e.g., checklist, analytical) (Knight & Jenkins, 2009); the nature of the tool (e.g., qualitative, quantitative) (Bovea & Pérez-Belis, 2012), and (ii) The shape and features of the tool, such as diagrams or computer-based tools (Rossi et al., 2016), and the assessment of the tool's development over time (Pigosso et al., 2015).

As far as we are aware, there have been only two studies that particularly investigate methods and tools for CBMI (Bocken et al., 2019). However, to begin implementing the concept of CE as described by (Blomsma & Brennan, 2017), it is essential to thoroughly examine the tools being used. This is particularly important because there are numerous sustainable innovation tools available that have not been utilized in practice (Bocken et al., 2019). There is a concern that using "generic tools" such as those proposed by (Johnson, 2012; Ries, 2011) may not lead to the desired CBMs with clear ecological benefits (Manninen et al., 2018). Even tools developed in academic settings often lack a defined scientific methodology and tool assessment frequently are lacked (Madani et al., 2017). In this paper, a decision matrix with all potential development of CBMI tools based on CE's sustainability performance attributes is constructed.

## 4.2. NBFS-FWZIC method development

This section enhances and expands the FWZIC method by incorporating the NBFS. The proposed NBFS-FWZIC method consists of the following five points.

- i. The evaluation and modeling of the selected CBMI tools include examining the list of CE's sustainability performance attributes.
- iii. A group of structured expert judgments is formed, consisting of at least three experts in areas such as circular economies, sustainable economies, or industries. Following that, the chosen experts give their approval to a questionnaire that has been designed for the purpose of gathering data. In addition, the experts assess the importance of the CE's sustainability performance attributes using a validated questionnaire and five linguistic measurements of importance (e.g. a 5-point Likert scale), as outlined in Table 3.
- iii. The group of structured expert judgments and the CE's sustainability performance evaluation attributes of the CBMI tools are crossed to build the expert decision matrix (EDM). As per Eq. (3), where the linguistic measurements from the prior step are substituted with a numeric scale, given in Table 3, for further analysis. Denote by \{A_j\}_{1\leq j\leq n} the set of CE's sustainability performance attributes, that are evaluated by every expert E_i, for i=1,\cdots,m.

$$
EDM = \begin{bmatrix} E_1 & \cdots & A_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ E_m & e_{m1} & \cdots & e_{mn} \end{bmatrix} 
(3)
$$

iv. The NBFS-EDM is formed by implementing the NBFS membership on the EDM. The numerical scale in the EDM is substituted with the equivalent NBFN (given in Table 3). This step is essential when handling uncertain and ambiguous information. **Definitions 1–3** provide the definitions of NBFS and NBFNs. The process of converting the EDM into a fuzzy representation using NBFNs results in the NBFS-EDM as given in Eq. (4).

$$
NBFS - EDM = \begin{bmatrix} E_1 \\ \widetilde{e}_{11} & \cdots & \widetilde{e}_{1n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ E_m & \widetilde{e}_{m1} & \cdots & \widetilde{e}_{mn} \end{bmatrix} 
(4)
$$

where \tilde{e}_{ij} is the NBFN associated to e_{ij} for i=1,\cdots,m and j=1,\cdots,n.

v. This step involves determining the importance (weight) of the CE's sustainability performance attributes. The weighting of each attribute is decided based on the NBFS-EDM generated in the previous step. Initially, the averaging operator given in Definition 4 is used to combine the NBFN across the three experts, as indicated in Eq. (5). The aggregated NBFN for the *j*-th CE's sustainability performance attribute is:

$$
A_{w}\left(\widetilde{e}_{1j},\widetilde{e}_{2j}\cdots,\widetilde{e}_{nj}\right), \text{for} w = \left(\frac{1}{m},\cdots,\frac{1}{m}\right) 
(5)
$$

Then, the scoring function of NBFS, given in Definition 5, is used to calculate the final crisp weight of the CE's sustainability performance attributes, as indicated in Eq. (6). The initial weight for the *j*-th attribute is

$$
s_{j} = S_{NB} \left( A_{w} \left( \widetilde{e}_{1j}, \widetilde{e}_{2j} \cdots, \widetilde{e}_{nj} \right) \right) (6)
$$

Finally, the CE's sustainability performance attributes total weights are assigned to one. If this criterion is not satisfied, Eq. (7) is used to rescale the calculated weights.

$$ w_j = s_j / \sum_{j=1}^n s_j (7) $$

**Step 1**: Determining the normalized decision matrix. The linear normalization is used to normalize attributes values as follows:

$$ y_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{s} x_{ij}^2}} (8) $$

for i = 1, \dots, s and j = 1, \dots, n.

**Step 2**: Computing the weighted normalized decision matrix. The weighted normalized attributes values are computed as follows:

$$ r_{ii} = w_i(y_{ii} + 1) \tag{9} $$

where the weight of the *j*th attribute is indicated by w_j (0 < w_j < 1), and \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j = 1.

Step 3: Calculating the negative-ideal solution as follows:

$$ y^* = \begin{bmatrix} y_j^* \end{bmatrix}_{1 \le n} \tag{10} $$

$$ y_i^* = \min_i r_{ij} \tag{11} $$

**Step 4**: Determining the Euclidean and Taxicab distances of the alternatives from the negative-ideal solution, as demonstrated below:

$$ E_{i} = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m} \left(r_{ij} - y_{j}^{*}\right)^{2}} (12) $$

$$ T_i = \sum_{i=1}^{m} |r_{ij} - y_j^*| \tag{13} $$

**Step 5**: Constructing the relative assessment matrix, expressed as follows:

$$ H = \left[ h_{ik} \right]_{s \times s} \tag{14} $$

$$
h_{ik} = (E_i - E_k) + \psi(E_i - E_k, \tau)(T_i - T_k) 
(15)
$$

where k is an element of the set \{1, 2, \dots, n\} and \psi represents a threshold function used to determine if the Euclidean distances of two alternatives are equivalent. The function is defined as follows:

$$ \psi(x,\tau) = \begin{cases} 1if|x| \ge \tau \\ 0if|x| < \tau \end{cases} (16) $$

The decision-maker can modify the threshold parameter, denoted as \tau, for this function. It is recommended to modify this parameter within the range of 0.01 to 0.05 (Keshavarz Ghorabaee et al., 2016). Two alternatives are being examined using the taxicab distance if the difference between their Euclidean distances is less than \tau.

**Step 6:** Calculating the assessment score for each alternative as given below:

$$ S_i = \sum_{k=1}^{s} h_{ik} \text{ for } i = 1, \dots, s (17) $$

**Step 7**: Sorting the alternatives in a decreasing order based on the assessment score (S_i)_{1 \le i \le s}. The alternative that has the highest S_i value among all the alternatives is considered the most optimal one.

## *5.1.* *Theoretical implications *

Neutrosophic sets, in contrast to other types of FSs, provide a broader and more comprehensive sets to handling uncertain and ambiguous information. However, they do not address scenarios that involve both positive and negative aspects. Hence, the NBFS, as proposed by ([Hashim ](#page-18-0)

[et al., 2018](#page-18-0)), has been utilized in the present study. It aids in accurately conveying the actual perspective of experts and managing the ambiguity inherent in human cognition. The FWZIC method utilized in this study does not necessitate conducting pairwise comparisons of attributes. Thus, the occurrence of subjective errors during the collecting of expert responses is nearly nonexistent. Moreover, the combined NBFS-FWZIC and CODAS methods effectively tackle the issues of data variation and the level of uncertainty associated with determining attribute weights. The integrated NBFS-FWZIC and CODAS methodology is successfully utilized to model CBMI tools, resulting in the selection of the most sustainable tool.

## *6.1.* *Sensitivity and correlation analysis *

Sensitivity analysis involves examining the level of uncertainty in the output of the MADM methods when different inputs are altered. This examination is conducted to assess the consistency of the results obtained from the MADM approaches under various settings, favorable or unfavorable. The favorable outcomes derived from the sensitivity analysis demonstrate the durability and robustness of the solution. By following the technique used by ([Ghailani et al., 2023; Mourad et al., ](#page-18-0) [2023)](#page-18-0), a sensitivity analysis is conducted on the outcomes of the NBFS-FWZIC and CODAS methods. This analysis involves generating different weight sets and examining how they influence the overall modeling of the CBMI tools. The new weight sets are determined within different scenarios that are generated by utilizing the elasticity weight coefficient (∝*c*). This ∝*c*enables the adjustment of weight coefficients based on changes in the weight of the most important attribute, ensuring relative compensation for all values.

As given in[ Table 8,](#page-8-0) the attribute "The tool incorporates CE principles and broader sustainability goals, ensuring that these aims are maintained even when the tool is used by anyone other than its inventor (A7)" was identified as the most important attribute since it has the highest weight value, that is, *w*^A^^7^ = 0*.*1031. Thus, the ∝*c*of A7, as well as the limits for changing the weight coefficient (Δ*x*) of A7, were computed based on the interval [− 0.1031, 0.8969]. The ∝*c*values of all attributes are given in [Table 9](#page-9-0). After obtaining the interval of variation, the new weights of the remaining attributes are computed using Eq. (18).

$$
w_c = (1 - w_s) \times \left(\frac{w_c^0}{W_c^0}\right) = w_c^0 - \alpha_c \times \Delta x, \tag{18}
$$

where the weight of the most important attribute is *ws*, *w**^o^* *^c^*refers to the original weights of the attributes determined by NBFS-FWZIC method, and *W*^0^ *^c^*refers to summation of the original weights.

Last but not least, the association between the sets of modeling results derived from new scenarios and the proposed method is tested using a statistical test, namely Spearman's rank correlation test. Spearman's coefficient measures the degree of correlation between two or more sets of modeling results. The calculation of Spearman's rank (*rs*) can be performed using Eq. (19).

$$ r_s = 1 - \frac{6\sum d^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}, -1 \le r_s \le +1 (19) $$

where *n *denotes the number of tools, whereas *d *reflects the disparity between two modeling outcomes. The resultant score indicates the correlation between the two modeling outcomes. A score closer to +1 indicates a high positive correlation, whereas a value closer to − 1 indicates a strong negative correlation. The correlations between the modeling of new scenarios and the proposed method are shown in[ Fig. 5](#page-10-0).

According to correlations results presented in[ Fig. 5](#page-10-0), the original and new modeling results showed a high positive correlation in two scenarios (S1 and S2), with correlation coefficients *rs *of 0.9411 and 0.9813, respectively. In addition, there were moderate positive correlations seen between the original and new modeling results in S3-S9. The correlation coefficients *rs *values were 0.7244 in S3-S8 and 0.7203 in S9. ## **7.** **Conclusion **

This paper integrates two MADM methods, namely NBFS-FWZIC and CODAS, for modeling the CBMI tools based on multiple CE's sustainability performance attributes. The NBFS-FWZIC approach is formulated to calculate the weight values of the attributes, while the CODAS method is utilized to model CBMI tools based on the constructed decision matrix. The integration of NBFS-FWZIC and CODAS methodologies offers several benefits, such as reduced complexity, the ability to calculate priority weights for the CE's sustainability performance attributes, consideration of data variation. In addition, the proposed methods' validity is assessed using sensitivity analysis, Spearman's rank correlation test, and comparison with the most closely relevant study. Modeling CBMI tools can assist stockholders in making informed decisions. Modeling CBMI tools serves as a foundation for ongoing enhancement by identifying areas in which current CBMI tools may be lacking. Managers can utilize this knowledge to improve and optimize tools gradually, remaining flexible in response to changing sustainability problems. Furthermore, managers can utilize CBMI tools' modeling to pinpoint potential risks and uncertainties linked to each tool. This facilitates the creation of risk mitigation methods, guaranteeing that the selected tools are robust and able to withstand potential problems. Furthermore, upcoming researchers and developers can gain advantages from modeling CBMI tools to propose their own tools.

Despite the benefits of the proposed work, there are certain constraints: The NBFS-FWZIC method is formulated by utilizing a single aggregation operator and score function. Furthermore, irrespective of their level of expertise, all the experts were accorded equal importance. Furthermore, the EDM was generated using only five importance measurements. Furthermore, there is a reliance on an augmented dataset.

To resolve these constraints in the future, the following steps can be taken: The NBFS-FWZIC method can be formulated with additional

aggregation operators and score functions. Furthermore, it is feasible to assess and assign value to the experts based on their degree of expertise. Furthermore, incorporating seven or eleven importance measurements in the construction of the EDM. In the future, researchers and developers can propose real datasets if they adhere to the CE's sustainability performance attributes in the creation of their CBMI tools.
